# Adversarial review: Triangle Shirtwaist Factory

## Verdict

**REVISE.**

The chapter has the hardest thing right: it owns one exclusive decision — a control whose
release depends on a key and a person — and it keeps that decision intact whether or not the
door was locked on 25 March, which is the only way this case can be written honestly against a
record that acquitted. Its locators are unusually sound: I tested all forty-five receipt
locators against the local transcript and every one held, four with trivial page drift. But the
draft breaks two of the book's non-negotiable rules in ways that are small to repair and
disqualifying if left: it quotes Leon Stein's Vol. II "Library notes" inside quotation marks as
though they were verbatim testimony, in direct contradiction of its own receipts note and of
`MANIFEST.md`, and it puts the 1911 trial judge on the 1910 choice screen. It also builds the
choice screen from one side of a litigated question — the ninth-floor Washington Place door is
described as a closed, keyed, habitually-unused door, while the substantial defence evidence
that the door was in routine daily use (Samuel Bernstein up and down it ten times a day without
a key, foreladies leaving by it, girls using it to reach the tenth floor, the door standing open
all summer) is reduced to half a clause. Fix the horizon, the attribution and the missing
counter-evidence and this is publishable and probably the strongest human-inheritance section in
the pilot.

## Decision contract audit

**The decision question the draft actually teaches.** *May an organization make an exit depend
on a key, a person or a permission that may not be present when it is needed?* All three options
on the screen (A make the second door release without a key; B abandon the single-exit search on
the ninth; C keep the arrangement) are versions of the same question. Housekeeping, drills, the
fire escape, warning and building code appear only in Competing diagnoses, never as things the
reader is asked to choose. **The suspected weakness — that the chapter quietly teaches several
decisions — is not supported.** This is a clean, exclusive contract and matches the dossier's
item 9.

**The best decision point.** The draft's choice — "the last routine evening on which the second
stair door's release could have been made independent of a key and a person" — is correct and
better than the obvious alternative (the moment somebody turned a key on 25 March 1911, which is
neither established nor reversible by anyone the reader can inhabit). It is reversible (any
evening), consequential (it fixes what three hundred people think of first and what the second
door is worth when they reach it), and it survives the acquittal. Keep it.

The draft dates the decision "in 1910" (line 37) and the Judgement says "the 1910 decision"
(line 111), but the role card says "any closing time" and the cold open is placed roughly eight
months before the fire, i.e. mid-1910. Pick one date and hold it; at present the chapter is
vague about when the reader is standing.

**Nearest overlapping chapter.** Titanic (accepted practice, inspection passed, bad outcome) is
the nearest, not BP Texas City. The dossier's distinguisher is right and should be in the prose:
Triangle's safeguard was not degraded over time, as at Texas City — it was designed from the
start to depend on a key. The closing italic line currently compares to Texas City only; add or
substitute the Titanic distinguisher, because a reader who has read Titanic will otherwise ask
why this is not the same chapter.

## Findings

### BLOCKER

**B1. Vol. II "Library notes" quoted as verbatim testimony — three phrases.**

- Line 65: Fannie Selmanowitz "rubbing against the walls going down the shaft," and room for
  "not even a pin" more.
- Line 67: Kate Alterman "pulled the handle in and out, all ways".

Both come from Stein's summary of the lost Volume II (Selmanowitz, notes pp. 763–765 /
l. 27519–27529; Alterman, notes pp. 1154–1167 / l. 28388–28569). The chapter's own receipts
(line 142) state: "Items marked *(notes)* are Leon Stein's summaries … and are paraphrased, not
quoted as verbatim." `MANIFEST.md` line 98 is a hard condition of use: "Vol. II notes are Leon
Stein's summaries and must be cited as such, not as verbatim testimony." The prose therefore
breaks the source condition *and* the receipts make a false statement about the prose.

*Evidence status:* confirmed by direct inspection. The notes are also not a neutral record at
this point — l. 28389 marks the Alterman passages as "(These are the long passages for comparison
purposes)", i.e. Stein selected them precisely to display the repetition Steuer was attacking.

*Repair:* remove the quotation marks and paraphrase. "She turned the handle every way she could
and it did not open" carries the same weight. If the exact words are wanted, the chapter must
say "in Stein's notes she is recorded as saying …" and accept that this is a summary of speech,
not speech. Then the receipts statement becomes true again.

**B2. The 1911 trial is on the 1910 choice screen.**

Line 25, in the **Known** ledger: "*a hand-bag search at one door is common in the trade, and the
judge who later tries you will say aloud that no law compels a factory to send its people out of
more than one door.*"

`CONTEXT.md` rule 1: "Preserve the decision horizon. No fact learned later enters the choice
screen." This clause imports the trial, the existence of a prosecution, and a judicial
endorsement of the arrangement into the ledger the reader must reason from — and it does so on
the single point that most protects option C.

*Evidence status:* the underlying fact is true and horizon-legitimate. Crain, p. 1870 /
l. 55342: "there is no provision of law that compels factory owners to have their employees go
out of more than one door." The Constraint line (line 21) already states it correctly and
without the judge.

*Repair:* delete the clause. The Constraint line carries it. If the chapter wants the fact in the
ledger, state it as law ("no statute requires a factory to route its employees out of more than
one door; §80 requires only that doors not be locked during working hours") and put the Crain
locator in the receipts as the local evidence for the 1910 legal position.

### HIGH

**H1. The cold open announces the outcome in its first six words.**

Line 5: "*About eight months before the fire…*" and line 7: "*Nothing has burned.*"

`TEMPLATE.md` §1 requires a cold open with "no verdict and no outcome." Compare the exemplar,
which dates itself by calendar ("In September 1909") and says "Nothing has failed yet." Here the
reader is told there is a fire before being told there is a factory, and "Nothing has burned"
then names the hazard the choice screen is supposed to leave uncertain. AGENT.md pre-ship test 2
("Did any sentence smuggle the outcome backward?") fails at sentence one. The book's reader is
assumed to know the famous ending, which mitigates but does not license this: the method is that
the *chapter* does not supply it.

*Repair:* date the anecdote ("In the summer of 1910…"; Harris put it at about eight months before
25 March 1911, pp. 1865–1866 / l. 55144–55157) and replace "Nothing has burned" with a
neutral equivalent — "Nothing has gone wrong. The rule that everybody leaves one way, past one
man, is working as designed." Also reconsider "working *exactly* as designed", which is an
authorial wink.

**H2. The choice screen states one side's account of the door as fact.**

Line 15: "*It is kept closed. There is a key in its lock, tied to the knob with a string.*"

This is the defence's account of the routine (Levantini p. 1293 / l. 32097; S. Bernstein
p. 1519 / l. 41167; Rapp p. 1601 / l. 44463; Alter p. 1753 / l. 50669 — all verified). The
chapter's own Reconstruction (line 75) correctly separates it: "On the defence's best evidence
the door was closed with the key in it… On the People's evidence the key was not there." Mary
Bucelli, asked directly whether she saw a key, answered "There was no key, I saw no key"
(p. 327 / l. 13332). The choice screen asserts what the analysis later marks as contested.

*Evidence status:* the defence account is the only affirmative testimony about the *standing
routine* as opposed to the state of the door on the day, so the sentence is defensible — but only
if marked.

*Repair:* "On the account the firm's own people give, it is kept closed with a key in the lock,
tied to the knob with a string." Six added words; the ledger stays honest and the Reconstruction
stops contradicting the screen.

**H3. The chapter adopts the prosecution's picture of the ninth-floor door by omission.**

The suspected weakness is real, and it has a specific form: the record contains substantial
defence evidence that the ninth-floor Washington Place door was in ordinary daily use, and
almost none of it reaches the chapter.

- Samuel Bernstein, the manager, p. 1519 / l. 41163–41172: he went up and down the Washington
  Place stairs between the eighth and ninth floors "ten different times" a day, "in each day",
  "during the years you worked and had charge of this place", and "Never used a key."
- Wortman, p. 1796 / l. 52351–52368: foreladies went home that way; the door "stood open" in
  summer, "closed in winter time, and had a key."
- Steuer's summation, p. 2109 / l. 58592–58600, reciting testimony that ninth- and eighth-floor
  girls came up the Washington Place stairs to the tenth floor to hear a phonograph, until the
  complaint about refuse on the stairs led Blanck to put a phonograph on the eighth.
- Against this: Monick, p. 488 / l. 19921 — "did you ever see anybody come through the
  Washington place door leading to the stairway? A. No, sir." The point is genuinely litigated.

The chapter gives this one clause ("In summer, when the loft is hot, the door is sometimes open
during machine hours") and then, in the Reconstruction, asserts the opposite as settled: the
door is "an exit that required *a stranger to the routine* to find a key on a string in smoke."
On the defence evidence, the manager, the foreladies and an unknown number of operators were not
strangers to it.

*Repair:* put the counter-evidence on the choice screen where it belongs — it is the strongest
1910 fact in favour of option C, and without it option C is a straw man. Then narrow the
Reconstruction: the door was not part of the *operators' nightly exit routine* (which the People's
witnesses establish and the defence does not contest), which is all the argument needs. Drop
"stranger to the routine."

**H4. A quotation is attributed to the wrong speaker.**

Line 25: "*The value of goods actually found on employees in the past year is, by your own later
reckoning, ten or fifteen dollars, "not over $25."*"

Transcript p. 1868 / l. 55251–55261: Harris's own figure is "ten dollars or fifteen dollars or
twelve dollars or eight dollars, something like that." "Not over $25" is **Bostwick's** leading
question — "You would say it was not over $25, wouldn't you?" — to which Harris answered "No, it
would not exceed that much." The chapter quotes the prosecutor's phrase as the owner's reckoning.

*Evidence status:* the substance is conceded, so this is a quotation error rather than a factual
one; priority-stack rule 1 ("never false") still bites.

*Repair:* quote Harris — "ten dollars or fifteen dollars" — and paraphrase the rest: "a figure he
did not dispute was under twenty-five dollars." Separately: "by your own later reckoning" is a
second small horizon leak (it points at the witness box). Since the number is a 1910-available
fact, drop the tense marker and carry the locator in the receipts.

**H5. The counterfactual ignores the chapter's own best evidence against it.**

Line 99: "*The later problem changes in kind at one point: an exit exists for whoever reaches
it.*"

The chapter has already narrated, three pages earlier, the one piece of local evidence that
bounds this claim — and does not carry it forward. Louis Brown, pp. 1647–1648 / l. 46315–46345:
on the eighth floor the key "wouldn't turn", and the door still opened, because Brown pushed the
crowd back and "pulled the door with all my strength". "Were the girls packed against the door?
A. Packed as packed — you couldn't get it no tighter." Even open, the door admitted people only
while he held it: "Meanwhile they were closing the door and I held all my strength up to get the
door open."

So the record's one observed instance of this door type under load shows that an inward-opening
door with a crowd against it is not an exit until somebody with strength and standing clears the
crowd — a failure mode entirely independent of the key. The counterfactual, which is otherwise
the most disciplined section in the chapter, should say so. This is the precise sense in which
the door is currently made to carry more causal weight than the record supports.

*Repair:* one added sentence in the counterfactual, sourced to Brown: an unlocked
inward-opening door on the ninth floor still needs someone to push three hundred people back off
it, and on the eighth floor that person existed and the record names him. This strengthens the
chapter's actual thesis (a release that depends on a person) rather than weakening it.

### MEDIUM

**M1. The "Reasonably inferable" ledger is the chapter's verdict moved forward.**

Line 27: "*A rule that everyone leaves one way trains three hundred people to think of one door.
A door whose release depends on a key being in the lock and someone turning it is not the same
as an open door.*" Both sentences are the Judgement (line 105) in advance. Formally they satisfy
the template's "Inferable" definition; the failure is one-sidedness — no inference is offered
that supports C. The obvious ones are available: a door the manager uses ten times a day is
known to be passable; an arrangement inspected three times in fourteen months and found unlocked
each time gives the proprietor a reason to believe it complies. *Repair:* add one counter-
inference, or move the two existing sentences to the Reconstruction where they belong.

**M2. The inspection evidence is truncated to the one card that suits the chapter.**

Line 25 cites only "a state inspector who called in January 1910 marked the doors unlocked."
Williams produced three reports (pp. 1219–1233 / l. 29646–29734):

- Exhibit F, 6 January 1910 — check between "out" and "practical"; "unlocked" checked; "Notice
  sent January 19, 1910" (l. 29677–29679).
- Exhibit G, 13 January 1910 — "doors open out" blank, "**not**" written over "practical";
  "unlocked" affirmative (l. 29711–29716).
- Exhibit H, **27 February 1911** — "practical" marked "**No**", doors marked "open in",
  "unlocked" checked (l. 29723–29734).

The last is dated four weeks before the fire and is the single strongest horizon fact for option
C; the state's own inspectors also twice recorded that outward-opening doors were *not*
practicable there, which bears on CHECK 4 and on the "Building and code" diagnosis. Bostwick then
established on cross that these reports were not made pursuant to any statute and that the
Commissioner had lost jurisdiction over some of their subject matter (l. 29750–29765) — which is
the honest qualification. *Repair:* three clauses in the Known ledger; one clause of Bostwick's
qualification.

**M3. The elevator men and the manager are props.**

Brief item 8. Line 55 has the elevators as machinery — "the two passenger elevators, which made a
few more trips" — and no elevator operator appears in "The people who inherited it," which is
otherwise the chapter's best section. No operator testified, and the chapter should say so; but
their decisions are in the record at second hand. Blanck, notes p. 1949 / l. 28722–28726: "I
said to the elevator man, take down these girls, and then you will come up again… He took them
down, as many as he could." And l. 35621: "The elevator man pushed her in." Samuel Bernstein,
the manager, likewise appears only in the receipts, though he gave Brown the order that put him
at the door (p. 1646 / l. 46282: "Brown, I think you can't do anything, try to get all the girls
out if possible"). *Repair:* two sentences — the elevator men chose to come back up, and the
record does not preserve their account; the manager chose to send Brown to the crowd rather than
to the fire.

**M4. An inference is stated as an observation about the cuttings.**

Line 85: "*More than a ton of cuttings had accumulated since January.*" What Levy establishes
(pp. 246–253 / l. 10260–10268) is that 2,252 lb were *removed* on 15 January 1911 — and Steuer's
question puts that removal on the eighth **and tenth** floors, which Bostwick immediately
disputed ("my recollection was … it was from the eighth floor," l. 10272). Nothing measures what
had accumulated by 25 March. Defence witnesses testified the bins "were kept clean" (l. 10485,
l. 10498). The Known ledger's version (line 25 — "Cuttings pile under the cutting tables between
rag collections; the dealer took away more than a ton in mid-January") is exact and should be
reused verbatim in the diagnosis.

**M6. Margaret Schwartz at the door is stated as fact but rests on one notes account.**

Line 57: "*was last seen at the Washington Place door.*" The witness evidence is Kate Alterman's,
in Stein's notes (pp. 1155–1158 / l. 28390–28402); Bostwick asserted it in opening (l. 365). Given
that the whole indictment turned on where and why she died, this must be attributed: "on Kate
Alterman's account, recorded in Stein's notes of the lost volume."

**M7. Option A's hardware is asserted, not established.**

Line 39: "*Take the key out of the equation: the door opens from inside without one.*" The record
establishes that the tenant controlled the keys, the watchmen and the closing routine (Alter,
p. 1753 / l. 50663–50673; Harris, p. 1853 / l. 54670) and that in summer the firm simply left the
door open (Wortman, l. 52357). It does not establish that a tenant could alter the landlord's
door hardware, and the chapter's own Constraint line says "you did not build the doors." *Repair:*
either lead option A with the personnel/routine version, which is unambiguously available, and
mark the hardware version as inferable; or find local support. Brief item 4 is otherwise
satisfied — the options are temporally and institutionally available.

**M8. The record's only candidate "warning" is neither used nor disposed of.**

`CONTEXT.md`'s mechanism runs `promise → warning → commitment`. The chapter never says whether
there was a warning. The one thing in the record is Bostwick's cross of Samuel Bernstein,
p. 1560 / l. 42802–42808: "Weren't complaints made to you by various employees that they should
be permitted to go down at night by the Washington place way on account of the crowd on the
Greene street stairs? A. No, sir." Alleged by the prosecutor, denied by the witness, not
established. That is a finding, and it is the strongest single fact for a "reasonable practice,
bad outcome" reading. *Repair:* one sentence in the Judgement or the "Reasonable practice"
diagnosis — the record establishes no pre-fire warning to the owners about the ninth-floor door;
the only attempt to establish one was a question the witness denied. `CONTEXT.md` requires the
chapter to say where the mechanism does not fit.

### LOW

**L1.** Line 85: "the fire started in them and spread, in the prosecutor's word, 'like tinder'."
Bostwick applied "like tinder" to the *material*, not to the spread: "some of it has been
introduced in evidence — it was like tinder" (p. 2153 / l. 60160; also opening, l. 209). Re-attach
the phrase to the cuttings.

**L2.** Line 89: "*Doors opened inward on landings too small for outward swing.*" Stronger than
Whiskeman, who said the doors "could have opened, but they would have clogged the passage-way"
and that outward swing would have needed four feet against a passage of 3 ft 9 in on Washington
Place, 3 ft 1 in on Greene Street (pp. 59–61 / l. 2307–2331). Restate as "landings where an
outward-swinging door would have blocked the stair."

**L3.** Line 43: option C's "*there has been no fire in the loft since 1902 that pails could not
handle*" parses correctly but reads as "no fire since 1902." Harris admitted five fires, three of
them after 1902, including one in a cuttings basket about 1909 and one in a box on the ninth
floor at night, both put out with pails (pp. 1860–1862 / l. 54925–54990). Since the Known ledger
already has this, the option-C line can simply say "three since, all put out with pails."

**L4.** The receipts are a single ~600-word run-on paragraph. The exemplar (ch. 07) uses grouped
bullets by source with claim-to-locator mapping. Restructure into four or five bullets (trial
record — routine and rule; trial record — the day; Vol. II notes, marked; Cornell archive pages;
reuse). No net word cost, large usability gain.

**L5.** The receipts cite "Lock exhibit and expert dispute *(notes)* pp. 1904–1996 / l. 28675–28897;
Bostwick p. 2167 / l. 60622" for a claim the prose never makes. The lock found with the bolt shot
and the expert dispute over whether heat could shoot it (Hurwitz, notes pp. 1940–1996 /
l. 28677–28897) is the physical evidence on the disputed question and appears nowhere in the
chapter. Either use it in one clause in "What happened" — it is the reason the door question could
not be settled — or drop the receipt.

**L6.** `sources/06-triangle/cornell-legacy.html` is in `MANIFEST.md` (lines 103–110) expressly to
source the Factory Investigating Commission sentence, but is absent from the chapter's receipts.
Same for `trial-records-introduction.html`, which is the source of the death toll.

**L7.** Dossier line 136 gives stair width as "p. 52 / l. 2014"; the chapter receipts give p. 53.
The transcript page marker at l. 2010 is 53. The chapter is right; correct the dossier.

**L8.** Line 18's "at 4:45 on Saturdays" is horizon-legitimate (it is the standing Saturday
quitting time, l. 155) but naming the exact minute of the fire in the role card primes the
outcome. Consider "a quarter to five on Saturdays" or simply "at the quitting bell."

### Suspected problems I could not support

The brief asked me not to assume these are real. Four of the five are not, or not in the form
suggested:

- **"The chapter may adopt the prosecution's narrative because it is the famous one."** Partly
  true, but not where expected. The chapter never says the door was locked; lines 59, 75 and 101
  are unusually disciplined ("It does not tell you the door was locked; that was litigated and
  remains disputed"; "'They locked the door' is therefore an incomplete diagnosis even where it
  is true"). The prosecution-leaning is confined to the *routine use* question (H3).
- **"'Keep the door unlocked' may be presented as costless."** Not supported. Option A names its
  exposure ("a second, unwatched way out of a loft you have been searching for years"), the theft
  purpose is in the cold open and the ledger, and the absence of a legal duty is in the Constraint
  line. This is handled well.
- **"The eighth and ninth floors may be blurred."** Not supported — this is a strength. The
  telautograph reaching only the tenth, Brown's door on the eighth, the partition on the ninth,
  Blanck's door on the tenth and the absence of a superintendent on the ninth are all kept
  distinct and correctly attributed.
- **"The fire escape, elevator and Greene Street evidence may be undercounted."** Not supported in
  the counterfactual section, which is the chapter's strongest analytic passage: the fire escape
  still ends above a skylight, the elevators still stop, the ninth still gets no call, and the
  chapter explicitly refuses to count the saved ("The record cannot count them, and the chapter
  must not"). The one genuine undercount is Brown's crowd evidence (H5).
- **"Kate Alterman's testimony… must not be quoted as verbatim transcript."** **Supported** — see
  B1.

## What survives

These are the elements a revision must not lose.

1. **The decision itself.** "That is a decision about a control, not about a padlock. It stands
   whether or not the door was locked at 4:45." This sentence is why the chapter can be written
   against an acquittal, and it is the book's method working at full strength.
2. **Lines 75 and 79.** The paired "On the defence's best evidence… On the People's evidence…"
   construction, and the separation of decision, execution and outcome quality that follows
   ("The execution of the rule was, on the record, ordinary: watchmen watched, keys hung on
   strings, an inspector found nothing wrong"). This is the best prose in the pilot at handling
   a contested record.
3. **The counterfactual's refusal to count.** "Some would have lived; some would have met the
   eighth-floor crowd or the smoke and not. The record cannot count them, and the chapter must
   not." Also "Restoring the door does not restore the minutes" — the honest concession that
   makes the diagnosis credible.
4. **"The people who inherited it."** Six named ninth-floor workers with a state, a choice and an
   outcome each, including two defence witnesses given exactly the same treatment as the People's
   — Levantini's cable and Greenspan's face full of flame sit beside Bucelli's table and Weiner's
   two attempts. "None of these people set the exit rule. Each spent the margin it left them."
5. **"Where this stops being true."** Four bullets, one of which genuinely reverses the
   recommendation (a second exit into a smoke-filled unenclosed stair may only move the crowd —
   sourced to the two defence witnesses who opened this one and turned back). Satisfies AGENT.md
   pre-ship test 5, which is the test chapters usually fail.
6. **The afterlife section.** Both memories named, both marked as afterlives, neither used as
   evidence; the distinction between "flattens a litigated door into a fact" and "treats a reform
   as proof" is precise.
7. **The transfer and field exercise.** "Try its release without you and without your key. If it
   does not open, that is the decision." Solo-actionable, observable, recordable, and it does not
   require the reader to run a factory.
8. **The locator discipline.** Forty-five for forty-five is the strongest source performance in
   the pilot so far; the failures are of attribution and selection, not of citation.
