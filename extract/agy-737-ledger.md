# Boeing 737 MAX Evidence Ledger

## 1. THE DESIGN CHANGE — WHAT AND WHEN
- **RECORD** | OIG-Timeline p. 9 / l. 306: Boeing developed MCAS for the 737 MAX 8 to compensate for changes in aircraft handling characteristics and to provide consistent handling qualities.
- **RECORD** | OIG-Timeline p. 25 / l. 996: MCAS was originally designed for high-speed conditions such as upset recovery and wind-up turns.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 203 / l. 6794: "In March 2016, Boeing determined that MCAS should be revised to improve wings-level, flaps up, low Mach stall characteristics."
- **RECORD** | House p. 168 / l. 9123: Boeing Coordination Sheet, Revision D, March 30, 2016, detailed the MCAS requirements.
- **RECORD** | OIG-Timeline p. 23 / l. 897: (paraphrase) MCAS authority was increased from 0.55 degrees to 2.5 degrees, an increase of over 300 percent.
- **RECORD** | House p. 28 / l. 1352: The redesign in March 2016 meant MCAS operated from a single AOA sensor, rather than multiple inputs.
- **RECORD** | House p. 110 / l. 5825: As originally designed, MCAS was only capable of activating above a certain G-force and AOA. The G-force requirement was removed.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 248 / l. 8591: MCAS design on single AOA inputs made it vulnerable to undesired activation; its repetitive activation was possible.
- **RECORD** | OIG-Timeline p. 11 / l. 392: "Boeing did not submit formal safety risk assessments related to MCAS until November 2016 and January [2017]"

## 2. WHO OWNED IT — ROLES
- **RECORD** | House p. 5 / l. 189: Michael Teal served as Vice President and 737 Chief Project Engineer.
- **RECORD** | House p. 5 / l. 183: Keith Leverkuhn served as Vice President (VP) and General Manager of the 737 MAX program.
- **TESTIMONY** | House p. 126 / l. 6754: Both Mr. Leverkuhn and Mr. Teal said they were unaware of the redesign details (single sensor, 2.5 degrees, repeat activation) despite Teal approving the redesign.
- **RECORD** | House p. 105 / l. 5535: Mark Forkner (Chief Technical Pilot) requested in March 2016 to remove MCAS from differences training and the FCOM.
- **RECORD** | House p. 32 / l. 1604: Boeing's ODA (Organization Designation Authorization) prepared and approved internal Coordination Sheets but there is no record they were shared with the FAA.
- **FINDING** | House p. 81 / l. 4276: The FAA BASOO and AEG employees were not adequately informed by Boeing's Aero-B or ODA about MCAS changes.

## 3. WHAT THE SAFETY ASSESSMENT ASSUMED
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 203 / l. 6808: Uncommanded MCAS operation was classified as "Major" equivalent to a 3-second mistrim.
- **FINDING** | JATR p. 34 / l. 1447: Test pilots are told that recovery action should not be initiated until 3 seconds after recognition, as per AC 25-1329 / 25.671 guidance.
- **FINDING** | JATR p. 35 / l. 1511: The 3-second reaction time assumption dates back decades and may not be appropriate for automatic trim systems.
- **RECORD** | House p. 124 / l. 6622: A 10-second response in the simulator was described as "catastrophic" in a 2012 internal Boeing finding, which Teal was unaware of.
- **FINDING** | EAIB p. 242 / l. 8368: The System Safety Assessment did not validate single AOA input erroneous activations; failure rate assumptions were assessed on high-speed conditions only.
- **FINDING** | KNKT p. 195 / l. 7933: The assumption was that flight crews would treat the failure as a runaway stabilizer.

## 4. WHAT THE FAA SAW
- **FINDING** | OIG-Cert p. 18: The DOT OIG found weaknesses in FAA's certification and delegation, noting a vast majority (79 of 91) of certification plans were delegated.
- **RECORD** | House p. 32 / l. 1583: Internal Coordination Sheets were not shared with the FAA.
- **RECORD** | KNKT p. 200 / l. 8212: Beginning in March 2016, Boeing proposed removing MCAS from the FCOM.
- **RECORD** | House p. 105 / l. 5535: Boeing sought and received approval from the FAA's AEG/FSB to remove MCAS from the differences training.
- **RECORD** | FAA-RTS p. 55 / l. 2480: The FAA-RTS describes what the FAA would have required if the full scope of MCAS (single point of failure, 2.5 degrees) was properly communicated.

## 5. INTERNAL WARNINGS BEFORE THE FIRST ACCIDENT
- **RECORD** | House p. 164 / l. 8844: Mark Forkner emailed about pressure to avoid sim training, stating, "I just jedi mind tricked this [sic] fools. I should [be paid more]."
- **RECORD** | House p. 164 / l. 8863: Boeing internally pushed heavily against Simulator training to hold firm on Level B differences training.
- **RECORD** | House p. 178 / l. 9638: AOA DISAGREE software-display issue was known in 2017 but Boeing deferred the software fix without grounding the planes or notifying airlines immediately.

## 6. LION AIR 610 — WHAT THE CREW HAD
- **RECORD** | KNKT p. 194 / l. 7889: On the previous flight (JT043), a third pilot recognized the trim runaway and instructed the use of cutout switches. The maintenance log only partially reflected the issue.
- **RECORD** | KNKT p. 40 / l. 1620: The AOA sensor was replaced with a unit from Xtra Aerospace, which had a 21-degree bias.
- **RECORD** | KNKT p. 26 / l. 951: Accident timeline: MCAS activated repeatedly. Stick shaker active. IAS/ALT disagree. FO ran checklists while the Captain fought the trim, culminating in handing over control before final descent.
- **FINDING** | KNKT p. 264 / l. 10453: KNKT listed nine contributing factors, including uncommanded MCAS function operation equivalent to 3-second mistrim, lack of documentation in manuals, and the 21-degree biased sensor. The crew knew nothing about MCAS as it was not in the manuals.

## 7. ETHIOPIAN 302 — WHAT THE CREW HAD
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 18 / l. 779: Accident sequence: AOA sensor failure at rotation. Stick shaker activated. MCAS activated.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 30 / l. 1184: Cutout switches were used, but manual trim was not moved successfully due to aerodynamic forces on the stabilizer. Cutout was re-engaged later.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 126 / l. 4461: To resolve a mistrim of -1.5°, the manual trim wheel would have to be rotated through 22.5 revolutions, requiring immense force.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 299 / l. 9957: Post-Lion-Air FAA Emergency AD 2018-23-51 and Boeing bulletin had been issued.
- **FINDING** | EAIB Probable Cause focused on MCAS design flaws. NTSB disagreed partially (NTSB-EAIB), pointing more toward crew thrust management and not following the AD procedures precisely.

## 8. THE AFTERLIFE
- **RECORD** | FAA-RTS p. 55: MCAS redesign implemented two sensors, one activation per high AOA event, and limited authority to ensure manual trim could counter it.
- **FINDING** | JATR p. 39 / l. 1694: JATR Recommendation R3.8: The FAA should review the prescriptive use of 3 seconds for pilot response.
- **FINDING** | NTSB-ASR p. 13 / l. 389: NTSB ASR-19-01 Recommendation A-19-11: "The NTSB recommends that the FAA require that Boeing..." [evaluate assumptions regarding pilot response times and workload].

## 9. THE HUMAN BILL
- **RECORD** | KNKT p. 26: Lion Air 610: 189 fatalities.
- **RECORD** | EAIB p. 18: Ethiopian 302: 157 fatalities.
- **RECORD** | KNKT & EAIB (Crew details): Captain and First Officer hours and experience are detailed in the respective accident reports.

## 10. NOT FOUND / GAPS
- **DISPUTED** | House and FAA-RTS focus heavily on Boeing's omission of information, while EAIB focuses exclusively on the technical failure and KNKT highlights maintenance (Xtra Aerospace). EAIB and NTSB-EAIB explicitly disagree on the crew's contribution (NTSB highlighting failure to reduce thrust).
- **NOT FOUND** | Specific internal emails regarding the 10-second simulator test by the test pilot were referenced but the raw test data document itself was not fully replicated in the text extractions here.
