# Adversarial review: Boeing 737 MAX

## Verdict

**REVISE.** The chapter has a real decision, a real horizon and a receipts layer that survives testing — I checked eighty-eight locators against the ten local text files and every page/line pair resolved correctly, which is better than any draft in this book so far. But the decision screen is contaminated in two places that matter more than anything else in the chapter: the evidence ledger hands the role-holder a document the record never places in the room, and Option C is argued with a simulator re-check that the only source for it dates merely as "after this change." The cold open then inverts the day's chronology, making the request to delete MCAS from the manual a premise of the approval rather than the consequence the record says it was, which is hindsight wearing the costume of a timestamp. Downstream, the counterfactual's Ethiopian bound rests on the single proposition the US accredited representative formally disputed, and the Judgement calls "unambiguous" a causal attribution that the Indonesian report explicitly refuses to rank. None of this requires new sources — every repair below is available in the nine reports already archived — but the chapter cannot ship until the decision screen is clean, because a decision-forcing case that quietly stacks the deck is not a decision-forcing case.

## Decision contract audit

**Decision question the draft actually teaches.** *How much operator recovery may a design assume when the operator has not been given the system model needed to diagnose the failure — and what must be re-evidenced when the configuration changes?* This is the dossier's question (§9), and the Transfer, Boundary and Field exercise all serve it without drift. The four-part boundary test ("Where this stops being true") is the cleanest statement of it and is the chapter's best paragraph.

That said, the chapter part-teaches three other things it does not own: certification delegation (the 79-then-91-plans material in "What happened" plus competing diagnosis 3), the training-cost incentive (three undated Forkner quotations), and the AOA DISAGREE deferral of August 2017 (a separate governance failure, downstream of the decision, ninety words). Each is defensible as a competing diagnosis; none is defensible at its current length. Exclusivity is recoverable by compression, not by rewriting.

**Best decision point.** 30 March 2016 holds, and is correctly located: it is the last moment at which the safety case, the certification deliverables and the manual could all still be re-opened by one role-holder, and it is dated, owned and documented (House p. 111 / l. 5878–5895; OIG-T p. 23 / l. 890–909). But the chapter never *argues* for it against the two earlier candidates it puts on the page itself — the June 2013 framing decision and the November 2012 simulator result. See H4.

**Nearest overlapping chapter.** Triangle. Both turn on a safeguard whose safe failure depends on a person acting in seconds; both ask what a control's release depends on. The draft's closing paragraph distinguishes them correctly ("this chapter asks whether the person expected to save the machine in four seconds was told the machine existed"), and the distinguisher is genuine: Triangle's operators knew the door existed and could not open it; here the crews did not know the system existed. Challenger is the second overlap and is further away. No collision.

## Findings

### BLOCKER

**B1. The ledger puts a document in the room that the record does not put in the room.**

> "**Known — in front of you.** Coordination Sheet Rev. D, dated today… Rev. D also carries a functional hazard assessment… The same sheet has said, since October 2015 and will say through 2018: 'A slow reaction time scenario (>10 seconds) found the failure to be catastrophic…'"

**Why it fails.** The chapter's declared honesty device is the split between "in front of you" and "in the building, not in the room." The device fails at its single most consequential item. The record establishes what the Leadership Review *was*: a presentation titled "737 MAX / Stall Characteristics – Mitigation" including a "Stall Characteristics Go-Forward Plan," after which the flight-control configuration was approved (House p. 111 / l. 5878–5889 — verified, and the underlying evidence is an email of 1 April summarising the meeting). It does not establish that Coordination Sheet Rev. D, its functional hazard assessment, its four-second assumption or its ten-second sentence were on the table. The chapter's own dossier lists this under **Open**: "What Leverkuhn and Teal were shown in the room on 30 March 2016 beyond the presentation title and go-forward plan." And the only direct evidence about the role-holder's knowledge runs the other way: Teal told Committee staff that when he approved the redesign he was unaware MCAS ran on one sensor, could activate repeatedly, or that the ten-second result existed (House p. 28 / l. 1350–1355 — verified; restated and confirmed at p. 123–124 / l. 6625–6638).

**Evidence status.** Author inference presented as Known. The chapter is entitled to say the sheet existed and carried that sentence (House p. 32 / l. 1575–1584 and p. 122 / l. 6522–6544 establish six sheets 2015–2018, and footnote 678 lists Rev. D of 30 March 2016 among them — verified). It is not entitled to place it in the role-holder's hands.

**Repair.** Move the FHA, the four-second assumption and the ten-second sentence into a third tier — "On the sheet you are approving, if anyone reads it to you" — and state in one sentence that the record establishes the sheet's contents and the fact of approval but not what was shown in the room, and that the role-holder later testified he did not know the ten-second result. That sentence *strengthens* the case: the chapter's question becomes sharper when the signature is separated from the sheet. Keep in "in front of you" only what the record places there: the redesign proposal, the new envelope, the new authority, the stall-characteristics problem, and the go-forward plan.

**B2. A post-decision fact is inside the decision screen, arguing for the option the record shows was chosen.**

> "**C. Approve as presented.** The high-speed case was analysed and remains the critical one; **the hazard classes were re-checked in the simulator and did not move**; the crew procedure exists."

**Why it fails.** The re-check is real, but its only source dates it no more precisely than *after* the change: "In March 2016, Boeing determined that MCAS should be revised… The preliminary hazard assessments of MCAS were re-evaluated **after this change** by pilot evaluation in the motion simulator and determined to have not changed the hazard classification" (NTSB-ASR p. 5 / l. 250–253, footnote 10 — verified). Nothing places it before or on 30 March. The chapter has put an unavailable fact into the most protected structure in the book and used it as the strongest argument for Option C. CONTEXT rule 1 admits no exception here.

**Repair.** Cut the clause from Option C. Option C is still strong on the record's own terms: the high-speed regime was judged the critical one (JATR p. 54 / l. 2403–2405 — verified), the classification was Major, and the runaway-stabilizer memory item exists. Restore the re-check where it belongs — in "What happened" and in the Judgement, where the draft already uses it well ("Boeing's own re-check said no").

**B3. The day's chronology is inverted, and the inversion does causal work.**

Three sentences:

> cold open: "At 11:16 that morning the program's chief technical pilot **has already sent** an email…"
> ledger: "the crew who must catch it in four seconds **have been told, as of this morning, that it does not exist**."
> reconstruction: "the manual, which **by evening had nothing**."

**Why it fails.** The record puts the approval first and the email second. House: "**Just hours after** the approval for MCAS's redesign was granted, Boeing sought, and the FAA approved, the removal of references to MCAS" (p. 27 / l. 1283–1285 — verified). The Committee's own timeline graphic dates the Leadership Review 8:00 a.m. and the Forkner email 11:16 a.m. (House p. 107 / l. 5667–5670 — verified). And the Committee's framing assumes the meeting came first: Teal was asked whether he knew of the FCOM request "prior to the meeting that approved the redesign of MCAS" and answered "I don't recall" (House p. 126 / l. 6746–6751 — verified). The deletion was also not effective that day: Boeing requested it by email and "FAA approved it verbally **in a subsequent meeting**" (OIG-T p. 24 / l. 933–942 — verified). So "by evening had nothing" is false, and "have been told, as of this morning" is false twice over.

**What it costs.** Reversing the order converts a consequence into a premise. It lets the reader infer that the approval was made in the knowledge that the crew would not be told — which is exactly the coordination the House committee said it could not establish ("it is unclear if these actions were coordinated," p. 27 / l. 1290–1292, verified, and cited correctly elsewhere in the draft). The chapter is careful about this in the Reconstruction and careless about it in the cold open, and readers weight cold opens.

**Repair.** Restore the order: the review approves in the morning; hours later the chief technical pilot asks the AEG to delete the function; the FAA agrees verbally at a later meeting. Change the ledger line to what the record supports — that as of this morning the function is in the draft FCOM and in the differences tables, and a request to remove it is about to be made by a team the role-holder does not supervise. Change "by evening had nothing" to a dated statement of when the manual lost it.

### HIGH

**H4. The Reconstruction never adjudicates the earlier candidates it has already put on the page.**

The chapter presents the June 2013 decision to describe MCAS as "an addition to [the existing] Speed Trim" because "if we emphasize MCAS is a new function there may be a greater certification and training impact" (House p. 26–27 / l. 1225–1271 — verified), and the November 2012 simulator result in which a Boeing pilot took more than ten seconds and called the condition catastrophic (House p. 120–121 / l. 6426–6473 — verified). Both are earlier. Both were reversible. Neither is ever tested against 30 March 2016. CONTEXT rule 5 ("An earlier event is not automatically a cause. Find the earliest reversible and consequential decision") and TEMPLATE §7 both require the comparison, and the Triangle exemplar performs it explicitly ("Later decisions… spent margin. They did not design it").

**Repair.** Three sentences in the Reconstruction. November 2012 was a data point, not a decision — nobody was asked to choose, and the question the engineer raised ("should we step up to catastrophic…?") was addressed to colleagues, not decided by anyone the record names. June 2013 was a decision, and a real candidate, but it governed a function that then existed only in a corner of the envelope no line crew enters; it became consequential only when the envelope was widened. That is the argument for 30 March, and the chapter should make it rather than assume it.

**H5. The counterfactual's Ethiopian bound is the one proposition the US accredited representative formally disputed, and the dispute is not carried into the bound.**

> "Flight 302 had the name in an emergency directive and the procedure in memory, cut the switches, and could not turn the wheel at 340 knots against 2.5° of mistrim; **knowledge did not restore the authority the design had taken**… On this record the counterfactual changes the accidents in degree, not in kind, and design stays causal."

**Why it fails.** The NTSB's May 2022 comments say the opposite in terms: "appropriate crew management of the event, per the procedures that existed at the time, **would have allowed the crew to recover the airplane** even when faced with the uncommanded nose-down inputs" (NTSB-US p. 2 / l. 63–66 — verified); and "Appropriately countering uncommanded nose-down inputs with manual electric trim nose-up inputs, as was expected per crew procedure described in the FCOM bulletin and the emergency AD, would have resulted in control column forces remaining in a controllable regime during the flight, **including when the stabilizer trim cutout switches were in the CUTOUT position**" (NTSB-US p. 5–6 / l. 258–262 — verified). The chapter cites NTSB-US twice elsewhere and knows the dissent exists; it simply does not let it near the counterfactual, which is where it bites. The chapter's conclusion may still be right — the EAIB found the E-CAB "was not able to reproduce the force that would have been required from the ET302 crew" (p. 120 / l. 4252–4256, verified) and that reproducing the recorded control actions "resulted in loss of control of the Airplane" (l. 4248–4249, verified) — but presenting a contested bound as a settled one is precisely what AGENT.md's priority 1 forbids.

**Repair.** Two sentences inside the counterfactual: state that the EAIB's testing could not reproduce the forces and that the NTSB holds the airplane remained recoverable with correct thrust and manual electric trim; then say that the record cannot settle which is right, and that the chapter's "degree, not kind" conclusion is therefore held with lower confidence on 302 than on 610. The Judgement's confidence ladder already has the right shape; extend it here.

**H6. The 302 crew are stripped of the two decisions the record gives them.**

> "The Ethiopian 302 captain, 29, 8,122 hours, 103 on the MAX; the first officer, 25, 361 hours in total… **They had the procedure. They ran it. The trim wheel would not turn.**"

**Why it fails.** They did not simply run it. The record establishes two departures, one of which the EAIB itself states: the throttles reached takeoff thrust and "remained there" (EAIB p. 132 / l. 4610–4613 — verified), with the airspeed passing 340 kt and an overspeed warning active until the end (EAIB p. 28 / l. 1107–1111); and the cutout switches were returned to normal, of which the EAIB writes "**the decision to return the stab trims cut-out switches back to normal was not consistent with the AD nor the FCOM bulletin**," while adding that the captain evidently judged the column force beyond what he and the first officer could sustain (EAIB p. 232 / l. 8005–8014 — verified). The emergency AD had specifically emphasised "that the switches stay in the CUTOUT position for the remainder of the flight" (NTSB-ASR p. 11 / l. 560–562 — verified).

This is the Human rule inverted. CONTEXT requires the person who inherits the danger to have "an objective, incomplete information, constraints and agency of their own." The draft gives the JT043 captain full agency, the 610 captain partial agency, and the 302 crew none — they become the proof of a design claim. That is using them as props, even though the props are sympathetic ones. It is also the reason the passage reads as a rebuttal to the "foreign pilots" afterlife rather than as a reconstruction, which the chapter's own afterlife section says it will not do.

**Repair.** Give them the two decisions with their reasons: thrust left at takeoff power in a cockpit where the autothrottle had silently stayed in ARM with no alert (EAIB p. 26 / l. 1058–1063 — verified, and the report notes "There was no flight crew document… that states this could happen"), and the switches restored after a failed manual-trim attempt because the column force was unsustainable. Then note that the NTSB and the EAIB disagree about what those decisions mean. The chapter loses nothing and gains the one thing it is short of: two people deciding under load.

**H7. The Judgement overclaims with "unambiguous," and contradicts the chapter's own competing diagnosis.**

> "Course C was chosen, and **the record is unambiguous that its cost was borne by 346 people** and two crews…"

**Why it fails.** The KNKT lists nine contributing factors and states in its preamble that "The presentation is based on chronological order and **not to show the degree of contribution**" (KNKT p. 217 / l. 8917–8918 — verified). The EAIB and the NTSB disagree in print about both the vane failure mechanism and crew performance. And the chapter's own competing diagnosis 2 says: "An updated assessment could have re-affirmed Major; Boeing's simulator re-check did. What killed was one vane driving repeated full-authority trim." A Judgement that calls the attribution unambiguous cannot coexist with a diagnosis section that says the pivot might have changed nothing. CONTEXT's voice rule asks for bounded claims and names this failure mode directly.

**Repair.** "Course C was chosen. What its choice cost cannot be apportioned from this record — the KNKT declines to rank its nine factors and the two accident bureaus disagree about the second aircraft — but the record does establish that two crews were expected to do in four seconds what nobody had shown a line crew could do at that speed with those alarms." Same force, no overclaim.

**H8. The cold open closes in the future tense.**

> "Nothing has gone wrong. **The airplane will pass its stall demonstrations, and the pilots who fly it will never need to know why.**"

**Why it fails.** AGENT.md pre-ship test 2: "Did any sentence smuggle the outcome backward?" This one does, twice — the second clause is the chapter's verdict delivered before the reader has been asked anything, and the irony only works if the reader already knows the ending. Compare Triangle, whose equivalent line stays in the present and states only what the arrangement is doing: "Nothing has gone wrong. The rule that everybody leaves one way, past one man, is doing what it was made to do."

**Repair.** End on the present. Something in the register of: "Nothing has gone wrong. The airplane has a stall-characteristics problem, a software mitigation, and a manual that still mentions the mitigation by name." That is disturbance without verdict, which is what TEMPLATE §1 asks for.

**H9. The Southwest clause is in the role card's hand while the diagnosis section says the record does not put it in the room.**

Role card: "Under the December 2011 launch contract, **Boeing owes Southwest at least a million dollars per airplane** if the FAA requires simulator training; Southwest has 200 on firm order."
Competing diagnosis 5: "The incentive is documented; **the record does not show it in the room on 30 March**."

**Why it fails.** Both cannot stand. House p. 31 / l. 1526–1533 (verified) documents the contract term and the 200 firm orders; it does not establish that the role-holder knew of the clause or weighed it. The dossier itself lists "the Southwest training clause" under *In the building*, not *In the room* — the draft has promoted it into the Objective bullet, which is the strongest possible placement. That is the incentive being put in the role-holder's hand without evidence he held it, which is the specific weakness the brief asked me to test, and it is real.

**Repair.** Keep in the Objective only what the record supports: Level B differences training as a program design objective (House p. 32 / l. 1598–1600 — verified, and it is Teal's own word). Move the Southwest clause to "in the building, not in the room," where it is both true and more interesting.

**H10. The 610 sequence omits that the crew never identified the runaway and never used the cutout.**

The draft has the captain answering more than twenty inputs, the first officer looking "for a checklist that fit," the handover, and the recorder stopping. It never says that the crew "started the IAS DISAGREE Non-Normal Checklist, **but did not identify the runaway stabilizer**" (KNKT p. 20 / l. 685–686 — verified) and never moved the switches to CUTOUT. For a chapter whose entire question is what recovery a design may assume, this is the load-bearing observation — it is the exact difference between 043 and 610 on the same airplane with the same fault, and the chapter's own counterfactual depends on that contrast. Its absence is the one place where the draft under-states its own case.

**Repair.** One clause in the 610 paragraph. It costs nothing and it is the strongest sentence available to the chapter.

### MEDIUM

**M11. Trim units and degrees are silently merged.** "about 2.5° of mistrim at 340 knots, forty revolutions to correct." The "about 2.5 degrees at 340 kt" is EAIB footnote 20 (p. 124 / l. 4400–4402 — verified). The forty revolutions is derived from **−2.7 units** at fifteen turns per unit: "To resolve a mistrim of -2.7 units as on ET302, the wheel would need to be rotated through 40.5 revolutions" (EAIB p. 125 / l. 4425–4429 — verified). Units and degrees are different scales, and the EAIB itself carries the inconsistency (body says −2.7 units, footnote says about 2.5 degrees). Repair: use one measure — "about two and a half units of mistrim at 340 knots, roughly forty turns of the wheel to correct" — and drop the degree sign.

**M12. 0.55° and 0.6° are used as if they were one number, with a percentage that belongs to only one of them.** The ledger: "rises from about 0.6° to 2.5°—an increase of over 300 percent." The "over 300 percent" is the OIG's, computed from 0.55° (OIG-T p. 23 / l. 897–899 — verified). The 0.6° is House and KNKT (House p. 110 / l. 5825–5826 — verified). The chapter then uses 0.6° again for the simulation limit (KNKT) and 0.55° for the AEG's understanding (OIG-T) — each correct in its own place, but the reader is never told that two source families report the same original authority differently. Repair: one footnote-grade clause at first use ("0.6° in the Committee's record, 0.55° in the Inspector General's — the same limit, differently reported"), then pick one for the arithmetic.

**M13. Prosecutorial register borrowed from the House where the OIG and JATR are more measured.** Three instances: "**The record puts all three inside your program before you signed**" (House's rhetorical construction, and it sits in the ledger where the chapter is meant to be neutral); "the record is unambiguous" (H7); and the training-clause paragraph, which fires three Forkner quotations — "HOLDING FIRM THAT THERE WILL NOT BE ANY TYPE OF SIMULATOR TRAINING," "jedi mind tricked this fools" — **without dates**. Both are from March and December **2017** (House p. 164 / l. 8842–8867 — verified), a year and more after the decision, in a paragraph about 30 March 2016. The chapter then correctly says the record does not place the incentive in the room, but by then the quotations have done their work. Repair: date both quotations in the sentence that uses them, and cut one.

**M14. The AOA DISAGREE deferral is a separate case wearing this chapter's clothes — and is mis-stated.** The ninety-word passage in "What happened," plus "an airplane whose disagree alert had been **switched off** by a software pairing nobody told them about." The alert was never enabled: Boeing's software contractor "inadvertently paired the alert with an optional feature—an AOA indicator—which only approximately 20 percent of MAX customers purchased" (OIG-T p. 30 / l. 1249–1253 — verified). Nothing was switched off. Repair: compress to one sentence, fix the mechanism, and move the deferral decision into competing diagnosis 3 where governance lives.

**M15. The Jakarta engineer is listed as an inheritor and given nothing.** "The engineer in Jakarta who read that log." The record gives him a decision: he flushed the left pitot/static ADM and cleaned the elevator feel computer connector per the fault-isolation manual, the tests passed, and he released the aircraft at 1930 UTC on 28 October (KNKT p. 38–39 / l. 1528–1537 — verified). He acted correctly on the information he was given, which is the chapter's point about the log entry. Repair: give him the clause, or drop him from the list. A named person with no state is a prop.

**M16. Template functions missing.** Transfer has no "**What does not transfer**" (the dossier supplies it: Part 25 certification law; the cockpit). The Field exercise lacks four of TEMPLATE §13's six elements — the single question, the recording method, the review condition and the classification — all of which the Triangle exemplar carries as labelled bullets. Repair: add the "does not transfer" line; convert the field exercise to the six-bullet form. Roughly forty words net.

**M17. The counterfactual states no assumptions and quietly imports Option A's price.** TEMPLATE §9 requires "State assumptions." More substantively, the changed variable is "Keep the hardware and the software exactly as certified" plus FCOM entry plus differences-course page plus "one simulator session" — but a simulator session *is* the Level B reversal the whole decision was structured to avoid. The counterfactual therefore is not "one variable"; it silently includes the schedule and training cost that Option A was priced at. Repair: either run the counterfactual on disclosure alone (FCOM plus differences course, no simulator) and say what the simulator would add, or state plainly that this counterfactual costs Level B and is therefore Option A's outcome, not a free one.

**M18. Pre-reveal prolepsis in the ledger.** "**Unknowable.** Which crew, on which morning." This tells the reader an accident is coming before the decision screen. Triangle's equivalent ("How three hundred people behave when the room fills with smoke") is anchored to a hazard already established two tiers above it in Known — five fires on the premises. Here the anchor is thinner and the phrasing is fatalistic. Repair: anchor it to the Unknown directly above ("How often an AOA vane fails high in service") — e.g. "Which airframe, and how often" — or cut.

**M19. The two-minute flap extension is compressed out of the 610 narrative.** NTSB-ASR: after the first two automatic inputs, "the flaps were extended for almost 2 minutes. However, the flaps were then fully retracted, and automatic AND stabilizer trim inputs occurred more than 20 times over the next 6 minutes" (p. 2 / l. 71–74 — verified). The draft runs the first ten-second input straight into the twenty. The omitted interval is the one in which the system stopped — the fact the NTSB used against EAIB Finding 78 about flap position, and precisely the sort of thing a told crew might have exploited. Repair: one clause.

### LOW

**L20.** "an AEG that decided training on a verbal request" (competing diagnosis 3) inverts the record and contradicts the chapter's own cold open: the request was by email, the FAA's *approval* was verbal (OIG-T p. 24 / l. 938–942 — verified).

**L21.** "no additional flight testing" is quoted with the range House p. 116–117 / l. 6175–6300; the phrase is at **l. 6305, p. 118** (verified). Extend the range.

**L22.** "mis-calibrated by about 21° **at a repair shop in Florida**" — the receipt gives only KNKT p. 20 / l. 670–671, which states the 21° bias but not the location. The Xtra Aerospace attribution is at KNKT p. 216 / l. 8890–8893 and is hedged ("most likely improperly calibrated at Xtra Aerospace"). Add the locator and the hedge, or drop "in Florida."

**L23.** "Since June 2013 the program **has described MCAS to the regulator** as an addition to speed trim" is receipted to House p. 26–27 / l. 1225–1271, which documents the *plan*, not the practice. The practice is at House p. 112 / l. 5941–5948 quoting the OIG ("Boeing presented the software as a modification to the existing speed trim system"). Add the second locator.

**L24.** Receipts line for the counterfactual gives "EAIB p. 122–125" with no text-file lines, breaking the convention the Receipts preamble states. Use l. 4325–4431.

**L25.** "The single angle-of-attack input… unchanged" is receipted to NTSB-ASR p. 4 / l. 159–166, which says "as measured by **either** AOA sensor." The single-sensor-per-flight architecture is OIG-T p. 15 / l. 542–545 (verified) and is not cited anywhere in the chapter.

**L26.** "deferred the fix to 2020" is attached to the August 2017 discovery; House states "This decision to defer the fix to 2020 was made in **September** 2017" (p. 131 / l. 6965–6966 — verified).

**L27.** "four years into a five-year program" — the OIG says "**more than** 4 years into the 5-year certification process" (p. 27 / l. 1092–1093 — verified).

**L28.** The block quotation of the ten-second sentence is House's transcription of **Rev. C**, 19 Oct 2015 (fn 681, verified); House establishes only that the language "remained virtually unchanged" across the six sheets. "The same sheet has said, since October 2015" is fair, but if the exact Rev. D wording is load-bearing anywhere, say the quoted text is Rev. C's.

**L29.** Southwest: House says Boeing was obligated to **discount** each airplane by at least $1 million, not to pay (p. 31 / l. 1528–1530 — verified). "Owes… per airplane" is close but not the instrument.

**L30.** "the June 2013 framing… was a considered choice, **minuted**" — House's source is "the email that summarized the meeting minutes" (p. 26–27 / l. 1269–1271 — verified). Minor, but the chapter is otherwise scrupulous about the difference between a document and a report of a document.

### Suspected problems I could not substantiate

The brief asked me not to assume these were real. Four are not, and one is only partly.

- **Option A is not presented as costless.** "Cost: weeks in a schedule counted in days, and a real risk to Level B." That is the correct price and the chapter states it in the option itself. No finding.
- **The accidents do not leak into the pre-reveal material.** I read the cold open, role card, ledger and decision screen line by line. No death toll, no grounding, no DPA, no JATR/NTSB/House finding, no 2020 redesign, no accident. The forward-looking material is confined to the two items at H8 and M18. The horizon discipline is otherwise the best in the draft.
- **The disputed SSA question is handled honestly.** The chapter says the assessment was "never re-issued beyond Rev. C" (JATR p. 54 / l. 2399–2405 — verified) *and* that the hazard classes "were re-checked in the simulator and did not move" (NTSB-ASR p. 5 / l. 250–253 — verified) *and*, in the counterfactual and Judgement, that "Boeing's own re-check said no." Both sides are in the chapter and neither is stated as the other's refutation. This is right, and it is the hardest thing in the case. Keep it exactly as it is — subject only to B2, which is about *placement*, not about the claim.
- **The ET302 vane mechanism dispute is marked.** "the Ethiopian bureau says an electrical fault, the NTSB says a bird" — correct and correctly receipted (NTSB-EAIB p. 1–2 / l. 33–58 — verified).
- **The Forkner email date is not a genuine dispute.** House cites 30 March 2016, 11:16:45, at l. 1322, 4311, 5565, 5633, 6765, 7945–7947 and 11293; "March 3, 2016" appears only at l. 4962 and 6774, same subject line. This is a footnote typo, not a conflict in the record. Resolve it and delete the CHECK.
- **The FCOM deletion does not carry more weight than the record supports — quite.** The draft bounds it explicitly: "the FAA's own November 2018 directive, which *did* tell crews, was followed by 302. Telling was necessary and was not sufficient." That is the right bound and it is honestly drawn. The problem is not the weight; it is the chronology (B3) and the missing dissent (H5).

## What survives

Do not lose these in revision.

1. **"Where this stops being true."** The four-condition test — recognisable as itself; time demonstrated *at the configuration actually shipped, with the alarms that will actually sound*; recovery trained; failure inside a model the operator already holds — is the best transferable object in the book so far, and the verdict it delivers ("Rev. D failed the first and the fourth on the day it was approved, and the second was never attempted at low speed") is exact, bounded and independent of the accidents.
2. **The three-documents image in the Reconstruction.** "Each was internally coherent. They stopped describing the same airplane" is the chapter's thesis in twelve words. Keep it; fix only "by evening had nothing."
3. **The three-flight response spread.** "In three flights the responses were three different things: 043 recovered in three-forty; 610 held the airplane for six minutes and lost it at handover; 302 ran the procedure and could not physically finish it. None matched the assumption." This is the empirical core, correctly receipted (NTSB-ASR p. 5 / l. 219–229; p. 8 / l. 373–378 — both verified), and it does the work the accident narrative is otherwise too long to do.
4. **The honest handling of the SSA dispute.** "Re-checked under the old assumptions" versus "never looked at" — and the Judgement's split confidence ("high that the safety case should have been re-issued; moderate that re-issuing it would have changed the classification, because Boeing's own re-check said no"). This is the hardest evidential problem in the case and the draft solves it.
5. **The counterfactual's refusal to let disclosure off the hook.** "Telling was necessary and was not sufficient," anchored to the November 2018 directive that ET302's crew had. Keep the structure; add the dissent.
6. **The afterlife section.** Ninety-odd words that name both memories, refuse both, and end on the KNKT's nine unranked factors. It is the model for how this book should handle a case with a live public quarrel.
7. **The receipts apparatus and the internal-document rule.** "Internal Boeing documents are known only as quoted in the official reports and are cited that way" — stated in the preamble and observed throughout. Every locator held.
8. **The JT043 crew as decision-makers.** Three minutes forty, twelve activations, a third pair of eyes, and a log entry that fit the form. This is the chapter's best piece of human inheritance and its most useful evidence.
