# Adversarial review: Boeing 737 MAX

## Verdict

**REVISE.** The central case is publishable: 30 March 2016 is a reversible engineering gate, the operator-recovery question transfers, and the chapter does distinguish the un-reissued SSA from the later simulator re-evaluation. The forced choice is not yet valid because “Known — in front of you” contains facts the record does not show Teal had, a 2019 investigator finding, a later FAA agreement, and the 2020 remedy. The counterfactual then makes a factual error—the emergency AD did not name MCAS—and turns an unresolved prevention question into “degree, not kind.” The outcome also alternates between the EAIB and NTSB accounts of ET302 without preserving the disagreement every time crew execution or physical recoverability is used causally. Keep the case, but rebuild the pre-choice horizon, narrow the action Teal owns, bound the counterfactual honestly, correct the receipts, and cut at least 550 body words.

## Decision contract audit

The draft actually teaches: **When a design change enlarges a failure that depends on rapid human recovery, may the engineering approver accept the old recovery analysis and omit the changed system from the operator's model, or must approval be conditioned on revalidation and disclosure?** That is close to the dossier's exclusive question, but the current A/B/C screen also asks the reader to decide architecture, certification-plan control, regulator communication, training, and manual content. The later diagnosis section then offers delegation, maintenance, crew execution, and financial incentive as if they were additional decisions rather than rival explanations.

The best decision point remains the 30 March 2016 leadership approval, provided the player is allowed only to approve, reject, or condition engineering sign-off. It is a year before certification and is the first dated high-level gate at which the low-speed/2.5-degree configuration could be tied to a fresh recovery case. The record does **not** establish the contents of Teal's briefing beyond its title, go-forward plan, approval, and later testimony; if the chapter insists on a fully populated technical hand, the evidence-richer fallback is the 22 June 2016 MCAS Review, though that record does not identify an equally clean individual owner.

The nearest overlap is **Triangle**: both ask whether a safeguard remains real when its safe state depends on a person acting under load. This case earns its place by asking whether that person was given the system model and demonstrated time, rather than whether a physical release depended on a key. Challenger is a secondary overlap on proof at a go/no-go boundary.

## Findings

1. **BLOCKER — The choice screen gives Teal a hindsight-selected hand.** Exact phrases: “**Known — in front of you**” (line 27), “Nobody in the record can say what study those numbers rest on” (line 31), “it is roughly what the FAA will require in 2020” (line 49), and “the hazard classes were re-checked in the simulator and did not move” (line 51). Why it fails: the surviving record shows the meeting title and approval, but not that Rev. D, single-sensor logic, repeat logic, the ten-second result, or the FHA were presented to Teal; Teal told House staff in 2020 that he did not know three of those facts. JATR's failure to find a validating study is a 2019 finding, the simulator re-evaluation occurred after the change, and the remedy is from 2020. “In the building” is literally honest, but methodologically it gives the reader precisely the internal facts later investigators selected while admitting that the role-holder did not have them. The complete horizon flag is: line 5's detailed low-speed/software account is later reconstruction beyond the demonstrated slide content; line 7's FAA agreement occurs later; line 9's “will pass” and “will never need to know” are future outcomes; lines 13 and 19 use 2020 testimony; line 17's 2013 framing and line 19's Southwest clause were organizational records not shown to Teal; line 23 overstates that the regulator had “accepted” the framing; lines 27–29 place unproved briefing content and “through 2018” before the reader; lines 31 and 49 import 2019/2020 knowledge; line 33 deliberately supplies facts Teal later denied knowing; line 35 turns an omission request into crews having been “told” nonexistence; line 39's combined-alert scenario is author technical inference later shown not to have been evaluated; and line 51 imports a post-change re-evaluation. The certification dates and scheduled test window at line 20 are contemporary constraints. **Evidence status:** meeting/approval are records quoted by House; Teal's ignorance is later testimony; JATR and the 2020 design are later findings/actions; exact 2016 briefing content remains unknown. **Repair:** restrict “in front of you” to what the record places there; move the ten-second/single-sensor/repeat material to reconstruction as discoverable program information; move JATR and the redesign after commitment; replace C's future simulator result with the contemporary rationale that the existing high-speed analysis and runaway procedure were believed to cover the condition.

2. **BLOCKER — The counterfactual contains a false premise and an unsupported verdict.** Exact phrases: “Flight 302 **had the name in an emergency directive**,” “the counterfactual changes the accidents **in degree, not in kind**,” and “**Telling was necessary** and was not sufficient” (line 103). Why it fails: OIG-T states that neither the Boeing bulletin nor the emergency AD specifically mentioned MCAS (p. 32 / l. 1307–1312). ET302 had the expanded runaway procedure and Ethiopian had incorporated the bulletin/AD; a separate multi-operator message described MCAS characteristics. JT043 shows that recovery without the name was possible, while ET302 does not test the chapter's full intervention of disclosure **plus** a faithful simulator session. Earlier recognition, fuller electric nose-up trim, thrust reduction, or earlier cutout could have prevented accumulated mistrim, so a change in kind remains plausible. **Evidence status:** AD content is record; EAIB and NTSB disagree about execution and recoverability; the kind/degree result is author inference not settled by either investigation. **Repair:** say ET302 had the directive and operator communications, not that the AD named MCAS; list the counterfactual assumptions; conclude that disclosure alone was insufficient on ET302, while whether disclosure plus representative simulator training prevents either accident remains unresolved.

3. **HIGH — The alternatives exceed the documented authority and make hindsight's remedy an option.** Exact phrases: “what the FAA is told and what the manual says” (line 22), “Three courses are within your authority” (line 45), and option B's two sensors/one shot/limited authority (line 49). Why it risks the method: Teal was engineering authority, but the record does not establish that he directly controlled Forkner's technical-pilot chain, AEG communication, FCOM disposition, or delegation decisions. House records Teal answering “I don't recall” about the FCOM request and says Forkner was in another chain. The KC-46 comparison proves an architectural precedent existed; it does not prove that porting that shape to the MAX was a ready, one-year option, and “months”/“new software qualification” are unsourced estimates. **Evidence status:** Teal's role and redesign approval are supported; authority over the bundled actions and the time estimates are not established. **Repair:** make A “condition my engineering approval on a configuration-specific safety review and require the responsible owners to resolve certification/AEG/FCOM consequences”; make C unconditional approval; remove B from the forced choice or recast it as “return to the design team for alternatives” without specifying the 2020 answer.

4. **HIGH — The judgement claims outcome causation that the chapter's own rival evidence denies.** Exact phrase: “the record is unambiguous that its cost was borne by 346 people” (line 107). Why it fails: the record is unambiguous about fatalities and MCAS's role, not that choosing A would have prevented both accidents. Boeing's simulator re-evaluation retained the classification; JT043 recovered without the system name; ET302 had the post-Lion-Air procedure; KNKT lists nine unranked contributors; NTSB says ET302 remained recoverable with appropriate electric trim and thrust management. Reissuing the analysis buys an explicit, reviewable question, integrated alerts, FAA visibility, and a chance to change design/training—it does not guarantee a different classification or outcome. **Evidence status:** the fatality count is fact; the attribution of all 346 deaths to C is author causal inference. **Repair:** judge C as poor decision quality because it accepted a materially changed recovery assumption without configuration-matched evidence, then state moderate confidence—not certainty—that the gate would have changed design, disclosure, training, or either outcome. Delete “cheap” unless cost is sourced.

5. **HIGH — ET302's contested crew-performance account is preserved once and then laundered into fact.** Exact phrases: “They ran it. The trim wheel would not turn” (line 79), “302 ran the procedure and could not physically finish it” (line 89), and “knowledge did not restore the authority the design had taken” (line 103). Why it risks the method: EAIB supports the unsuccessful manual-wheel attempt and extreme force account, with limits on reproducing the exact force; NTSB's May 2022 comments say inappropriate electric trim and thrust management left a controllable airplane in a high-speed mistrim and that the operator had not adequately prepared the crew. The outcome paragraph names the NTSB dissent, but the inheritance, reconstruction, and counterfactual revert to EAIB's causal reading. **Evidence status:** the sequence and the first officer's report are recorded; “could not physically finish” and “unrecoverable” are investigator conclusions disputed by NTSB. **Repair:** at every causal use, write the fork: EAIB says the prescribed manual recovery became physically impracticable; NTSB says earlier electric trim and thrust control would have kept forces manageable. Do not use either side as the counterfactual bound without the other.

6. **HIGH — The chapter owns more than one decision.** Exact phrases: A requires reissuing an FHA, SSA and certification plans, briefing AEG, and retaining MCAS in the FCOM; B redesigns three architectural properties; the judgement separately endorses analysis, eventual design, and disclosure (lines 47–49, 107). Why it risks the method: the transferable question is human-recovery validation, but the forced answer becomes a package of analysis governance, regulator disclosure, training policy, and architecture. That lets the chapter appear to prove whichever remedy the reader favors. **Evidence status:** all are real causal mechanisms, not straw men; their bundling is editorial. **Repair:** make the exclusive gate “approve only after the recovery assumption has been revalidated for the changed configuration and the operator model needed by that assumption is resolved.” Treat sensor architecture, regulator visibility, and training cost as possible outputs or competing diagnoses, not coequal decisions.

7. **HIGH — The human inheritance section lists people but withholds several of their decisions.** Exact phrases: the AR and June engineer were “both answered and both dropped” (line 75), and “The engineer in Jakarta who read that log” (line 77). Why it fails: House does not reproduce an answer to the December 2015 AR question; “dropped” is author inference. The Jakarta engineer is given no observed state, troubleshooting choice, or release-to-service decision, although KNKT supplies them. The AEG representative and delegating engineer have information states, but the section does not show what each could still ask, retain, or refuse. The accident crews themselves receive substantially better agency in the outcome chronology. **Evidence status:** AEG misunderstanding and delegation ignorance are supported; the two “dropped” claims are not equivalently documented; KNKT records the log, maintenance actions, and release. **Repair:** replace the roll call with two short inherited decisions—AEG/FAA document control and Jakarta troubleshooting/release—then retain one choice from each cockpit. Crew ages and hours are accurate and reasonably balanced; the suspected unfair use of age/hours is not supported so long as those numbers are not made causal.

8. **MEDIUM — The reconstruction does not earn why March 2016 beats the earlier candidates.** Exact phrase: “The decision of 30 March 2016 was…” (line 85). Why it risks the method: June 2013 deliberately framed MCAS as an addition to speed trim to limit certification/training impact, and the November 2012 simulation produced the ten-second question. The draft introduces both before commitment but never tests them as earlier reversible candidates. **Evidence status:** the 2013 minute and 2012 email are quoted by House; November 2012 is a warning/question rather than a clean decision, and MCAS was then a narrower, lower-authority function. **Repair:** add a compact comparison: 2012 generated evidence but no identified owned gate; 2013 framed a materially narrower function; 30 March is where the wider authority and old recovery assumptions met an identifiable approval one year before certification.

9. **MEDIUM — The same-day narrative adopts House's prosecutorial inference despite the source conflict.** Exact phrases: “At 11:16 that morning” (line 7) and “by evening”/“coincided” (line 85). Why it risks the method: House cites the same Forkner email as both 30 March at 11:16 and 3 March in a later footnote, and House itself says coordination is unclear. OIG uses 30 March as its timeline date but says FAA approval was verbal at a subsequent meeting. Thus the draft may say the request and approval review are recorded in the same period, but cannot use precise same-day sequencing as causal glue without preserving the conflict. **Evidence status:** explicitly disputed date; coordination unknown; later FAA agreement. **Repair:** remove “already,” “that morning,” “by evening,” and “just hours”; state the date conflict in the receipt and say the redesign approval and deletion request occurred in March 2016, possibly the same day, with no evidence they were coordinated.

10. **MEDIUM — Internal Boeing documents are narrated as documents in hand.** Exact phrases: “The sheet says” (line 27), “The engineer who wrote it up asked” (line 33), and “The 22 June MCAS Review minuted” (line 59). Why it risks source integrity: the packet does not contain the Coordination Sheets, emails, or meeting minutes; it contains official reports quoting or characterizing them. The receipts disclaimer is accurate but too remote to prevent the prose from implying direct inspection. House's aerodynamic-origin discussion also relies partly on later journalism, whereas NTSB-ASR and FAA-RTS give a more measured official account. **Evidence status:** report quotation of an internal record, not the underlying record itself. **Repair:** attribute on first use—“OIG reports that Rev. D…” and “House quotes an internal email/minute…”—and prefer NTSB-ASR/FAA-RTS for the engine/nacelle and control-law description.

11. **MEDIUM — Several exact claims outrun their attached CHECK.** Examples: “a 1967 wing” and “wind tunnel and then simulator” (line 15); Florida repair-shop causation (line 63); “189 people” attached only to the recorder lines (line 65); all-91 delegation and January 2017 SSA (line 61); and the three-flight conclusions attached only to NTSB's multiple-alert discussion (line 89). Why it risks the method: most are locally resolvable, but the current receipt does not support the whole sentence. “1967 wing” is also imprecise: the official record establishes a 1967 original type certificate/design lineage, not that the shipped wing is simply a 1967 wing. **Evidence status:** mixed; see CHECK and locator tables. **Repair:** narrow the prose or add the exact local locator; do not let one receipt at paragraph end appear to prove every clause.

12. **MEDIUM — The body remains roughly 540 words over the maximum before the needed qualifications are added.** Exact evidence: stripping headings and CHECK comments from lines 1–125 yields about 3,744 words, against a 3,200-word ceiling. Why it risks the method: the excess is mainly duplicated certification chronology and repeated causal summaries, not necessary cockpit state. **Evidence status:** direct count. **Repair:** use the compression map below; target about 3,000–3,100 words so the required uncertainty and human decisions fit without returning over 3,200.

13. **LOW — The field exercise is usable but incomplete against the house contract.** Exact phrase: “In your next three design or change reviews…” (line 123). It supplies an observable moment, recording fields, and a pass/fail condition, but not a clear review condition or the required decision/execution/uncertain classification; its final same-day trigger is also more case-specific than necessary. **Evidence status:** editorial. **Repair:** add one short classification field and a review trigger such as “recheck whenever configuration, cues, response time, or training changes.”

Suspected problems not supported by the audit: the draft does distinguish “SSA not re-issued” from “FHA re-evaluated in the simulator”; it handles 0.55 versus about 0.6 degrees fairly; it correctly explains four seconds as one second recognition plus three seconds reaction; “more than twenty,” 3:40, 12 activations, 340 knots, 90-plus pounds, and about 40 revolutions all have local support when attributed to the named report. ## What survives

- The title and quiet cold-open premise—“Nothing has gone wrong”—fit the book.
- The exclusive transferable mechanism is strong: human response time is a safety component that must be re-evidenced when configuration and cues change.
- March 2016 is reversible and consequential without needing the crashes as proof, if the hand is kept honest.
- “Re-checked under old assumptions” is substantially more accurate than “never looked,” and the draft already preserves that distinction.
