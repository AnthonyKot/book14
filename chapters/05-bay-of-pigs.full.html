Case 5 · full chapter with receipts

The plan that kept its approval

Bay of Pigs · 16 April 1961 · Decision-forcing case

At about 9:30 in the evening, the telephone rings at the CIA command post.

McGeorge Bundy tells Deputy Director Charles Cabell that the exile bombers will not be permitted to strike Cuban airfields at dawn. No further attacks may occur until Brigade 2506 captures an airstrip inside Cuba and missions can be launched from there. Further argument should go to Secretary of State Dean Rusk.

Cabell and Richard Bissell reach Rusk's office at about 10:15. They describe what their planners expect without the strike: greater danger to the ships, more air attack on the beachhead and attrition among the exile B-26s meant to isolate the battlefield. In Cabell's later account, Rusk says he understands the military importance but considers the political cost overriding. Ambassador Adlai Stevenson's position at the United Nations may become impossible to sustain.

Then Rusk offers them access to the President.

This chronology comes from Cabell's 9 May memorandum, endorsed by Bissell, and the later Cuba Study Group record—not a transcript. Do the officers who fear the consequences carry the issue one level higher?

Role card

  • You are: Charles Cabell, Deputy Director of Central Intelligence; Richard Bissell, the CIA officer responsible for the operation, is with you.
  • Objective: Preserve a viable landing while remaining within the President's ban on overt United States combat.
  • Clock: Minutes before the cancellation must reach bomber crews preparing in Nicaragua; the landing force is close to commitment.
  • Primary resource: Time spent to preserve operational protection and ownership.
  • Authority: You can accept and transmit the order, explain its operational effects, and accept Rusk's offer to appeal directly to Kennedy. You cannot reverse presidential policy yourself.
  • Constraint: Cabell later wrote that by the meeting with Rusk it was physically impossible to stop the overall landing. That was his assessment, not a surviving time-stamped response from the ships.

What is on the table

Known to this role

  • Bundy has conveyed the decision: no dawn strike until missions can originate from an airstrip held inside Cuba.
  • The 15 April attack used eight exile-flown B-26s. Initial pilot reports claimed heavy damage, but CIA planning proceeded on the understanding that Cuban combat aircraft remained.
  • The plan uses exile B-26s against aircraft and ground forces. Cabell and Bissell describe the dawn strike as important protection for ships and troops.
  • Kennedy has prohibited overt use of United States forces. Brigade leaders have been told not to expect them.
  • Rusk says another strike could make the United States position at the United Nations unacceptable.
  • Rusk offers direct access to Kennedy. Cabell and Bissell have not yet taken it.

Inside the government, but not established as received by this role that night

  • Stevenson warned at 6 p.m. that evidence of attacks launched outside Cuba could gravely alter the United Nations debate. His earlier defense had assumed pilots defecting from inside Cuba.
  • Earlier military evaluation warned that one attacking Cuban aircraft might sink much of the invasion shipping if surprise were lost.
  • Kennedy had asked earlier in April for the last point at which he could delay or cancel the operation and had been told 16 April.

Reasonably inferable

  • A second attack from outside Cuba will further damage deniability, regardless of the markings on the aircraft.
  • Withholding a protection planners described as vital exposes shipping and troops to greater air attack.
  • Silence after being offered an appeal will be operationally indistinguishable from accepting the change.

Possible

  • The surviving Cuban aircraft may be too few, damaged or poorly employed to defeat the landing.
  • The dawn strike may destroy enough of them to preserve the ships and beachhead.
  • A successful landing may produce an uprising, but resistance reports do not establish its scale or readiness.
  • A rapid mitigation—closer naval protection, revised air tasks or faster unloading—may reduce some of the added exposure without reversing the order.

Unknown

  • The exact number, condition and location of operational Cuban aircraft at dawn.
  • Whether the exile bombers will find and destroy them.
  • How rapidly Cuban forces can concentrate against the beachhead.
  • Whether meaningful support inside Cuba will appear, and on what timetable.
  • Whether a recall, diversion or delay remains physically possible. Cabell believes it does not; the surviving record does not show a fresh query to the force confirming it.
  • The international response to another strike—or a failed landing.

Later information—excluded from the choice

  • The Cuban aircraft's effectiveness and the ships, supplies and aircraft that will be lost.
  • The speed of the Cuban ground response.
  • The Cuba Study Group's later conclusions about the strike, ammunition shortage and structural weaknesses of the operation.

Unknowable from the surviving record

  • Whether Cabell or Bissell privately expected Kennedy to authorize overt rescue if the beachhead collapsed.
  • Why they ultimately decided that a direct appeal would serve no purpose beyond the explanation they later recorded.

The decision

A. Appeal directly to Kennedy

Accept Rusk's offer. State what changes operationally, what remains uncertain and why the modified plan needs presidential ownership. The appeal may restore the strike or confirm its cancellation. It may also consume minutes needed to organize mitigation.

B. Accept the decision and mitigate

Transmit the cancellation immediately. Re-task exile aircraft for close support, seek whatever ship protection remains within the ban and tell field commanders what has changed. This respects the political limit and preserves time. It cannot reproduce a strike against aircraft still on the ground.

C. Verify whether the landing can still be recalled or redirected

Send an immediate query to the operational force rather than relying solely on the Washington assessment. Do not assume that recall is available: Cabell's later account says it was not. If the answer confirms commitment, return at once to A or B. The query may identify a remaining option, but it may instead spend scarce time proving that the option has gone.

What do you do? What fact would make you change your answer?

Do not answer “raise the concern.” Decide whether you personally carry it to the person who can reverse the order.

What happened

Cabell and Bissell did not accept Rusk's offer to contact Kennedy. In Cabell's later account, they judged that the Secretary had understood their argument and that a further appeal would serve no useful purpose.

Cabell's memorandum separates Bundy's 9:30 call from the later message to the pilots, which it says caught them in their cockpits. It does not independently time send and receipt—or establish that recall remained possible.

The landing continued.

Brigade troops began going ashore at about 1 a.m. on 17 April. Cuban aircraft attacked after daylight. The freighters Rio Escondido and Houston were hit; the former carried ten days of reserve ammunition and other supplies. Other ships moved away from the beachhead. Ammunition became the immediate constraint on the force ashore.

A second decision: Blue Beach, 18 April

At about 8 p.m., according to the later Cuba Study Group narrative, CIA headquarters offered Brigade commander José Pérez San Román a way to leave the beachhead.

His evidence horizon was narrower than Washington's had been:

  • Known: Red Beach had been lost; Blue Beach was under pressure from tanks and aircraft; ammunition was scarce; requests for resupply and air support had not restored a secure supply line.
  • Unknown: whether another supply attempt would arrive, how long the position could hold, and what further United States support—if any—would be authorized.
  • Choice presented: accept personal evacuation or remain with the force. The record does not establish that he was offered evacuation of the whole brigade.

San Román refused. On the afternoon of 19 April, with tanks approaching and ammunition nearly gone, his last recorded message said that equipment was being destroyed and the survivors were taking to the woods. Organized resistance ended. More than 100 brigade members were killed and almost 1,200 captured. Cuban soldiers, militia and civilians were also killed or wounded. The surviving brigade prisoners remained in Cuba for about twenty months.

San Román's choice concerned command after operational options had collapsed; it could not restore missing capabilities. The sequence supports a bounded conclusion: surviving Cuban aircraft materially disrupted shipping and resupply. It cannot show that one more strike would have overthrown Castro.

The people who inherited the new plan

Landing teams tried to unload before daylight. Troops defended the roads and rationed weapons. Brigade air crews flew repeated missions from Nicaragua; American contract pilots later entered combat. These choices affected survival and the duration of resistance. They could not reproduce the cancelled airfield attack.

Cuban defenders were actors, not resistance in an American failure story. Local forces detected the landing and fought for the access routes; commanders sent surviving aircraft against the ships and concentrated ground forces. A declassified Cuban collection preserves orders and reports from 17–20 April, but this draft does not yet have an equal evidentiary view into every Cuban unit or casualty.

Most of this reconstruction comes from United States institutions investigating themselves. Those records cannot establish Cuban intentions or popular attitudes.

The decision before the decision

The offered appeal is the last sharply documented decision in Washington before the landing. The operation's contradiction is older.

The March 1960 concept called for cadres to organize resistance inside Cuba. It grew into an amphibious assault while the rule of plausible deniability remained.

The Cuba Study Group later identified November 1960 as an earlier reversible point: transfer the growing military operation to Defense and accept visibility, reduce it to something genuinely covert, or abandon it.

That produces three separate evaluations.

  • Decision quality: Withholding an attributable air strike was coherent with a real political objective. Cabell and Bissell nevertheless believed the change carried grave operational consequences and declined an offered route to the official who could own both sides of the choice.
  • Execution quality: Critical approvals and changes travelled through oral briefings and ad hoc meetings. The cancellation moved from a Washington decision to crews already preparing to fly without a documented, integrated review of what protection or exit remained. The brigade then faced command and resupply decisions made far from the beach.
  • Outcome quality: The beachhead collapsed. The brigade was killed, captured or dispersed, and the United States suffered the attribution it had tried to limit.

The error was not simply choosing politics over military advice. Presidents are supposed to impose political limits. The narrower decision failure was allowing a material change to pass without using the offered route to make the owner of the objective own its operational consequences too.

Competing diagnoses

Kennedy cancelled the strike that could have saved the brigade

This diagnosis has substantial support. Operational planners knew Cuban aircraft survived. Those aircraft attacked shipping, drove away resupply and contributed directly to the ammunition shortage. The Cuba Study Group called cancellation of the dawn strike probably the most serious of the air restrictions.

The operation was structurally incompatible with its political limits

This explains more. A 1,400-man landing, foreign bases, bombers and ships could not remain meaningfully deniable once fighting began. The uprising was unverified and the guerrilla fallback unrealistic in the terrain and encirclement that developed. The final air restriction appeared so consequential partly because the plan had little margin.

The brigade lost through execution and logistics

There is evidence here too: difficult unloading, remote command, failed air drops and later timing errors. Better execution could have extended resistance, but the plan still exposed its logistics to a known surviving air capability.

The strongest diagnosis combines the first two. The strike decision materially worsened the brigade's problem; the operation was already too marginal for the restored strike to promise its political objective.

Counterfactual: put the strike back

Restore the dawn attack. Keep everything else: the same brigade, landing site, uncertain internal support, long flight from Nicaragua, Cuban response and prohibition on overt American combat.

In the Cuba Study Group's 25 April record, Colonel Jack Hawkins later reported that late-afternoon photographs on 16 April showed surviving Cuban aircraft concentrated at San Antonio de los Baños. This was retrospective operational testimony, not a fact shown to have reached Cabell or Bissell during the Rusk meeting. A successful attack might have destroyed more aircraft, protected the freighters and kept ammunition moving ashore. That could change the later problem in kind: instead of immediate isolation and shortage, the brigade might retain a supplied beachhead and a possible sea withdrawal.

But the counterfactual cannot award perfect bombing. Some aircraft may survive or be missed. The exile B-26 force still has limited crews and no fighters. Cuban ground forces still greatly outnumber the landing force. The uprising still may not occur. The Study Group judged that even with air control, long-term survival was unlikely without substantial support from the Cuban population or overt United States assistance.

Restoring the strike improves the chance of survival and evacuation. It does not turn the overthrow of Castro into the expected result. That is why “Kennedy failed to provide air cover” is a partial explanation, not a complete chapter.

Judgement

Best-supported diagnosis: failure to escalate a material plan change to the authority that owned the objective, with moderate-to-high confidence.

Rusk's political reason for withholding the strike was legitimate. Cabell and Bissell's warning about the danger to ships, aircraft and the force ashore was also material. Rusk offered them a route to Kennedy; they declined it. Given their own assessment, accepting the change without that appeal was weaker than making Kennedy explicitly choose between the political cost and the operational exposure. An appeal might still have ended with the strike cancelled.

The record cannot establish what Kennedy understood, whether an operational recall was still available, or whether a dawn strike would have succeeded. It supports the more limited finding that the two officers who described the consequences did not use the direct access offered to them. It also shows a system in which the political and operational claims were never forced into one documented decision.

Memory divided the failure into presidential failure, CIA incompetence, exile betrayal and, in Cuba, the victory at Playa Girón. After the prisoners' 1962 release, brigade survivors presented their flag to Kennedy in Miami. This afterlife does not settle the decision in Rusk's office.

Where this stops being true

A material change does not always require escalation to the highest authority.

A degraded plan can continue when a tested fallback exists, the objective is reduced, or withdrawal creates greater immediate danger. Good contingency plans delegate authority and thresholds for doing so.

The rule reverses when escalation consumes the last useful minutes, authority is already delegated within clear thresholds, or the supposed prerequisite was merely a preference. Escalate changes to feasibility, acceptable loss or required authority—not every deviation from the first draft.

What transfers

Maintain a short list of conditions that make a plan approvable. Write them as claims:

This plan is acceptable only if ______ remains true.

When one becomes false, do not ask only, “Can we continue?” Ask, “Who has approved the plan that now exists?” If an escalation route is open, recording dissent is not a substitute for using it.

What does not transfer

Ordinary projects do not carry presidential authority or the risk of interstate war. Do not imitate covert secrecy or compressed command.

Solo-actionable version

Keep one line in your own decision record:

Prerequisite → current status → evidence → owner → reauthorization trigger

You do not need authority to stop the organization. You can make the changed plan visible, identify its owner and ask that person to accept or reject the new risk.

Field exercise

For your next three consequential projects:

  1. Record one condition without which the plan should be modified or stopped.
  2. Mark the first observable moment that condition changes.
  3. Ask one question: Who is authorizing the plan we have now?
  4. Record the answer as continue, modify, stop or no owner.
  5. After the result, classify the problem as decision, execution or uncertain without changing what was known at the marked moment.

Pass condition: all three projects contain a named prerequisite and owner; every changed prerequisite produces an explicit new decision.

Review condition: if the project succeeds despite an unowned change, do not erase the entry. Ask whether success validated the fallback or merely concealed that no one approved the risk.
