Case 8 · full chapter with receipts

Colonel and above

Coalition Provisional Authority · Baghdad and Washington · May 2003 · Decision-forcing case

On 19 May 2003 the Administrator of the Coalition Provisional Authority sends a two-page memorandum to the Secretary of Defense. "In the coming days I propose to issue the attached order (Tab A)," it begins, "carrying forward the de-Ba'athification effort by dissolving Saddam's key security ministries. I could also implement the policies in Tab B on payments related to the dissolved entities." Tab A abolishes the Ministry of Defence, the intelligence services, the armed forces and the regime's paramilitaries. Tab B says who will be paid afterwards, and who will not.

The memorandum has a paragraph to hold on to. "There is obviously something that catches in the craw about paying anything to people who worked at the heart of Saddam's regime. Against this, however, we must balance the risks of serious discontent, increased terrorism, and much higher crime rates that may result if we cut [off] all military and security sector pensioners in a heavily militarized society."

It has another. "There will be some delay in making these payments, because… we do not have employee rosters, or contacts with administrative officials of the dissolved entities."

Nothing has been signed. The order is a draft, the payment policy a separate outline, and the man who wrote both has been in the country for a week. This chapter does not decide whether the invasion was lawful or wise, whether de-Ba'athification was right, what troop levels were needed, or whether this order "caused" the insurgency. None of that is needed to play what follows.

The hand you are dealt

You are L. Paul Bremer III, Presidential Envoy to Iraq and Administrator of the CPA. You landed in Baghdad on 12 May, less than two weeks after the President appointed you: sixty-two, a career diplomat, no service in the Middle East, no post-conflict operation behind you, no Arabic. The city was being looted. On 16 May you issued Order No. 1, de-Ba'athification, conceived in Washington; the same day Iraqi political leaders urged you to widen it to the security services and the army, and you promised a proclamation on both within days.

Your adviser for national security and defence is Walter Slocombe, formerly Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. He drafted Tab A with the Office of the Secretary of Defense. Under Secretary Feith later wrote, in a memoir the Iraq Inquiry quotes, that you and Slocombe briefed him on 9 May, before you left Washington, on "their plan to dissolve the Iraqi Army."

  • Objective: demonstrate, in your memorandum's words, "that neither Saddam nor his gang is coming back," and begin a force that can in time defend the country.
  • Clock: days. The memorandum asks for concurrence "in principle"; you have told the Iraqi leaders a proclamation is coming.
  • Primary resource: credibility with the Kurdish and Shi'a majority, spent to buy a clean break — against whatever security the old institutions might still supply, and the income of the households they employed.
  • Authority: binding orders in occupied Iraq under the laws of war; Resolution 1483, which the signed order will cite, is adopted on 22 May. You do not set troop levels or the speed of a payroll built without rosters.
  • Constraint: the barracks have been looted and the men have gone home; on the CPA's account, given later by Slocombe, the Kurdish and Shi'a leaders would not accept a reconstituted version of Saddam's army.

Evidence ledger

Known — in front of you. Your own memorandum, including its figure: "some 400,000 employees of the MOD alone." And what Tab A does and does not contain. It dissolves the entities, dismisses their employees from 16 April, cancels their ranks, continues pensions being paid before that date, and announces a New Iraqi Corps. It has no clause about officers, no exclusion from pensions, no termination payment. Those are in Tab B, which your memorandum offers separately: officers of colonel rank and above "will be rebuttably presumed" disqualified and "will not be paid until they establish that they are not"; other dismissed employees get a month's pay; former employees may be hired "as individuals… for example as security guards. They are not hired as reconstituted old units, as soldiers, or as part of the future Iraqi military."

Known — in the building, not in the room. The land-force and CENTCOM plans for the occupation, the latter approved by the President in March, assumed the bulk of the Iraqi Army would be recalled and used, and Coalition leaflets had promised no reprisals to soldiers who laid down their arms. From mid-April the land-force staff negotiated with senior Iraqi officers about restoring order. In early May Garner began working to bring old units back and arranged for the US Government to pay 300,000 soldiers; across the country, brigade commanders had begun reconstituting units. When Slocombe met the land-force commander, McKiernan, and his deputy, Webster — in a room with Iraqi generals who had volunteered to serve — Webster briefed him on their plans and recalls the answer as "No, you are not doing that." All of this sits inside your organisation and with your senior adviser. The record does not establish what reached your desk, or when.

Reasonably inferable. Against signing: your memorandum names the risk of cutting off "military and security sector pensioners" and then offers a policy that cuts off every officer of colonel rank and above; the machinery you call necessary does not exist; and Tab B forbids hiring "reconstituted old units," which is at least consistent with someone reconstituting them.

Possible. That recalled units would fracture or refuse; that a Kurdish and Shi'a reaction would cost more than the dismissal; that the men would wait for their money.

Unknown. How many of the 400,000 can be found and paid without rosters, and how long that takes. How large the replacement force will be and when it will exist. What the presumed-disqualified officers will do meanwhile. Whether the brigades' reconstitution is a few units or many.

Unknowable. What Washington would have said had you asked to wait. Nobody did.

The decision

Strip it to what is actually on your desk this week. Two things: whether the old army ceases to exist now, and whether its officers stop being paid now. Money is not the constraint — Garner has a payroll arranged and the order promises payments. What does not exist is the list of names and the machinery to pay them.

A. Dissolve now, and cut off the officers now. Sign Tab A and Tab B as one order. Every regime force abolished today; every rank cancelled; colonels and above presumed disqualified and unpaid until they prove otherwise; everyone else paid once the rosters exist. Gain: one clean act, and the majority's confidence. Risk: the one you wrote down — 400,000 dismissed men, the officers among them cut off, and no mechanism yet.

B. Do not dissolve yet. Let the American commanders keep recalling and vetting Iraqi units, as they are already doing; keep the men standing by on a payroll; decide what to abolish later, once you know who and where they are. Gain: an existing force, and — as units report in — the names you lack. Risk: you are seen to restore Saddam's army, and the Kurdish and Shi'a leaders withdraw their cooperation.

C. Dissolve the regime's organs today; hold the army and the pay. Abolish now what nobody proposes keeping — the secret police, the Special Security Organization, the Republican Guards, the Fedayeen. Keep the regular army and its officers' pensions on the books for a dated second decision, when the rosters exist. In the memorandum's own terms: issue Tab A, narrowed, and defer Tab B. Your memorandum already keeps three ministries out of the order for stated reasons, and offers Tab B as something you "could also" do. Gain: most of the political signal, a fraction of the human bill. Risk: the line between the army and the regime is contested, and drawing it takes time you have told the Iraqi leaders you will not take.

Commit. Then say what would change your answer. If the answer is "nothing, because the army no longer exists," ask why Tab B needed a sentence forbidding its reconstitution.

What happened

You signed on 23 May. Order No. 2, Dissolution of Entities, is Tab A with Tab B written into it. Section 3(4): a termination payment, in an amount to be determined by the Administrator. Section 3(5): pensions continue, "including to war widows and disabled veterans." Section 3(6): neither payment to any Senior Party Member, and "any person holding the rank under the former regime of Colonel or above, or its equivalent, will be deemed a Senior Party Member," subject to a rebuttal procedure to be prescribed. Section 6(2): the Administrator may grant exceptions at his discretion.

Secretary Powell first learned of it at a principals' meeting on 22 May, when you announced by video link that you would sign the next day: "You couldn't even tell who had decided it." Garner protested the day it was announced.

Within days, former soldiers were at the palace gates. On 26 May five thousand officers and soldiers demonstrated in Baghdad; their spokesman demanded the recall of the old army, the payment of military salaries, and a new government. In Mosul, dismissed soldiers demonstrated for days; Iraqi police shot one dead; in two days of rioting eighteen of Petraeus's soldiers were wounded. The Army's historians record further violence in Mosul a month on, over "the failure to provide the financial assistance promised."

You moved on pay, and the sources date it differently: SIGIR has you modifying the policy "ten days later"; the Army's historians date a monthly stipend for career soldiers to 23 June; you told Congress in 2007 that "it took us a month." The stipend excluded senior party members, internal-security officers and those accused of abuses; whether the colonel-and-above presumption still governed it, or how the exception power was used, this record does not say. Order No. 22 created the New Iraqi Army on 7 August — three divisions in two years, forty thousand men, built by a general who arrived in June with five staff.

In February 2007 you told the House Oversight Committee: "I stand by the decision not to recall the army… The mistake I made, and I admit it, is when we announced that we were going to build the new army, we should have immediately said we are also going to pay the officers from the old army. It took us a month to do that." You added that pensions "were always paid." Section 3(6) as signed barred them to colonels and above, rebuttably, through a procedure that did not yet exist, under an order that reserved to you a power of exception; this corpus does not record how that power was used, so it can neither confirm nor refute the sentence.

The people who inherited it

The men Section 3(6) named: some 400,000 on your count, half a million by SIGIR's; among them every officer of colonel rank and above, dismissed from a date five weeks in the past, owed nothing until a procedure not yet written said otherwise, in a country whose weapons caches were unguarded and known to them.

Forty-five of them were offered a decision in July, when the new army's builders asked the division commanders for former lieutenant colonels to seed the officer corps. They refused to serve without their Saddam-era rank and pay. The builders dismissed them and lowered the bar to major; three-quarters of the new force, almost all its officers and NCOs, would nonetheless have prior military experience. That is the one downstairs choice this record plays out in full: men told their rank was cancelled declining to return without it.

No Iraqi-authored primary source is in this corpus; every Iraqi in this chapter is reported by an American or British investigator. Two reach it through SIGIR's interviews, both retrospective. Ali Allawi: the armed forces "generated considerable sympathy and respect throughout Iraq," and it was difficult "even for the Shi'a, to accept a wholesale dissolution." Laith Kubba: "the measure to dissolve the Iraqi army was not a smart one." Set them against the CPA's account of what the Kurdish and Shi'a leaders would accept, and against the Army historians' finding that on the level of national politics the policy was "largely successful."

Reconstruction

The last documented formal gate on this record is the signature of 23 May. Until it, the recall could still be used, the officers still held rank, and the colonel bar was still a tab. After it, none of that was true.

Decision quality. The record does not support the claim that the risk was unseen. It was named in writing, by the proposer, four days before he signed — and then a payment policy was attached that ran the risk on the cohort best able to organise a response. Whether dissolution was right is arguable both ways; the Army's historians call the premise "reasonable." Whether dissolution-before-mechanism was right is harder to defend, because the man deciding had said what the mechanism was for. Execution: the rosters were not made to exist first; the rebuttal procedure was not written; the stipend came in about a month. The 2007 admission concedes the sequencing point without conceding the decision. Outcome: demonstrations, a riot, weeks of dismissed soldiers awaiting a mechanism, a long gap before the planned force was complete; how much of the insurgency this explains is contested and left contested below.

Now the ownership dispute, which this chapter must not resolve by staging. RAND's 2008 study, as the Inquiry quotes it, places the decision in Washington in early May, before Bremer deployed; Powell could not tell who had decided. Against both: Feith's memoir has Bremer and Slocombe briefing him on their plan on 9 May; the 19 May memorandum proposes, in the first person, and asks for concurrence; and the instrument changed in the Administrator's hands between 20 and 23 May, the colonel bar moving from a separate tab into the order's text. NSPD-24, which would define his formal authority, is classified; nothing in this corpus records an instruction removing his discretion. The honest position: Bremer is the documented proposer and signatory, with apparent but not fully recoverable discretion, inside the circle that formed the policy rather than downstream of it; where ultimate authority sat is unresolved.

Competing diagnoses

  1. Sequencing — this chapter's pivot. Abolition before the replacement force and the payment mechanism existed, by a decision-maker who had written down what the mechanism was to prevent.
  2. The dissolution itself, regardless of timing. Sir David Manning, to the Inquiry: men who might have been co-opted became "natural dissidents and potential insurgents." Petraeus, as SIGIR reports him: "tens of thousands, if not hundreds of thousands, of additional enemies."
  3. Ratification, not decision. At its strongest: on 23 May there was no fielded army to remove; the barracks were looted; a recall might have failed regardless. Slocombe, in a 2004 interview the Inquiry quotes: "We didn't disband the army. The army disbanded itself." The reply is narrower than usually made: what the order changed was legal, organisational and payment capacity — ranks, employment, pensions, the standing of counterparties — not physical capacity. Sir Kevin Tebbit: "Well, true, but not the 10,000 officers."
  4. Diffusion of ownership. No NSC vetting; Powell told the day before; Feith later calling it "an error in judgment" not to have consulted other agencies. On this account 23 May is a symptom.
  5. Order No. 1, not Order No. 2. The colonel bar imports Order No. 1's "Senior Party Member"; where the two orders interlock they must be read as a pair.
  6. Distributed causes. Sir John Sawers, to the Inquiry: "I don't think it is credible to lay… the roots of the insurgency, in the decision to disband the army… It may, in some areas, have compounded it." General Reith adds a narrower effect: without the order "there was still some structure there we could have built on."

Counterfactual: the same order, signed later

Keep Order No. 2 exactly as signed — every entity, every cancelled rank, Section 3(6) intact. Change one thing: it is not signed until the rosters exist, the payments can be made on the day of dismissal, and the officers' rebuttal procedure is published alongside it. No better people, no more troops.

The 26 May spokesman demanded three things: recall of the old army, payment of salaries, a new government. Pay on the day addresses the second and does nothing about the first or third; the later Mosul violence is reported as financial. The colonels are still barred; the planned force still takes as long. Timely payment plausibly reduces some pay-driven confrontations in the first weeks; whether that is a change in kind or in degree the record cannot settle, and beyond the first weeks it is degree at most. Assumptions: rosters could be built in weeks, not months; payment would reach men on dismissal day; the recall would keep running while you waited; the majority's leaders would tolerate the delay; the regime organs would not re-entrench; the protests were mainly financial. Any one failing narrows the gain.

Judgement

Course A was chosen; C was available on the memorandum's own terms; B was in motion in the field — whether the Tab B sentence was written because it was is an inference, marked as such, and not needed for the point that the policy, once adopted, foreclosed it.

Confidence: high that the risk was known to the decision-maker before he signed, because he wrote it; high that the mechanism was known not to exist, for the same reason; moderate that signing before the mechanism made the first weeks worse than they needed to be — the demonstrations were partly about pay, and the claim that they stopped when pay was announced is Bremer's, made in 2007; low on how much of what followed the order explains. What the record cannot establish: what Bremer was told of the objections and when; whether Washington would have let him wait; whether a recalled army would have held. The afterlife — the single blunder that made the insurgency, or the abolition of an army that had already abolished itself — the record supports neither alone.

Where this stops being true

Abolishing an institution before its replacement exists is right when the institution is itself the active danger, when the people it employs are not the ones you rely on for security, and when delay would let it entrench. The Mukhabarat, the Special Security Organization and the Fedayeen meet that test in May 2003; a reader who signs Course C has abolished them. This chapter judges the dispersed regular army and its pension roll differently — a judgement, not a recovered fact; the record also holds the army's history of repression and a real political risk in keeping it. Where the whole of an institution is the danger, waiting for a payroll would be the error.

Transfer

When you dissolve something, the people you dismiss become a decision of yours, not a side effect. What they are owed, on what date, by what mechanism, belongs on the same page as the dissolution.

What transfers: dismissal before mechanism, and its tell — a decision document that names a risk on page two and defers the thing that would contain it to "the next step."

What does not transfer: occupation law, a viceroy's authority, war powers, the legality of the invasion, and the weight of an army used for genocide.

Solo-actionable version: for any shutdown, cancellation or reorganisation you sign, write on the same page who is dismissed, what they are owed, when it reaches them, and what happens if the mechanism is not ready.

Field exercise

The next time a team, contract, service or system is retired in your organisation, find the approval and ask one question: on what date does everyone displaced by this receive what they are owed, and does that machinery exist today? Write the answer beside the decision. Pass when the date is earlier than the shutdown date, or later and accepted in writing by the person signing. Review on the shutdown date. Classification: decision, if the risk was named and the mechanism deferred; execution, if the mechanism existed and failed; uncertain, if nobody wrote either down.

Before somebody has to be brave, where is the last reversible decision?

This chapter teaches the reader to look for a payment mechanism on the same page as a dismissal. Bay of Pigs asks whether phase two had an owner; 737 MAX asks what was in the building that was not in the room; this chapter asks what a decision-maker does with a risk he has already written down.
