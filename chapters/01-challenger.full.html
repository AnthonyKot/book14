Case 1 · full chapter with receipts

The temperature no shuttle had flown

Challenger · 27 January 1986 · Decision-forcing case

At 10:30 at night, the rocket engineers in Utah mute the telephone line.

On the other end are NASA managers in Alabama and Florida. The launch has already been delayed by weather. It is now scheduled for 9:38 the following morning. Florida is heading for a hard freeze.

The people in this room have given NASA an answer: do not launch below 53°F. Nobody on the call has launched this joint in tomorrow’s predicted cold. NASA has challenged the answer. The engineers’ chart does not prove a temperature at which the joint fails. It shows damage on a previous cold flight, several other damaged seals at warmer temperatures, and too few points to draw the clean line management wants.

The line is muted. The decision has come back into the room.

Role card

  • You are: Bob Lund, vice-president for engineering at Morton Thiokol’s Wasatch plant.
  • Objective: Give NASA the contractor’s launch recommendation for the solid rocket boosters.
  • Clock: Roughly eleven hours to the scheduled launch.
  • Primary resource: Schedule time spent to preserve safety margin and gain information.
  • Authority: You can maintain or change Thiokol’s recommendation. NASA holds final launch authority.
  • Constraint: The evidence is sparse, the proposed threshold is being challenged, and a delay may be much longer than one day if the cold persists.

This is not yet the story of a shuttle disaster. Nothing has failed. Seven astronauts are asleep or preparing for the morning. Your problem is smaller and harder: what does an absence of data permit you to recommend?

What is on the table

Known

  • The forecast low at Kennedy Space Center is about 22°F; the forecast air temperature near the planned launch is about 26°F. The precise O-ring temperature is not available.
  • The booster segments are sealed at their field joints by O-rings. NASA has classified an O-ring failure as Criticality 1: a single failure point capable of causing loss of the vehicle and crew.
  • O-ring erosion or soot has appeared on earlier flights. The coldest previous shuttle launch, mission 51-C, was about 53°F at the relevant comparison point and showed blow-by.
  • All 24 previous shuttle launches nevertheless completed their missions.
  • Thiokol engineers believe cold makes the rubber slower to recover its shape while the metal joint moves at ignition. They have recommended not launching outside the flight-experience boundary of 53°F.
  • NASA managers argue that the presented data do not establish 53°F as a physical failure threshold. The official launch criteria contain no such temperature limit.
  • Before the line was muted, Thiokol’s program manager said he could not recommend launch on the engineering assessment presented.

Reasonably inferable

  • Tomorrow’s conditions are not merely another observation inside the existing flight data. They extend well beyond its cold edge.
  • A slower seal response could lengthen the period in which hot gas passes the primary seal.
  • The secondary O-ring may provide protection, but its ability to move into sealing position is part of the same joint behaviour being disputed.

Possible

  • Both seals could fail to contain combustion gas during ignition.
  • The joint could behave acceptably, as it has on every completed mission so far.
  • A delay could produce warmer conditions, more analysis, a changed launch criterion—or only the same argument on another night.

Unknown

  • The joint and O-ring temperature at ignition.
  • A quantitative relationship between temperature and probability of seal failure.
  • A temperature below which failure becomes inevitable.
  • Whether one more day is enough to change either the weather or the evidence.

Unknowable from the surviving record

  • The private balance of fear, customer pressure, professional confidence and self-doubt in each participant’s mind.
  • Whether any individual privately expected catastrophe. Concern is documented; prediction of this outcome is not.

The decision

You have three defensible-looking choices.

A. Maintain the no-launch recommendation below 53°F

This keeps the vehicle inside the coldest relevant flight experience. It also turns one damaged flight into a threshold the data cannot precisely justify. The launch may be delayed for days or longer, and NASA may conclude that the contractor is replacing analysis with fear.

B. Recommend launch with the uncertainty recorded

The proposed limit is not an established qualification boundary, no previous mission has been lost to the joint, and the secondary seal is intended to provide redundancy. This allows NASA to accept the residual risk. It also converts “failure has not been quantified” into permission to expose a crew to a condition never flown.

C. Refuse both answers and escalate the unresolved constraint

Thiokol can state that it lacks a supportable launch recommendation and require the complete anomaly history, joint experts and final program authorities to review the issue. That avoids pretending 53°F is proven. It may also be interpreted as a no-launch answer without the discipline of proposing a technically grounded return-to-flight condition.

What do you do? What single fact would make you change your answer?

Stop here long enough to answer. “Ask for more data” is incomplete unless you can name the data and explain how it could arrive before commitment.

What happened

The off-line caucus lasted about half an hour, not the requested five minutes. According to Roger Boisjoly’s later Commission testimony, a senior executive asked Lund to take off his “engineering hat” and put on his “management hat.” Lund changed his position. At about 11 p.m. the teleconference resumed, and Thiokol recommended launch. Its written rationale said temperature remained a concern but the evidence was inconclusive. Engineers in the room continued to oppose the recommendation.

The launch did not occur at 9:38. A hatch problem and wind produced further delay. At 11:38 a.m. on 28 January, Challenger lifted off.

The Presidential Commission later concluded that seals in the lower field joint of the right solid rocket motor failed. Hot gas escaped the joint and eventually impinged on the external tank. The vehicle broke apart 73 seconds after launch. All seven crew members— Francis Scobee, Michael Smith, Judith Resnik, Ellison Onizuka, Ronald McNair, Gregory Jarvis and Christa McAuliffe—died.

That outcome tells us what the joint did on this flight. It does not, by itself, tell us what the recommendation should have been at 10:30 the night before. For that we have to return to the evidence horizon.

The people who inherited the decision

Many chapters in this book turn here toward improvisation: the sailors loading boats, the operators trying to read a flooded tower, the brigade asking for ammunition. This case does not.

Once the solid rocket boosters ignited, the crew could not shut them down. The Commission found no other shuttle element caused the joint failure. No cockpit diagnosis, checklist or display could restore the seals. The astronauts inherited the risk without inheriting a decision capable of controlling it.

That absence matters. “The people below will handle it” can be a real mitigation when they have time, information, authority and a rehearsed recovery. Here, the crew had none of the four. Calling them highly trained did not turn an irreversible hardware failure into an operator problem.

The remaining downstream decisions belonged to people on the ground. Allan McDonald, Thiokol’s senior representative at Kennedy, did not agree with the revised recommendation and refused to sign it. Engineers including Boisjoly later gave evidence about the decision process. Their testimony helped establish what the final recommendation had hidden: the contractor’s formal answer had changed, while the engineering opposition had not.

That was consequential courage. It could explain the decision after the loss. It could not rescue the crew from it.

Where did failure become likely?

The visible answer is the management caucus. It is not the whole answer.

By January 1986, the field joint had already produced erosion and blow-by. A launch constraint had been imposed after erosion of a secondary O-ring on mission 51-B and then waived for successive flights. The Commission found that the constraint, the reason for it and the sequence of waivers had not reached key Level I and Level II managers during the flight-readiness review for 51-L. The certification papers did not surface the O-ring problem. The night teleconference was therefore trying to create, in a few hours, a decision boundary that the program’s ordinary readiness process had failed to maintain.

That gives us three different evaluations.

  • Decision quality: Recommending launch was weak because the proposal moved far beyond the coldest relevant flight experience in a Criticality 1 component, while the engineers closest to the joint did not accept the recommendation.
  • Execution quality: The teleconference did not communicate the continuing engineering opposition or the anomaly history to all authorities who would normally own such a risk. The formal recommendation concealed a live disagreement.
  • Outcome quality: Catastrophic. But the judgement above does not require the outcome; the same recommendation would remain weak if the seals happened to work.

The earliest reversible and consequential decision was not necessarily that night. Joint redesign, launch-constraint governance and transparent anomaly reporting offered earlier exits. But the caucus was the final moment when a person with relevant authority could still prevent this hardware from flying in these conditions. Earlier explains more. Final makes the choice playable. The two should not be confused.

Competing diagnoses

Diagnosis 1: Management overrode engineering

This is strongly supported. The Commission concluded that Thiokol management reversed its position at Marshall’s urging and contrary to its engineers’ views. The change converted an unresolved safety objection into a contractor recommendation to fly.

Diagnosis 2: Engineering offered an unsound rule

This also deserves space. The 53°F threshold was not derived from a demonstrated failure curve. The evidence package was hurried and difficult to interpret. NASA managers were right to ask why the coldest prior launch should automatically become a new limit. A responsible decision system must permit technical recommendations to be challenged.

That does not make launch the default. A weakly justified stop rule and a justified go rule are not opposites. Both can be absent. When a Criticality 1 component moves beyond experience, inability to prove the proposed threshold does not prove the proposal safe.

Diagnosis 3: The teleconference was already too late

This may explain the organization better than either answer above. Repeated anomalies, waivers and incomplete reporting allowed a design problem to arrive on launch eve as an improvised weather exception. Better behaviour inside the caucus could stop this flight; only earlier system decisions could prevent the next caucus.

Counterfactual: buy one day

Assume Thiokol maintains its no-launch recommendation and the mission is delayed for one day. Do not assume a redesign. Do not assume a perfect analysis. Do not even assume warmer weather.

If the temperature and evidence remain unchanged, the technical problem does not change at all. The organization has purchased time but not used it. A delay is not a safety control merely because nothing moves.

The problem changes in kind only if the time buys one of three things:

  1. conditions return inside an accepted experience boundary;
  2. testing or analysis establishes a defensible new boundary;
  3. the unresolved constraint reaches the authority empowered to accept, reject or suspend it with the complete disagreement visible.

This counterfactual sharpens the judgement. “Delay” is not the lesson. Spend schedule to restore evidence, margin or ownership. Otherwise the same decision is waiting tomorrow.

Judgement

Best-supported diagnosis: a decision and communication failure, with high confidence.

The recommendation to launch treated absence of a quantified failure threshold as if it were evidence that the proposed conditions were acceptable. The risk concerned an irreversible, Criticality 1 component outside relevant flight experience. Continuing engineering opposition did not travel with the formal answer to the people who owned the launch.

The record does not establish that every manager expected failure or chose schedule over known loss. It establishes something more ordinary and more transferable: the organization changed which side had to prove its case. Engineers were asked to prove the flight unsafe in conditions for which the program lacked flight evidence.

Where this stops being true

No complex system can prove absolute safety. If “outside experience” always means stop, nothing genuinely new can operate. Sparse evidence is not a permanent veto.

Proceeding can be reasonable when:

  • the departure from experience is bounded and physically understood;
  • independent protections do not share the same uncertainty;
  • the consequence is recoverable;
  • operators can recognize and control the failure;
  • residual risk is stated plainly and accepted by the correct authority;
  • the action is designed to produce the evidence needed for the next step.

None of those conditions should be assumed merely because skilled people are present.

What transfers

When a proposal sits beyond the tested envelope, write this before debating probability:

We lack evidence here because ______. The consequence if our assumption is wrong is ______. The protection that does not depend on the same assumption is ______.

If the third blank stays empty and the consequence is irreversible, uncertainty weighs against commitment. This is not because pessimism is wiser. It is because the proposed action is the experiment, and somebody else may be inside it.

What does not transfer

A project meeting is not a shuttle launch. Most workplace decisions are reversible, their failure modes are observable, and delay itself carries cost or danger. Do not imitate a no-launch rule where a small controlled trial would safely create evidence.

Solo-actionable version

Label the kind of uncertainty:

  • inside experience: variation around conditions already observed;
  • outside experience: a condition, scale or interaction not represented in the evidence.

Do not let the second appear in a meeting as merely “more risk” of the first.

Field exercise

Over your next three consequential approvals:

  1. Mark the first moment somebody proposes operating outside observed experience.
  2. Record the missing evidence in one sentence.
  3. Record the consequence if the assumption is wrong.
  4. Name an independent protection or write none.
  5. Classify the final result as decision, execution or uncertain—without using the eventual outcome to change the original evidence ledger.

Pass condition: all three approvals are recorded, and every outside-experience proposal has either an independent protection or an explicit escalation owner.

Review condition: if the proposal succeeded, ask whether success supplied new evidence or merely another uneventful exposure. Survival is data, but it may be weak data about the next boundary.
