Case 7 · Scott and Amundsen · London and Norway, 1909–1910

What lies beneath the last stage

Everyone knows the ending: Amundsen reached the Pole first and came home; Scott arrived five weeks later, found the Norwegian tent, and died with his companions eleven miles from a depot. The story has been told twice — first as heroism, then as bungling — and both versions skip the part where the decision was actually made: not on the ice in 1912, but in offices and shipyards in 1909, when nobody was cold and every purchase was still reversible. This piece is about that decision, why it was not obvious, and what to do with it — which is not to pick the winner.

Where they were standing

London, 1909–1910. Robert Falcon Scott is assembling a British national expedition with two public promises: a large scientific programme and the South Pole. The base will be at McMurdo Sound, which serves the science and gives a known route up the Beardmore Glacier. Money is tight. His people are mostly naval and scientific; man-hauling — men in harness, pulling sledges — is what they know. Ernest Shackleton has just come back from within a hundred miles of the Pole using ponies across the Barrier and men beyond, and survived. Scott's transport plan is a portfolio: three experimental motor sledges, ponies, dogs, and human hauling for the last stage.

Norway, September 1909. A telegram: the North Pole has been claimed. Roald Amundsen has spent years raising money for an Arctic drift; his expedition has just lost its point. He still has the ship, the debts, and a crew picked for polar travel. He decides to redirect south — and tells almost nobody. His objective can be narrower than Scott's: no national science programme, no coastal laboratories. He and his men know skis and dogs; he can recruit around that one system. His base and his route are untested.

For both men the clock is months, not days: the ships must sail in 1910. The resource being spent is preparation time, to buy transport margin. And the constraint that governs everything: competence you do not have at departure cannot be bought on the ice.

Why it was not obvious

Here is what makes this a real decision and not a morality tale. Almost every consideration cut both ways.

Diversity looked like safety — and it was, against one kind of failure. Motors, ponies, dogs and men: if one component fails, the expedition need not end. That is real. What a portfolio does not protect against is accumulation — every early shortfall lands on the next stage, and Scott's last stage was men, hauling on the plateau, farthest from help, latest in the season.

Specialisation looked like mastery — and it concentrated risk. Amundsen's dogs-and-skis system was coherent and rehearsed. It also rested on a base built on Barrier ice that many contemporaries thought might be afloat, an unscouted route through the mountains, and a start date driven partly by anxiety about Scott. Had the base or the route failed, coherence would not have saved him.

Scott's mixed system had precedent — Shackleton had come back. Ponies were not irrational on the information available. Motors were a legitimate experiment that promised depot-hauling power without fodder. Nobody had proved either in Antarctica; nobody had disproved them either.

"Use dogs" was not a thing Scott could simply buy. Dogs need drivers. British skill with dogs and skis was uneven, and whether a full cadre of expert dog drivers could have been hired in Britain in 1909 is unresolved to this day. A dog system run badly is not safer than a mixed system run well.

Scott carried promises Amundsen did not. A science programme, a fixed coastal base, subscribers, the public. Amundsen could redirect a whole expedition by telegram and secrecy. Scott's design was bundled with obligations that were not his alone to drop.

Neither man knew the other's final staffing, doctrine or depot geometry. Neither knew the weather of February and March 1912. That is the point: two intelligent designs, each with a hole the other did not have.

The three doors

Strip it down. One question was on each man's desk in 1909: what carries the last stage, and what lies beneath it if the earlier stages under-deliver?

ASpecialise.

Build the whole expedition around one system — dogs and skis. Recruit operators as carefully as navigators; rehearse the complete system; design depots, route, season and animal attrition around it. (This is the door Amundsen took.)

You get: mastery and coherence; every stage speaks the same language. You risk: concentration — if the route defeats dogs, the base fails, or you cannot hire the operators, there is no second system underneath.

BStage several modes.

Motors and animals across the Barrier, then men for the glacier and the plateau. A single failure need not cancel the expedition. (This is the door Scott took.)

You get: resilience against any one component breaking. You risk: cumulative shortfall — every stage that under-delivers passes the gap forward, and the final absorber is people, with nothing beneath them. Diversity of equipment is not diversity of competence.

CChange the objective.

Keep the science, but narrow the polar attempt: change the base, reduce parallel commitments, or refuse to proceed south without a larger depot margin than the transport can guarantee.

You get: slack. You risk: surrendering part of the promise made to subscribers and the public — and, for a national expedition, that is not a small thing.

Do not choose. Read it again.

Most decision exercises ask you to pick. This one asks you not to — not yet. And it asks you to resist the easy move, which is to grade the doors by who reached the Pole first.

Instead

Go back through the three doors and imagine not the outward march but the return — the last three hundred miles home, in February and March, with the season closing. Ask, for each door:

  • Who is pulling the sledge on the plateau, and what is beneath them if they are slower, weaker or fewer than the plan assumed?
  • Where did the depots end up — not where the plan put them, but where tired animals and a cautious leader actually left them — and who pays for the difference?
  • If the motors fail, the ponies weaken, and the dogs turn back early, which stage absorbs each of those gaps? Is it always the same one?
  • Under Door A, what happens if the base is on floating ice, or the route through the mountains does not exist? What is beneath that?
  • Under Door C, what would you have had to give up in 1909, publicly, to buy the margin — and would anyone have thanked you?

Notice that Door B is not the choice of an amateur. It has precedent, it survives single failures, and it fits the men and money Scott had. Notice that Door A is not proof of genius; it nearly failed twice before it succeeded. Notice that the difference between them is invisible on the outward march and decisive on the way home.

That is the shape this book is trying to teach you to see: in a staged plan, the question is not whether each stage is good but what absorbs each stage's shortfall — and what lies beneath the final absorber. "The team will make it up" is not a reserve. It is a risk concentration with a name.

What happened

Amundsen put his base at the Bay of Whales, on Barrier ice, and laid roughly three tons of supplies at three southern depots, marking their approaches with rows of numbered bamboo flags. On 8 September 1911 he started too early — his own account says uncertainty about Scott made him determined to leave as soon as possible, despite the danger. Temperatures fell to between −58° and −75°F; the party retreated with dogs lost and men frostbitten, and the expedition split in a quarrel his book does not explain. Five men left again on 20 October with fifty-two dogs, crossed an unscouted glacier, killed twenty-four dogs at the planned point, reached the Pole on 14 December, and were home on 25 January with eleven dogs.

Scott lost one motor through the sea ice while unloading; the others ran "well, but not very well." On the depot journey the ponies weakened, and One Ton Depot was laid about thirty miles short of where it was planned — Scott judged that forcing the animals farther would have been "ridiculous." Oates argued for pushing on; Scott refused. The southern march went by stages: motors, ponies and dogs stopped or turned back; men continued. On 3 January 1912 Scott enlarged the final party from four to five; the fifth man had no skis. They reached the Pole on 17 January and found the Norwegian tent. On the return Edgar Evans deteriorated and died below the glacier. Oates, his feet destroyed, walked out of the tent. Scott, Wilson and Bowers died in March, about eleven miles from One Ton Depot, short of food and critically short of fuel.

The people in the last stage still decided. On 17 February, after Evans repeatedly fell behind, the other four turned back for him and tried to carry him on. In one of the returning support parties, Lieutenant Evans collapsed with scurvy; Lashly stayed to nurse him while Crean walked more than thirty-five miles alone for help — an arithmetic problem turned into a rescue by two men who had not designed the arithmetic. None of them chose the architecture. Each spent the margin it left them.

The result tells you what the combined system met. It does not, by itself, tell you which 1909 design was reasonable — Amundsen's success is one trial from a different latitude, route and season, and his September start shows a coherent system can be endangered by one optimistic decision just as easily as a diverse one.

The thing to take with you

You will never haul a sledge across the Barrier. But you run plans with dependent stages, where one stage is said to protect the next — a rollout, a project, a supply chain, a handover. The question this case leaves you with belongs in the meeting where somebody says "if that slips, the next phase will absorb it":

When this stage under-delivers, who or what absorbs the shortfall — and what lies beneath the final absorber?

If the honest answer for the last stage is "the people will work harder," you are standing where Scott stood in 1909 — with a defensible plan, a precedent, and nothing beneath the men.
