Case 6 · Triangle Waist Company · New York, 1910–1911

The door that opened for the manager

Everyone knows the ending: a fire in a shirtwaist factory, a hundred and forty-six dead, "the owners locked the doors." Almost nobody knows that the owners were tried for it and acquitted, or that the arrangement they ran was legal, had passed three state inspections, and had cost nothing for nine years. This piece is about the decision underneath the slogan — a decision that was not obviously wrong on the day it was made, and was made again every evening at the quitting bell.

Where he was standing

Isaac Harris runs the shop floor of the Triangle Waist Company; his partner Max Blanck runs the business. They lease three lofts — the eighth, ninth and tenth floors of the Asch Building off Washington Square — and employ about five hundred people, most of them young women; some three hundred sit at sewing machines on the ninth.

Each floor has two stair doors. The Greene Street door is beside the freight elevators; on the ninth it sits behind a partition. The Washington Place door is at the opposite corner, by the passenger elevators. Both open inward — an outward door would block the stair. Only the Greene Street stair goes up to the roof. An iron fire escape hangs in the back court.

The firm's rule is old and simple: at closing, operators leave by Greene Street, one at a time, past a watchman who looks into their handbags. The Washington Place door on the ninth is kept closed with a key in the lock, tied to the knob by a string. The manager crosses that stair ten times a day without a key; foreladies sometimes use it; in summer it stands open. The operators say they never saw it used and always went out past the watchman. Nobody — on either side — says it was part of the nightly exit.

The reason for the rule is not invented. Theft is real: in the summer of 1910 a presser walks toward the Greene Street door with two shirtwaists folded into the pad of false hair under her own; a thread shows; the girl behind her pulls it. Harris has tried prosecuting — six girls arrested three years earlier, then lawsuits for damages — and concluded that "the best way is to discharge them and not be bothered with them any more." She is discharged. The line keeps moving: open the pocketbook, show the watchman, go down.

Why it was not obvious

Here is what makes this a real decision and not a villain's choice. Every consideration cut both ways.

Theft was real — but small. Goods walked out. What Harris had actually caught in the past year, in his own words, was worth "ten dollars or fifteen dollars." What walked out uncaught he could not say. A supervised single exit is a lawful, ordinary loss control; it is also a rule that teaches three hundred people one door.

Fires had happened — and gone out. Five on the premises: two in 1902 with insurance claims, three since, put out with pails of water. Cuttings piled under the cutting tables between rag collections; the dealer had taken away more than a ton in mid-January. Nobody had ever needed the second door in an emergency.

The law was met — and said nothing about this. Section 80 of the Labor Law said factory doors "shall not be locked, bolted or fastened during working hours." State inspectors called in January 1910, twice, and again on 27 February 1911; each card marks the doors unlocked, and two mark outward-opening doors "not practical." No law required a second exit in daily use.

The second door was passable — for some people. A door the manager uses ten times a day is not a wall. But a door kept closed with a key on a string is an exit only for whoever finds and turns the key. The manager did. Some foreladies did. The operators, on their evidence, never had.

Nobody had complained. Asked at trial whether employees had ever objected to not being allowed down the Washington Place way, the manager said no. The record contains no warning to the owners about that door.

So the arrangement was legal, inspected, cheap, and had never mattered. That is exactly the kind of decision this book is about — the ones that look like nothing until they are everything.

The three doors

Strip it down. One thing was on Harris's desk, every evening: what is the second door for at closing time?

AKeep the bag search; make the second door a real exit.

At closing, someone on the floor opens the Washington Place door and stands by it — as the firm already does in summer. The way out no longer depends on a key being found. A stronger version — hardware that opens from inside without a key — might need the landlord.

You get: an exit that exists whether or not anyone finds the key. You risk: a second, unwatched way out of a loft you have been searching for years.

BGive up the single-exit search on the ninth floor.

Accept an unmeasured loss, or control it some other way. Two doors in daily use, so people learn them both.

You get: three hundred people who know two ways out. You risk: theft you cannot measure, and a rule your partner regards as normal thrown away for a fire that has never come.

CKeep things as they are.

One supervised exit; second door closed, key on its string. Three inspection cards say unlocked. It costs nothing. The last three fires went out with pails.

You get: the loss control you have, at no cost. You risk: the thing you cannot see, because it has never happened.

Do not choose. Read it again.

Most decision exercises ask you to pick. This one asks you not to — not yet.

Instead

Go back through the three doors, and this time do not imagine a fire. Imagine an ordinary evening — and then one thing going wrong on the floor below. Ask, for each door:

  • Which way do three hundred people try first, because it is the way they have gone every night for years?
  • When some of them reach the second door, what does it need in order to open — and is that thing there? A key on a string, in smoke, for someone who has never turned it?
  • Who on that floor has ever opened it? The manager. Some foreladies. Is any of them standing next to it at 4:45 on a Saturday?
  • What is the second door worth on the evening the first one is not enough — and what did keeping it closed actually buy, in dollars, this year?
  • Whose margin is being spent by the rule, and who set the rule?

Notice that Door C is not the choice of a monster. It is the choice most proprietors made. Notice that Door A costs almost nothing and asks only one question — what is the second door for? Notice that the difference between them is invisible on every evening but one.

That is the shape this book is trying to teach you to see: a control installed for the owner's protection that, on the one night it matters, has to open for the people on the other side of it — and was never designed to.

What happened

At the quitting bell on Saturday, 25 March 1911, rags caught fire on the eighth floor by the Greene Street cutting tables and went into the cuttings underneath. Cutters threw pails of water. The first street alarm reached the fire houses at 4:45.

The eighth floor's warning instrument ran to the tenth floor only. The timekeeper wrote, got nothing back, and telephoned the tenth: "Tell Mr. Blanck about it." Nobody called the ninth. The ninth floor learned by a scream, by smoke, or by flame at the windows.

On the ninth, roughly a dozen witnesses later swore they tried the Washington Place door and could not open it. Two defence witnesses swore they opened it, saw flame and smoke rising from the eighth, and turned back. The passenger elevators kept coming for a time, then stopped; people slid down the cables and dropped into the shaft. A life net caught jumpers "until the net broke."

A hundred and forty-six people died. Harris and Blanck were tried for the manslaughter of one of them, Margaret Schwartz, a ninth-floor operator last seen at the Washington Place door, on the theory that a door locked in working hours was a crime and her death followed from it. A lock with its bolt shot was found on the Washington Place side sixteen days later; locksmiths disagreed whether heat could shoot a bolt. The jury, told it must find the door locked, the owners aware of it, and the lock the cause of her death, acquitted in under two hours.

The result tells you what happened when an eighth-floor fire met that ninth-floor arrangement. It does not tell you the door was locked; that was litigated and is still disputed. Nor does the acquittal make the arrangement reasonable; the jury answered a narrower question. What the record does show, on the owners' own best evidence, is a door closed with the key in it — an exit for whoever could find and turn a key on a string in smoke, as the manager could and the operators never had.

The people on the ninth floor still decided. Mary Bucelli tried the door, saw no key, climbed on a table, broke a window and went out on the fire escape. Ida Nelson tried the door, found the fire escape thick with flame, crossed the loft and climbed to the roof. Lillian Weiner tried the door twice, went to a window to jump, saw the flames below, went back to the door, then knocked at the elevator until it came and "fell into the elevator, and a whole lot of more girls fell in on top of me." May Levantini turned the key on its string, opened the door, saw the eighth floor coming up through smoke and flame, and slid down an elevator cable instead. None of them set the exit rule. Each spent the margin it left them.

The thing to take with you

You own controls. A lock, a permission, a single sign-off, a gate in a process, a door only you have the key to. Most of them protect you, and most of them are reasonable. The question this case leaves you with is about the ones that stand on someone else's last way out:

If the person on the other side needed this to open now — without you, and without your key — would it? And is there another way out?

If the honest answer is "someone will open it," you are standing where Harris stood at every quitting bell. Try the release without you. If it does not open, that is the decision.
