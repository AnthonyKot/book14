# Colonel and above

*Coalition Provisional Authority · Baghdad and Washington · May 2003 · Decision-forcing case*

On 19 May 2003 the Administrator of the Coalition Provisional Authority sends a two-page memorandum to the Secretary of Defense. Subject: *Dissolution of the Ministry of Defense and Related Entities*. "In the coming days I propose to issue the attached order (Tab A)," it begins, "carrying forward the de-Ba'athification effort by dissolving Saddam's key security ministries." Tab A is a draft order abolishing the Ministry of Defence, the intelligence services, the armed forces and the regime's paramilitaries. Tab B is an outline of who will be paid afterwards, and who will not. <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 1 / l. 7–11; p. 3–6 / l. 99–260 -->

The memorandum has a paragraph the reader should hold on to. "There is obviously something that catches in the craw about paying anything to people who worked at the heart of Saddam's regime. Against this, however, we must balance the risks of serious discontent, increased terrorism, and much higher crime rates that may result if we cut off all military and security sector pensioners in a heavily militarized society." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 2 / l. 57–61; OCR — verify against page image -->

It has another. "There will be some delay in making these payments, because, in contrast to the situation for most civilian agencies, we do not have employee rosters, or contacts with administrative officials of the dissolved entities." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 2 / l. 67–69 -->

Nothing has been signed. The order is a draft, the payment policy is an outline, and the man who wrote both has been in the country for a week.

## The hand you are dealt

**You are L. Paul Bremer III**, Presidential Envoy to Iraq and Administrator of the Coalition Provisional Authority. You landed in Baghdad on 12 May, less than two weeks after the President appointed you. You are sixty-two, a career Foreign Service officer with a later career in the private sector; you have never served in the Middle East, have never run a post-conflict operation, and do not speak Arabic. Jay Garner's Office of Reconstruction and Humanitarian Assistance, which arrived in the Republican Palace a month before you, is being folded into your authority. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 90 / l. 3364–3376 -->

The city you arrived in was being looted. There were about four thousand police on duty in Baghdad, a city of seven million. Most of the ministries had been burned, including Finance. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 147 / l. 5771–5773; Hearing p. 185 / l. 3710–3714 (Bremer's own retrospective description) -->

On 16 May, four days after you arrived, you issued Order No. 1, de-Ba'athification, banning the top four ranks of the party from public employment and dismissing full party members from the top three layers of every government institution. It was conceived in Washington. The same day, in a meeting with Iraqi political leaders and the British special representative John Sawers, some of the Iraqis argued that de-Ba'athification should be widened to the security services and the army; you promised a further proclamation on both in the days ahead. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 94 / l. 3542–3556; Chilcot p. 32 / l. 1795–1799 -->

Your senior adviser for national security and defence is Walter Slocombe, formerly Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. He drafted the order at Tab A, in consultation with the Office of the Secretary of Defense; it has been circulated to senior military leaders, including the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs. On 9 May, before either of you left Washington, you and Slocombe briefed Under Secretary Feith on what he would later call "their plan to dissolve the Iraqi Army." On 12 May, your first day, you raised "dissolving the MOD and the security and intelligence organisations" and "establish[ing] a new national army" with Sawers; he raised no concerns and reported home that you had made a "good dynamic start." On 13 May Slocombe put the same plan to the British Defence Secretary in London, emphasising "a new Army, rather than a reconstituted version of the old"; nothing in the British record shows an objection. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3660–3663; Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1919–1921, 1940–1943, 1945–1986 -->

- **Objective:** show the Kurdish and Shi'a majority — the populations the old army was used against, and the ones cooperating with the occupation — that Saddam's instruments of repression are not coming back; and begin a force that can, in time, defend the country. Your memorandum puts it as demonstrating "that neither Saddam nor his gang is coming back." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 1 / l. 20–22; Hearing p. 186 / l. 3745–3756 (retrospective) -->
- **Clock:** days. The memorandum asks the Secretary to concur "in principle"; you have told the Iraqi leaders a proclamation is coming. On the record as it stands, the signature is four days out. <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 2 / l. 64–65 -->
- **Primary resource:** political credibility with the majority, spent to buy a clean break — against whatever security the old institutions might still supply, and against the income of the households they employed.
- **Authority:** as Administrator you promulgate binding orders in occupied Iraq, under Resolution 1483 and the laws of war. You do not set troop levels; you do not control how quickly a payroll can be built for people whose rosters you do not have. <!-- CHECK: Order2 casi p. 1 / l. 5–7 -->
- **Constraint:** the ministry buildings and barracks have been looted; the men have gone home; the majority's leaders have told the Coalition — on the CPA's account — that they will not accept a reconstituted version of Saddam's army. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3668–3671; Chilcot p. 33 / l. 1868–1879 -->

## Evidence ledger

**Known — in front of you.** Your own memorandum, and what it says. That the order "will affect large numbers of people: There were some 400,000 employees of the MOD alone." That the payment policy at Tab B continues pensions being paid before 16 April, provides a one-time termination payment to dismissed employees, and pays neither to "disqualified persons" — those barred by de-Ba'athification, and, by presumption, "all military officers with the rank of Colonel and above… unless they can establish otherwise." That there are no rosters and no payment mechanism, so payments will be delayed, and that the intelligence community is "starting to assemble the necessary data." That your recommendation to pay most pensions rests on the danger of not paying them, in your words, in "a heavily militarized society." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 1–2 / l. 33–36, 38–43, 50–55, 57–73; Tab B p. 6 / l. 224–240 -->

Tab B also says this, about the men you are dismissing: they may be hired "as individuals by Coalition military or civil authorities, for example as security guards. They are *not* hired as reconstituted old units, as soldiers, or as part of the future Iraqi military." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 6 / l. 254–257 -->

The New Iraqi Corps the order announces will start small. The plan you and Slocombe put forward for it is about 40,000 men in three light infantry divisions, the first ready in the autumn of 2004, the last in early 2006. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3682–3686; horizon — SIGIR presents this as proposed "to replace the disbanded forces"; confirm the size and timeline were in hand by 19–23 May or move this out of the ledger -->

**Known — in the building, not in the room.** The land-force plan for the occupation, ECLIPSE II, assumed that the bulk of the Iraqi Army would be recalled to duty and used in Phase IV. CENTCOM's plan, approved by the President in March, assumed the same; before the invasion the Coalition dropped leaflets telling Iraqi soldiers not to fight and promising no reprisals to those who laid down their arms. Around the time of Franks' Freedom Message in mid-April, the land-force staff opened negotiations with senior Iraqi officers about the army's part in restoring order. In early May Garner began working to bring old army units back and arranged for the US Government to pay the salaries of 300,000 soldiers. Across the country, brigade commanders had contacted Iraqi military leaders and, through them, had begun to reconstitute units. <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 93 / l. 4416; p. 113 / l. 5407–5445; SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3672–3681 -->

The objections were also in the building. When Slocombe met the land-force commander, Lieutenant General McKiernan, and his deputy, Major General Webster — at a meeting McKiernan had called with Iraqi generals who had volunteered to serve — Webster briefed him on their plans for a new ministry of defence and recalls the answer as "No, you are not doing that." McKiernan and Webster argued that a new force needed a trained senior cadre; Slocombe answered that the Coalition would grow leadership from the junior officers who survived de-Ba'athification. Webster: "We were surprised, shocked… That was a terrible night." SIGIR records that "key U.S. generals in Iraq expressed concerns about the order before it was issued." Major General Tim Cross, Garner's British deputy, told the Iraq Inquiry the decision was taken "against all advice from Garner and myself." The record establishes that these objections reached your senior adviser and your organisation. It does not establish what reached your desk, or when. <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 114–115 / l. 5496–5518; SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3672; Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1928–1930 -->

**Reasonably inferable.** For signing: the army did go home; the barracks were stripped; a force with, on Slocombe's later figure, eleven thousand general officers is not a force to preserve; the two communities that make up most of the country and are cooperating with you have said, through their leaders, what they will not accept. Against: your own memorandum names the risk of cutting off "military and security sector pensioners" and then attaches a policy that cuts off every officer of colonel rank and above; and the payment machinery you say is necessary does not exist. <!-- CHECK: Chilcot p. 33 / l. 1868–1879; Bremer p. 2 / l. 50–52, 57–61 -->

**Possible.** That the recalled units would have fractured or refused; that a Kurdish and Shi'a reaction to a reconstituted army would have cost more than the dismissal; that the men would have waited for their money.

**Unknown.** How many of the 400,000 can be found and paid without rosters, and how long that takes. What the officers you have presumed disqualified will do while the rebuttal procedure "to be prescribed" is written. Whether the reconstitution the brigades have begun is a few units or many.

**Unknowable.** What Washington would have said if you had asked to wait. Nobody did.

## The decision

Three courses are within your authority this week.

**A. Sign as drafted.** One order, one date, seven ministries and every armed formation of the regime gone in a stroke; ranks cancelled; conscripts released; a New Iraqi Corps announced. Termination payments and pensions to follow, once the data exist, except to senior party members and everyone from colonel up. Gain: the clean break, and the majority's confidence. Exposed risk: the one you have written down.

**B. Defer, and let the recall run.** The land-force negotiations and the brigade-level reconstitution are under way and Garner's payroll is arranged. Let them continue; vet unit by unit; dissolve what should be dissolved from a position where you know who the men are and where. Gain: an existing instrument, and a payroll with names on it. Exposed risk: the Coalition is seen to restore Saddam's army; the majority's leaders react; the vetting never catches up.

**C. Narrow the order.** Dissolve now what nobody proposes keeping — the intelligence services, the Special Security Organization, the Republican Guard and Special Republican Guard, the Fedayeen and the party militia. Hold the regular army, the Ministry of Defence and the officer pensions for a separate decision, on a stated date, once the rosters exist. Gain: most of the political signal at a fraction of the human bill. Exposed risk: the line between the regular army and the regime's instruments is contested, and drawing it takes time you have told the Iraqi leaders you do not intend to take.

Commit. Then say what would change your answer. If the answer is "nothing, because the army no longer exists," ask why Tab B needed a sentence forbidding its reconstitution.

## What happened

You signed on 23 May. Order No. 2, *Dissolution of Entities*: seven ministries and agencies, every regular and irregular armed force of the regime, the Revolutionary Command Council and the National Assembly, dissolved; every rank cancelled; every employee dismissed with effect from 16 April. Section 3(4): a termination payment. Section 3(5): pensions continue, "including to war widows and disabled veterans." Section 3(6): no payment of either kind to any Senior Party Member, and "any person holding the rank under the former regime of Colonel or above, or its equivalent, will be deemed a Senior Party Member," subject to a rebuttal procedure to be prescribed. Section 5: a New Iraqi Corps "in the near future." <!-- CHECK: Order2 casi p. 1–3 / l. 20–104; Annex p. 4–5 / l. 106–185 -->

Secretary Powell first learned of the order at a principals' meeting on 22 May, when you announced by video link that you would issue it the next day. "There was no meeting on it; there was no, 'Gee, is this a good idea?' You couldn't even tell who had decided it." He asked the Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs whether the military had known: "Hell, no!" The NSC's Iraq coordinator said the President had expected the army to continue, because the Coalition could not "afford to put 300,000 men with guns in their hands on the street." Garner protested the day it was announced. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 96–97 / l. 3641–3659; OnPoint p. 100 / l. 4816–4817 -->

Within days, former soldiers were at the palace gates. On 26 May five thousand officers and soldiers demonstrated in Baghdad, demanding the recall of the army, their salaries and a new government. In Mosul, where the 101st Airborne held the city, dismissed soldiers demonstrated for days outside the city hall; Iraqi police shot one dead and wounded three; in the two days of rioting that followed eighteen of Petraeus's soldiers were wounded and two Humvees burned. Riots elsewhere killed Americans and Iraqis. The Army's historians record further violence in Mosul a month on, over "the failure to provide the financial assistance promised by the CPA." <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 117 / l. 5635–5642; SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3709–3717; SIGIR places the first Mosul riot "within days", OnPoint a violent protest "one month later" — possibly two events, do not merge -->

You moved on pay. SIGIR has you modifying the policy "ten days later," announcing that the CPA would pay salaries and pensions to the disbanded army; the Army's historians date the monthly stipend for career soldiers to 23 June, four weeks after the order; you told Congress in 2007 that once commanders came to you "I immediately agreed," and that "it took us a month." The stipend was set to match old active-duty pay and excluded senior party members, internal-security officers and those accused of abuses; whether the colonel-and-above presumption still governed it the record here does not say. First disbursements came in July; by March 2004 the Coalition had paid some $45 million in stipends. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3717–3719; OnPoint p. 117–118 / l. 5644–5662; Hearing p. 186 / l. 3757–3762 -->

Order No. 22 created the New Iraqi Army on 7 August: a tenth of the old force's size, volunteer, civilian-controlled, external defence only; three divisions over two years, forty thousand men, a budget of $173 million. The general sent to build it arrived on 13 June with five staff and a twenty-four-page PowerPoint from CENTCOM, and later said the mission was "not a high priority" for the CPA or the US military. On 15 July his team asked the American division commanders for forty-five former Iraqi lieutenant colonels to seed the new officer corps; when the officers refused to serve without their old rank and pay, the team dismissed them and lowered the bar to major. Three-quarters of the new force, including almost all its officers and NCOs, had served in the old one. In August, security worsening, the Secretary of Defense halved the training timeline. <!-- CHECK: Order22 p. 1 / l. 1–25; SIGIR p. 149–150 / l. 5840–5900 -->

In February 2007 you told the House Oversight Committee: "there really was no army to disband. It certainly was a mistake to use the word, disband… I stand by the decision not to recall the army and to rebuild the army from the bottom up. The mistake I made, and I admit it, is when we announced that we were going to build the new army, we should have immediately said we are also going to pay the officers from the old army. It took us a month to do that." You added that pensions "continued all the way through our time there. They were always paid." Section 3(6), as you signed it, says otherwise for every officer of colonel rank and above. <!-- CHECK: Hearing p. 185–186 / l. 3726–3768; Order2 casi p. 2 / l. 77–84 -->

## The people who inherited it

The men Section 3(6) named. On your own count some 400,000 from the Ministry of Defence alone; SIGIR says half a million across every dissolved force. Among them every officer of colonel rank and above, dismissed with effect from a date five weeks in the past, presumed a senior party member, owed neither a termination payment nor a pension, with a rebuttal procedure that had not been written, in a country whose weapons caches were unguarded and known to them. The Army's historians put the plain fact plainly: Order No. 2 "rendered these men jobless and made their immediate economic prospects look bleak," and "the way the dissolution was conducted offended many Iraqis who saw it as disrespectful of their most respected institution." <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 1 / l. 33; SIGIR p. 96 / l. 3636–3639; OnPoint p. 115 / l. 5514–5518 -->

The Iraqi generals who had volunteered, sat in a room with the land-force commander and his deputy, and been "given guidance," and were then told by the CPA's representative that none of it would happen. McKiernan and Webster, who walked around in the dark that night. The brigade commanders who had begun reconstituting units, over leaflets that promised no reprisals, and now had to unwind it. <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 114–115 / l. 5496–5514; SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3674–3681 -->

The Iraqi policemen in Mosul who fired on a crowd of their own former soldiers. Petraeus's eighteen wounded. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3711–3716 -->

The five officers sent to build a replacement army from a PowerPoint. The forty-five lieutenant colonels who would not come back without their rank. The CPA staff who had to build, from intelligence reporting, a payroll for half a million people who had been dismissed a month before. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 149–150 / l. 5850–5890; Bremer p. 2 / l. 67–73 -->

## Reconstruction

The earliest reversible and consequential decision on this record is not the invasion, not de-Ba'athification, and not the plan formed in the OSD–CPA circle in early May. It is the signature of 23 May, because until it the recall could still be used, the officers still held rank, and Section 3(6) was still Tab B. After it, the counterparties the American commanders had been negotiating with held no position from which to negotiate, and every one of them was owed nothing.

Separate the three qualities the book asks about.

*Decision quality.* The record does not support the claim that Bremer was ignorant of the risk. He named it in writing, in his own words, four days before he signed, and then attached a payment policy that ran the risk on the cohort best able to organise a response. Whether dissolution was right is arguable on both sides — the Army's historians call the premise "reasonable," and the ratification argument is real. Whether dissolution-before-mechanism was right is harder to defend, because the man deciding had said what the mechanism was for. <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 115 / l. 5518–5519 -->

*Execution quality.* The rosters did not exist and were not made to exist first; the rebuttal procedure was not written; the stipend came in about a month and still excluded the colonels; the new army got five officers and $173 million; the officer bar was lowered on contact with reality. The 2007 admission — "we should have immediately said we are also going to pay the officers" — is an execution admission that concedes exactly the sequencing point without conceding the decision.

*Outcome quality.* Demonstrations, a riot, a month of dismissed soldiers with nothing, and a two-year gap before a force existed. How much of the insurgency this explains is contested in the record and left contested below.

Now the ownership dispute, which this chapter must not resolve by staging. RAND's account, quoted by the Iraq Inquiry, is that the decision "was… made in Washington… in early May 2003, before the deployment of Ambassador L Paul Bremer to Baghdad." Powell's is that no one could tell who had decided. Against both stand three documents: Feith's record of being briefed by Bremer and Slocombe on 9 May about *their* plan; the 19 May memorandum, written in the first person, proposing an order and asking for concurrence; and the absence in this corpus of any instruction removing the Administrator's discretion. The directive that would define his authority, NSPD-24, is still classified. The chapter's position: the policy formed in a small circle that Bremer was inside, not downstream of; the signature was his; and a reader who wants to move the seat to Washington must say who sat there. <!-- CHECK: Chilcot p. 33–34 / l. 1889–1893, 1919–1926 -->

## Competing diagnoses

1. **Sequencing** — this chapter's pivot. Abolition before the replacement force and the payment mechanism existed, when the decision-maker had written down what the mechanism was to prevent.
2. **The dissolution itself, regardless of timing.** Manning: policies that "added to the difficulties," turning men who might have been co-opted into "natural dissidents and potential insurgents." Hoon: "disbanding the army fuelled the insurgency in a way that made it much harder to contain." Petraeus: "tens of thousands, if not hundreds of thousands, of additional enemies." Colonel Paul Hughes, who was working with the old military in April and May: "strategic blunder." <!-- CHECK: Chilcot p. 35–36 / l. 2017–2050; SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3721–3725; OnPoint p. 118 / l. 5689–5691 -->
3. **Ratification, not decision.** Slocombe: "We didn't disband the army. The army disbanded itself… all the facilities were trashed." Riley, deputy head of the training team: "by the time he made that decree, the army had disbanded itself." Tebbit's answer: "Well, true, but not the 10,000 officers. So I didn't quite buy that." <!-- CHECK: Chilcot p. 33–34 / l. 1868–1888, 1932–1938 -->
4. **Diffusion of ownership.** A decision taken outside any process that would have surfaced the objections: no NSC vetting, Powell told the day before, the Vice Chairman not at all. Feith later called it "an error in judgment" not to have discussed it with other agencies — and maintained the substance. On this account 23 May is a symptom. <!-- CHECK: SIGIR p. 96–97 / l. 3641–3668 -->
5. **Order No. 1, not Order No. 2.** The clause that cut off the officers works by importing Order No. 1's "Senior Party Member," and the two orders together removed the top layers of every ministry as well as the officer corps. Where they interlock they must be read as a pair.
6. **Distributed causes.** Sawers: "I don't think it is credible to lay the insurgency, the roots of the insurgency, in the decision to disband the army… It may, in some areas, have compounded it, but it wasn't the fundamental reason behind it." Reith adds a narrower, less contested effect: without the order "there was still some structure there we could have built on, whereas, as it was, we really had to start from scratch." <!-- CHECK: Chilcot p. 36 / l. 2057–2070 -->

## Counterfactual: the same order, signed later

Keep Order No. 2 exactly as written — every dissolved entity, every cancelled rank, Section 3(6) intact. Change one thing: it is not signed until the rosters exist, the termination payments and pensions can be paid on the day of dismissal, and the officers' rebuttal procedure is published alongside the order. No better people, no more troops.

What changes? The demonstrations of late May were about salaries, pensions and recall; the Mosul violence the Army's historians record a month later was over "the failure to provide the financial assistance promised." Pay on the day plausibly removes the salary and pension grievances and does nothing about the recall. The colonels are still cut off; the two-year gap before a force exists is still two years; the officers who would not serve without their rank still would not. On this record the counterfactual changes the first month in kind and the next two years only in degree — which is the honest limit of the sequencing diagnosis. It also has a cost the record makes plain: the rosters did not exist, and "wait for the mechanism" might have meant waiting for months in a city being looted, with the majority's leaders watching. Bound it from the other side too: the recall was running, in the brigades and in Garner's payroll arrangement, so the wait need not have been a vacuum. <!-- CHECK: OnPoint p. 117 / l. 5635–5642; SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3679–3681 -->

## Judgement

Course A was chosen, and the record is unambiguous about who chose it and when. Course C was available and is not a hindsight construction: the memorandum itself keeps the Ministry of Interior, the military-industry commission and Higher Education out of the order for stated reasons, so the instrument was already being drawn selectively; it could have been drawn one line further. Course B was in motion in the field and was foreclosed by a sentence in Tab B. <!-- CHECK: Bremer p. 1 / l. 24–31 -->

Confidence: high that the risk was known to the decision-maker before he signed, because he wrote it; high that the payment mechanism was known not to exist, for the same reason; moderate that signing before the mechanism made the first month worse than it needed to be, since the record ties the demonstrations to pay and the stipend to their stopping; low-to-moderate on how much of what followed the order explains, because the record disagrees with itself and the chapter does not pretend otherwise. What the record cannot establish: what Bremer was told of the objections and when; whether Washington would have let him wait; whether a recalled army would have held.

## How the story was remembered

As the single blunder that made the insurgency, or as the abolition of an army that had already abolished itself, marred by a month's delay in a press release. The record supports neither alone. It shows a decision-maker who wrote the risk down and accepted it; a payment clause that cut off the officers while the popular account says nobody was paid and the official account says everybody was; a recall running in the field that the order's own annex was written to stop; and a dispute about who decided that the participants have not settled twenty years on. The chapter keeps all of it, and declines to decide the war.

## Where this stops being true

Abolishing an institution before its replacement exists is right when the institution is itself the active danger, when the people it employs are not the ones you are relying on for security, and when delay would let it entrench. The Mukhabarat, the Special Security Organization and the Fedayeen meet that test in May 2003; nobody in the record proposed keeping them, and a reader who signs Course C has abolished them. The regular army and its pension roll do not meet it, on this record. Where the whole of an institution is the danger — a secret police, a militia — this chapter's caution does not apply, and waiting for a payroll would be the error.

## Transfer

When you dissolve something, the people you dismiss become a decision of yours, not a side effect of it. Their payment — what they are owed, on what date, by what mechanism — belongs on the same page as the dissolution, and dated. Bremer's memorandum had the risk on page two and the mechanism nowhere; that is the shape to recognise.

**What transfers:** dismissal before mechanism, and the tell that goes with it — a decision document that names a risk and then defers the thing that would contain it to "the next step."

**What does not transfer:** occupation law, a viceroy's authority, war powers, the legality of the invasion, and the moral weight of an army that had been used for genocide.

**Solo-actionable version:** for any shutdown, cancellation or reorganisation you sign, write on the same page who is dismissed, what they are owed, on what date it reaches them, and what happens if the mechanism is not ready by then.

## Field exercise

The next time a team, a contract, a service or a system is retired in your organisation, find the approval and ask one question: on what date does everyone displaced by this receive what they are owed, and does that machinery exist today? Write the answer and the date beside the decision. Pass when the date is earlier than the shutdown date, or is later and explicitly accepted in writing by the person signing. Review on the shutdown date. Classification: decision, if the risk was named and the mechanism deferred; execution, if the mechanism existed and failed; uncertain, if nobody wrote either down.

Before somebody has to be brave, where is the last reversible decision?
