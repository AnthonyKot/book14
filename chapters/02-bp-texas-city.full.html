Case 2 · full chapter with receipts

The alarms can wait

BP Texas City refinery · 22–23 March 2005 · Decision-forcing case

Instrument technicians are checking the critical alarms before a refinery unit starts. They have not finished.

A frontline supervisor tells them to stop. The unit is starting, and there is no time for the remaining checks. Some alarms have been tested; most have not. On the startup procedure, however, the alarm checks are initialled as complete.

Feed has not yet entered the raffinate splitter tower. The immediate question is narrower than the accident that will follow: what may be written beside an unfinished protection?

Role card

  • You are: Supervisor A, the anonymized, ISOM-experienced frontline supervisor whom the Chemical Safety Board records as stopping the critical-alarm checks.
  • Objective: Start the raffinate section and return it to service without losing containment.
  • Clock: Hours before the night shift begins introducing hydrocarbons.
  • Primary resource: Turnaround time exchanged for verified alarm status and an accurate startup record.
  • Authority: You can refuse to initial an unfinished check and communicate that work remains open. The record does not show that you can cancel or authorize the startup.
  • Constraint: Other managers own parts of the startup decision, but the surviving record does not identify one higher-level authorizer.

That final constraint matters. The CSB did not publish this supervisor's name. It found that frontline supervision initiated the startup, but no manager above the frontline level acknowledged authorizing it. This chapter will not fill that hole with an invented executive giving an invented order. You occupy the narrow decision seat the record actually preserves.

What is on the table

Known to this role

  • Critical-alarm functionality checks are required before startup.
  • The technicians have not completed those checks because you stopped them.
  • Most alarms remain untested, but the procedure has a line on which their checks can be initialled as complete.
  • The raffinate section is expected to start when the connected aromatics unit is back in operation. Completing the turnaround has an operational value; delay is not free.

Reasonably inferable

  • An alarm that has not been function-tested is not an independent protection merely because its icon exists on a screen.
  • If the procedure is initialled, the next person reading it may reasonably treat the check as complete.
  • Leaving the check visibly open preserves a chance for the next decision-maker to ask what remains unavailable.

Possible

  • The unchecked alarms may work normally.
  • Completing the checks may reveal no defect and cost only time.
  • Completing the checks may reveal a defect that changes the startup decision.

Unknown

  • Whether each unchecked alarm will function.
  • Whether the remaining work can be completed within the planned startup window.
  • Whether someone reviewing the procedure later will distinguish an incomplete check from a completed one.

Unknowable from the surviving record

  • The supervisor's private estimate of the risk.
  • Whether schedule pressure, confidence in the crew, habit or some other consideration dominated the instruction to stop testing.

The decision

A. Certify the incomplete check

Initial the alarm-check line and let the planned startup continue. The unchecked alarms may work. This protects the schedule, but the record no longer tells the incoming crew or a later reviewer which protections were actually tested.

B. Keep the check open and request the formal review

Do not initial the line. Record which checks remain unfinished, communicate the open work and ask the operations superintendent or process-safety route to apply the refinery's pre-startup safety review. The review existed in refinery practice, although the version later cited by the CSB was still marked “For Comment.” You can request it; the evidence does not establish that you can compel it.

C. Require a named higher-level acceptance

Communicate that the required checks are unfinished and decline to let silence or an initial stand for completion. If someone with authority chooses to proceed, require that person's name and acceptance of the open item on the startup record. This does not repair an alarm or create a compensating safeguard. It preserves who decided what with the information then available.

What do you do? What new evidence would make you reverse that choice?

Commit before reading on. Choose what the incoming shift should see on the startup record.

What happened

The raffinate section began filling during the night shift, then stopped in a state the written procedure did not cover. The night lead operator left early after a brief turnover. The day board operator received little precise information about what equipment had already been filled. A separate morning meeting ended with an understanding that the section would not start because storage tanks were thought to be full, but that message did not reach the ISOM operating crew. Supervisor A told the crew to start.

At 9:51 a.m. feed resumed into a tower that already contained a high liquid level. For more than three hours, feed entered while little or no heavy raffinate initially left. The board display showed the tower level falling. The level transmitter was wrong, the independent high-level alarm did not sound, and the sight glass could not provide a visual check.

At 1:04 p.m. the display read 78 percent of the transmitter's narrow measurement span near the tower bottom. Post-incident calculations put the actual liquid level at 158 feet in a 170-foot tower.

Heating expanded the liquid until it overflowed into the overhead piping. At about 1:14, three relief valves opened and sent an estimated 51,900 gallons of flammable liquid toward an atmospheric blowdown drum and stack. The system could not contain it. The CSB calculated that approximately 7,600 gallons emerged during 107 seconds of stack flow before the relief valves closed, forming a vapour cloud near the ground.

At 1:20 p.m. the cloud ignited. The most likely ignition source was an idling diesel pickup near the blowdown drum. Fifteen contract workers were killed and 180 people were injured. All the fatalities occurred in or near temporary office trailers placed close to the ISOM unit.

This was the combined result. The unfinished check belongs in the causal record, but it is not a sufficient explanation by itself.

The people who inherited the start

The day board operator was not a novice. He had six years at the control board and had participated in four startups during the preceding five years. He was also one person monitoring three units while controlling an abnormal operation. He had worked twelve-hour shifts for 29 consecutive days. The night lead operator, who filled the tower during the early hours, had worked 33 consecutive days. The CSB judged that many ISOM operators were likely fatigued; its detailed performance analysis focused on the day board operator. That wider judgement is not evidence that every individual had the same degree of impairment.

The unit's established informal practice had a reason. Previous startups produced violent level swings. If the tower lost too much liquid, flow through a furnace could be lost and equipment could be damaged. Operators therefore tended to fill high and use manual control. Across nineteen startups examined by the CSB, only one kept both level within the transmitter's range and pressure within the stated alarm limits. The deviations had become the practical method for getting the unit started; they had not been investigated and converted into a safer, accurate procedure.

When pressure rose shortly before the release, the crew reduced furnace heat and worked to increase liquid flow out. The indications supported a familiar diagnosis: non-condensable gas or another pressure problem, not a tower almost full of liquid. After the first radio report that the blowdown stack was overflowing, the board and lead operators moved to shut off furnace fuel. Outside operators ran toward the road to redirect traffic. The CSB found that the crew had insufficient time to sound the site alarm before the explosion.

These actions did not fix the problem. They were still decisions made under bad information, not evidence that the people below were passive or incompetent.

The contractors in the trailers inherited a different kind of risk. They were not essential to the startup and had no useful control over tower level, relief design or ignition. The trailers were there primarily because the location was convenient for turnaround work. Emergency teams later mounted a rapid search-and-rescue response without responder injuries, but rescue could begin only after the blast had made the transferred risk visible.

The missing gate

It is tempting to identify the order to stop alarm testing as the cause. The record does not support that simplicity.

The refinery had a formal pre-startup safety review process that had been used after turnarounds during the preceding two years. Its status was administratively imperfect—the version cited by the CSB was marked for comment and was not formally adopted until after the incident—but its function was precise. A technical team led by the operations superintendent was to check procedures, training, alarms, equipment, staffing and removal of nonessential people. Higher management was to sign the checklist and authorize startup. None of those review steps occurred for this start.

That missing gate allowed facts to remain in separate rooms: an unfinished alarm test, an inaccurate transmitter report, an unreadable sight glass, a failed valve test, insufficient staffing, an unconventional stop-and-restart, trailers close to the unit and an atmospheric relief path with a history of releases. The historical file does not identify one person who held all of these facts and consciously accepted their combined risk.

The earliest reversible decisions were years earlier. Amoco and BP had multiple opportunities to replace the atmospheric blowdown system with a flare or another closed-system configuration. Earlier releases and tower overfills could have triggered redesign. Trailer-siting reviews could have moved the contractors. Those decisions explain why an overfill became lethal.

For this chapter, however, the final consequential gate is readiness to introduce hazardous material while safeguards are degraded and their interactions have not been reviewed. It is later than the deepest cause but earlier than the operator's struggle with a false display.

Three diagnoses

The operators failed to execute the procedure

This diagnosis has real support. Feed entered for hours without verified corresponding flow out. The level-control valve was not operated as the written procedure directed. A material balance or compliance with the specified tower level would probably have exposed or avoided the accumulation.

But the same procedure was outdated, omitted the interrupted-start condition, did not teach the material-balance calculation and conflicted with work practices crews had developed to protect equipment. Execution errors were immediate causes; “follow the procedure” does not explain why repeated deviations had become normal.

Supervision accepted a start without functioning safeguards

This is also strongly supported. Required checks were recorded as complete when they were not. Equipment problems remained. No formal readiness review integrated the degradations, and experienced technical help was not maintained throughout startup. This diagnosis reaches the chapter's decision question without pretending that one supervisor knew the eventual outcome.

Design and siting converted an upset into mass casualty

This diagnosis explains the human bill. A tower overfill did not require an atmospheric flammable-liquid geyser; a flare or closed-system configuration could change the release path. A vapour-cloud explosion did not require fifteen deaths; moving or hardening the occupied trailers could change the consequence. Operator execution alone cannot explain why contractors uninvolved in the start were placed inside its blast hazard.

The evidence supports all three: a defective readiness decision, execution failures and design choices that amplified the consequences.

Counterfactual: restore the level protections

Change two variables only. Before startup, calibrate the tower level transmitter for the actual process fluid and function-test the independent high-level alarm. Leave the procedure, staffing, blowdown stack and trailers unchanged. Do not assume perfect operator performance.

Restoring both protections substantially changes the detection problem: operators gain a reliable level indication and an independent alert. That would materially reduce the chance of this particular overfill and could prevent it. Prevention is not certain. Operators customarily ran above the transmitter's range; the ordinary high-level alarm remained active for long periods during prior startups; and the independent alarm's set point was not in their procedures or training.

The counterfactual does not change the consequence path if a large release still occurs. The atmospheric stack and occupied trailers remain, so flammable material can still vent beside people who cannot control the startup. Restored detection is consequential; it is not a substitute for correcting release design and siting.

Judgement

Best-supported diagnosis: a distributed system failure with a poor startup-readiness decision, high confidence.

For this role, initialling the check was weak on the information then available: most required alarm checks were known to be unfinished, but the initial conveyed completion. The system record separately establishes that no pre-startup safety review steps were performed, startup proceeded with malfunctioning instruments and equipment, and no ISOM-experienced replacement was assigned after Supervisor A left for a family emergency. Those are organizational facts, not proof that Supervisor A personally held all of them.

Execution quality: poor in consequential respects, but shaped by misleading instruments, fatigue, inadequate staffing, incomplete turnover, insufficient training and a procedure that did not represent actual work.

Outcome quality: catastrophic, amplified by an unsafe relief path and trailer placement.

The evidence cannot establish the private motive of Supervisor A, the identity of a single higher-level authorizer, or that completing the alarm checks alone would have prevented the explosion. It can establish that most alarm checks were unfinished although the procedure was initialled, that no pre-startup safety review steps occurred and that startup proceeded.

Where this stops being true

“Never operate degraded” is not a workable rule. Some operations face a failure whose repair or shutdown introduces a greater immediate hazard. Delay can also harm people.

Proceeding can be reasonable when the missing protection is explicitly identified; the compensating control is independent and function-tested; one person owns it; an observable threshold triggers retreat; the failure remains containable; and people who cannot influence the operation are removed from its consequence zone. Under those conditions, a controlled degraded start may preserve more safety than an improvised shutdown or indefinite delay.

The recommendation reverses when stopping is more dangerous and the workaround has been demonstrated—not merely described.

What transfers

Count dependencies, not defects. Three missing safeguards do not become safe because three people promise to watch harder if all three depend on the same display, the same procedure or the same overloaded operator.

Before accepting degraded operation, write:

The missing protection is _____. The independent replacement is _____. Its owner will observe _____. We stop when _____.

An empty final blank means the plan has no operational exit.

What does not transfer

A reader cannot infer refinery operating limits from this case, and an ordinary workplace workaround does not carry the same consequence as a hydrocarbon unit startup. Process design, instrument calibration, relief capacity and facility siting require qualified specialists and formal authority.

Solo-actionable version

Do not mark a check complete because the work has been scheduled, discussed or assigned. Mark it complete only when you can name the evidence of completion. If you lack authority to hold the start, record the open protection, its common dependencies and the person accepting the residual risk.

Field exercise

For the next three temporary-workaround decisions you encounter:

  1. Mark the first moment someone proposes continuing without a normal protection.
  2. Record the missing protection, the proposed substitute, its owner and one observable abort signal.
  3. Record whether the substitute depends on the same person, information source or failure mode as the missing protection.
  4. Afterward, classify the problem as decision, execution or uncertain without changing the original ledger to match the outcome.

Pass condition: all three cases have a named owner and an observable abort signal; every shared dependency is explicitly marked.

Review condition: if a workaround succeeds, ask whether it demonstrated that the substitute worked or merely that the initiating failure did not occur. An uneventful start is not a function test.
