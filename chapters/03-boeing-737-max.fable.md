# Transparent to the crew

*Boeing 737 MAX program · Everett and Seattle · 2016–2019 · Decision-forcing case*

On Wednesday, 30 March 2016, in a fourth-floor conference room on Boeing's Everett campus, the aerodynamics stability-and-control group briefs the leadership of the 737 MAX program. The slides are titled "737 MAX / Stall Characteristics – Mitigation." Two months into flight test the airplane is not stalling cleanly with the flaps up at low speed; the fix on the table is not aerodynamic but software—widen the envelope of an existing function so that it also works down at approach speeds, and give it more authority. The general manager approves the flight-control configuration as the mitigation. The chief project engineer approves it too. <!-- CHECK: House p. 111 / l. 5878–5895; p. 27 / l. 1278–1281 -->

At 11:16 that morning the program's chief technical pilot has already sent an email to the Federal Aviation Administration's Aircraft Evaluation Group. Subject: "MCAS lives in both FCCs." He asks to delete the function from the Flight Crew Operations Manual and the training-differences tables, because it is "completely transparent to the flight crew and only operates WAY outside of the normal operating envelope." The FAA agrees, verbally, at a later meeting. <!-- CHECK: House p. 126 / l. 6733–6737; OIG-T p. 24 / l. 933–942; date 30 Mar vs 3 Mar footnote inconsistency -->

Nothing has gone wrong. The airplane will pass its stall demonstrations, and the pilots who fly it will never need to know why.

## The hand you are dealt

**You are Michael Teal**, Vice President and Chief Project Engineer of the 737 MAX. You have held the job since August 2011. Keith Leverkuhn runs the program; you own the engineering. You supervise no engineers directly and sign off on their key decisions—as you will later put it, "none of them worked for me but all of them worked for me." <!-- CHECK: House p. 5 / l. 189–192; p. 124 / l. 6640–6648 -->

The MAX is a re-engined 737: bigger, more efficient LEAP engines hung further forward and higher on a 1967 wing. In 2012 the wind tunnel and then the simulator showed that at high angle of attack the nacelles add lift and the nose wants to keep coming up; the column force stops building the way Part 25 says it must. Vortex generators were tried and rejected. The answer was the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System: a control law inside the existing speed-trim system that, when angle of attack passes a threshold with flaps up, runs the horizontal stabilizer nose-down for a few seconds. As designed, it lived only in the corner of the envelope where a certification test pilot pulls a wind-up turn—high Mach, high g—and could move the stabilizer about six tenths of a degree. <!-- CHECK: House p. 95–96 / l. 5036–5097; p. 110 / l. 5820–5826; OIG-T p. 17 / l. 618–620 -->

Since June 2013 the program has described MCAS to the regulator as an addition to speed trim rather than a new function, because "if we emphasize MCAS is a new function there may be a greater certification and training impact." That was a considered choice, minuted, with an FAA-authorized Boeing representative concurring. <!-- CHECK: House p. 26–27 / l. 1225–1271 -->

- **Objective:** certify the MAX in March 2017 as the same type as the NG, with computer-based differences training and no simulator—Level B. You will later call Level B a "design objective." Under the December 2011 launch contract, Boeing owes Southwest at least a million dollars per airplane if the FAA requires simulator training; Southwest has 200 on firm order. <!-- CHECK: House p. 31 / l. 1526–1540; p. 32 / l. 1598–1600 -->
- **Clock:** the week of 30 March 2016. First flight was 29 January; the FAA issued its Type Inspection Authorization on 14 March; certification flight test with FAA pilots begins next month. <!-- CHECK: OIG-T p. 23 / l. 866–878 -->
- **Primary resource:** commonality—the schedule and training hours you spend, or do not, to keep the MAX "the same airplane."
- **Authority:** the design change; what the safety analysis re-examines; which certification plans get re-opened; what the FAA is told and what the manual says.
- **Constraint:** without MCAS the airplane does not meet the stall rules; the flight-test window is open now; the regulator has already accepted the "addition to speed trim" framing.

## Evidence ledger

**Known — in front of you.** Coordination Sheet Rev. D, dated today, revises the requirements for "737MAX Flaps Up High Alpha Stabilizer Trim (MCAS)." Activation, which used to require Mach 0.67 or more and elevated g, now runs from Mach 0.2 to 0.84; the maximum stabilizer command per activation rises from about 0.6° to 2.5°—an increase of over 300 percent. The single angle-of-attack input and the logic that lets the function fire again five seconds after the crew trims against it are unchanged; they simply now operate across ordinary flaps-up flight. The sheet says the function "shall not have any objectionable interaction with the piloting of the airplane" and "shall not interfere with dive recovery." <!-- CHECK: OIG-T p. 23 / l. 890–909; House p. 127 / l. 6797–6800; NTSB-ASR p. 4 / l. 159–166 -->

Rev. D also carries a functional hazard assessment. Uncommanded MCAS running until the pilot acts is assumed to be recognised as a runaway stabilizer, a memory-item procedure every 737 pilot has. A "typical" reaction of about four seconds makes the condition Hazardous; a statistical credit for how rarely a flight would be outside normal parameters brings it down to Major. Major means no redundancy is required—one sensor is enough. The same sheet has said, since October 2015 and will say through 2018: "A slow reaction time scenario (>10 seconds) found the failure to be catastrophic due to the inability to arrest the airplane overspeed." <!-- CHECK: OIG-T p. 24 / l. 918–926, 957–960; p. 28 / l. 1122–1130; House p. 122 / l. 6510–6520 -->

The four seconds are not Boeing's invention. FAA guidance for flight-guidance systems allows one second to recognise and three to react; test pilots delay their recovery by that much on purpose. Nobody in the record can say what study those numbers rest on. <!-- CHECK: JATR p. 34 / l. 1442–1466 -->

**Known — in the building, not in the room.** In November 2012, in the simulator, two Boeing pilots flew a stabilizer runaway in a wind-up turn. One, "with teamwork," reached the cutout switches in about four seconds. The other took more than ten and called it catastrophic. The engineer who wrote it up asked whether "with pilot training/knowledge of the system there will be a sufficiently quick response," or whether "we should step up to catastrophic with the assumption that not all pilots will recognize it quickly enough." In December 2015 an engineer who is also one of the company's FAA-authorized representatives asked in writing: "Are we vulnerable to single AOA sensor failures with the MCAS implementation or is there some checking that occurs?" You will later testify that when you approved the redesign you did not know MCAS ran on one sensor, could fire repeatedly, or that the ten-second result existed. The record puts all three inside your program before you signed. <!-- CHECK: House p. 120–121 / l. 6426–6473; p. 115 / l. 6136–6138; p. 28 / l. 1350–1355 -->

**Reasonably inferable.** For approving as presented: the FAA's own guidance sets the reaction time; the runaway procedure exists; the classification is Major and the sensor's service record runs to hundreds of millions of hours. Against it: a function that could once only fire in a corner of the envelope no test pilot enters by accident will now fire wherever an angle-of-attack vane reads high, and the crew who must catch it in four seconds have been told, as of this morning, that it does not exist.

**Possible.** That the AEG, told the same day what "transparent" now covers, would have asked for a simulator; that a fresh look at the low-speed case would leave the classification where it is.

**Unknown.** How often an AOA vane fails high in service, and what else lights up in the cockpit when it does. Whether a line crew—not two Boeing test pilots—reads a stick shaker at rotation, a speed disagree and a trim wheel turning as one problem or three.

**Unknowable.** Which crew, on which morning.

## The decision

Three courses are within your authority this week.

**A. Approve the aerodynamic fix; re-open the safety case.** Take Rev. D, and require the functional hazard assessment, the system safety assessment and the certification plans to be re-issued for the airplane you are actually going to ship: low-speed table, 2.5°, single sensor, repeat logic, and the flight-deck effects of a failed AOA vane. Brief the AEG on the new envelope before it acts on "transparent"; keep MCAS in the manual until that review is done. Cost: weeks in a schedule counted in days, and a real risk to Level B.

**B. Change the design instead of the analysis.** Two-sensor comparison, one activation per event, authority limited so that the column can always hold the nose. That is how the same function is built on the KC-46 tanker, and it is roughly what the FAA will require in 2020. Cost: months and a new software qualification, in flight test, a year from certification. <!-- CHECK: House p. 110 / l. 5812–5819; FAA-RTS p. 8 / l. 236–256 -->

**C. Approve as presented.** The high-speed case was analysed and remains the critical one; the hazard classes were re-checked in the simulator and did not move; the crew procedure exists. This is what the record shows the program did.

Commit. Then name one observation that would change your answer. If nothing would—if "the crew will catch it" is true by definition—you hold a doctrine, not a decision.

## What happened

Boeing approved as presented. The safety assessment was never re-issued beyond Rev. C, the high-speed table; the company judged high speed the critical case. Uncommanded MCAS to full authority was simulated only to the 0.6° high-speed limit, never to 2.5° at low speed; repeated activation was not simulated—engineers and pilots "deemed" it no worse than one, in a meeting summary email of 22 June 2016 that never entered the certification record. Rev. D's coordination documents were not certification deliverables and were not sent to FAA certification engineers. <!-- CHECK: JATR p. 54 / l. 2399–2410; KNKT p. 192 / l. 7797–7802; OIG-T p. 24 / l. 927–931; p. 28 / l. 1142–1146 -->

Flight test kept asking the question. On 13 June 2016 MCAS fought a pilot's trim in a low-speed manoeuvre; the same authorized representative called it "ratchiness" and asked for a squawk. On 15 June an engineer asked, "What happens when we have faulty AOA or Mach number?" and was told it "shuts down immediately." On 20 June another was told the concern was "not safety, other than the pilot could fight the MCAS input and over time find themselves in a large mistrim." The 22 June MCAS Review minuted "no need to redesign" and "no additional flight testing." <!-- CHECK: House p. 116–117 / l. 6175–6300 -->

On 16 August 2016 the FAA's Flight Standardization Board approved Level B differences training; the approved course contained nothing on MCAS. On 10 November the FAA delegated the stabilizer certification plan to Boeing's own designees; the FAA engineer who recommended it "was not aware of the increased MCAS range from 0.55 degrees to 2.5 degrees." The formal flight-control safety assessment reached the FAA in January 2017, four years into a five-year program. By March 2017 the FAA had delegated all 91 certification plans. The amended type certificate was issued on 8 March 2017; the first MAX flew for Malindo Air on 22 May. In August 2017 Boeing found that the AOA DISAGREE alert worked only on airplanes whose customers had bought an optional display—about one in five—and, with a designee's concurrence, deferred the fix to 2020. Neither accident aircraft had the alert. <!-- CHECK: OIG-T p. 26–31 / l. 1019–1032, 1101–1120, 1181–1194, 1241–1285; House p. 131 / l. 6963–6966 -->

**Lion Air.** On 28 October 2018 a replacement left AOA vane, mis-calibrated by about 21° at a repair shop in Florida, was fitted to PK-LQP in Denpasar. On the evening flight to Jakarta the left stick shaker started at rotation and ran for 96 minutes. The captain gave the airplane to the first officer, ran the airspeed-unreliable memory items, and—when the trim kept running nose-down—treated it as a runaway stabilizer and cut the switches; a deadheading MAX pilot in the jump seat had told him the airplane "was diving down." That took about three minutes and forty seconds and twelve MCAS activations. They landed on manual trim. The log entry mentioned speed and altitude disagreements; not the shaker, not the runaway procedure, not the cutout. <!-- CHECK: KNKT p. 20 / l. 670–678; p. 168–169 / l. 6613–6668; p. 201 / l. 8239–8240 -->

Next morning, 29 October, flight 610 lifted off with the same vane. Stick shaker at rotation; the captain's speed and altitude lower than the first officer's; flight-director bars pointing different ways. When the flaps came up the stabilizer ran nose-down for ten seconds. The captain trimmed against it; five seconds later it ran again. Over the next six minutes it ran more than twenty times, and he answered every one, while the first officer looked for a checklist that fit and Jakarta approach kept talking. At 23:30:48 UTC he handed over control. The first officer's answers were shorter. The recorder stopped at 23:31:54, with MCAS running; 189 people. <!-- CHECK: KNKT p. 29 / l. 1113–1115 --> The crew had never read the word MCAS. <!-- CHECK: NTSB-ASR p. 2 / l. 61–76; KNKT p. 21–29 / l. 712–1111; p. 215 / l. 8834–8837 -->

On 7 November the FAA issued an emergency airworthiness directive expanding the runaway-stabilizer procedure for erroneous AOA; it did not name MCAS. <!-- CHECK: NTSB-ASR p. 11 / l. 556–562; OIG-T p. 32 / l. 1307–1312 -->

**Ethiopian.** On 10 March 2019 the left AOA vane of ET-AVJ went to 74.5° seconds after liftoff from Addis Ababa—the Ethiopian bureau says an electrical fault, the NTSB says a bird. Stick shaker; speed disagree; then, flaps up, nine seconds of nose-down trim. The captain trimmed back, partly; five seconds later it ran again; the crew discussed the cutout and used it, forty-two seconds after the autopilot dropped out. The airplane was now out of trim at high speed with the electric trim dead, and the first officer could not turn the manual wheel: about 2.5° of mistrim at 340 knots, forty revolutions to correct, column forces the investigators calculated above 90 pounds. Thirty-two seconds before impact the switches were back on and the crew trimmed up electrically; five seconds later MCAS ran again. Descent rate at impact: 33,000 feet a minute. 157 people. This crew had the emergency directive. The NTSB, dissenting from the Ethiopian report, wanted the probable cause to add "the flight crew's inadequate use of manual electric trim and management of thrust": the throttles stayed at takeoff power throughout, and the crew barely used the electric trim that would have unloaded the wheel before they cut it. <!-- CHECK: NTSB-US p. 2 / l. 60–72; EAIB l. 4613 --> <!-- CHECK: NTSB-ASR p. 3 / l. 114–134; EAIB p. 120–125 / l. 4246–4431; p. 255 / l. 8871–8875; NTSB-EAIB p. 1–2 / l. 33–58 -->

The FAA grounded the fleet on 13 March 2019, three days after Ethiopian. <!-- CHECK: House p. 19 / l. 800 --> It returned in November 2020 with MCAS reading both vanes, firing once per event, and limited so the column alone can always hold level flight. <!-- CHECK: FAA-RTS p. 8 / l. 236–256; p. 28 / l. 1217–1227 -->

## The people who inherited it

The FAA Aircraft Evaluation Group representative who said yes to "transparent" on the understanding that MCAS was still a 0.55° high-speed function; the FAA engineer who delegated the stabilizer plan without knowing about 2.5°; the designee who asked about single-sensor vulnerability in 2015 and the engineer who asked about faulty AOA in June 2016, both answered and both dropped. <!-- CHECK: OIG-T p. 24 / l. 945–948; p. 28 / l. 1116–1119 -->

The Lion Air 043 captain and the pilot in the jump seat, who solved it in three minutes forty with a third pair of eyes and wrote up what fit the log. The engineer in Jakarta who read that log. The 610 captain, 31, 6,028 hours, 5,176 on the 737; the first officer, 41, 5,174 hours—both trained on a differences course that did not contain the system, flying an airplane whose disagree alert had been switched off by a software pairing nobody told them about. <!-- CHECK: KNKT p. 30–32 / l. 1149–1216 -->

The Ethiopian 302 captain, 29, 8,122 hours, 103 on the MAX; the first officer, 25, 361 hours in total, qualified on the MAX the same day he qualified on the NG, a month into line flying. They had the procedure. They ran it. The trim wheel would not turn. <!-- CHECK: EAIB p. 32–34 / l. 1250–1344 -->

Ethiopian's training department had asked Boeing, after Lion Air, to clarify MCAS, checklist priority and whether the runaway procedure was enough; Boeing did not answer everything, citing the open investigation. <!-- CHECK: EAIB p. 254–255 / l. 8855–8869 -->

## Reconstruction

The decision of 30 March 2016 was not to build a dangerous system. It was to change what a system could do without changing what anyone had said about it—and to say, the same day, that the crew need not be told. Three documents held the airplane's story: the coordination sheet, which had the new envelope and the ten-second sentence; the safety assessment, which had the old envelope and the four-second assumption; and the manual, which by evening had nothing. Each was internally coherent. They stopped describing the same airplane on the day the chief technical pilot's email and the leadership review coincided—coordinated or not, the House committee could not say. <!-- CHECK: House p. 27 / l. 1290–1292 -->

Set the internal record and the regulator's side by side. Boeing's position, given to the committee: it told the FAA "on numerous occasions" that MCAS's range had expanded, and FAA pilots saw the expanded function fly. The Inspector General's finding: FAA flight-test personnel knew about the 2.5° computer in the test airplane; the AEG and the certification engineers did not, and the safety assessment's hazard table was never changed. Both can be true. Knowledge of the new envelope reached the part of the FAA that flies airplanes and never the part that decides what pilots must be taught. <!-- CHECK: House p. 111 / l. 5897–5904; OIG-T p. 25 / l. 976–981 -->

The four seconds deserve their own line. The Joint Authorities Technical Review could find no study behind them; the NTSB found that Boeing's simulator work "did not evaluate all the potential alerts and indications" that come with a failed vane—the shaker, the speed and altitude disagrees were not simulated and were not in the assessment. In three flights the responses were three different things: 043 recovered in three-forty; 610 held the airplane for six minutes and lost it at handover; 302 ran the procedure and could not physically finish it. None matched the assumption. <!-- CHECK: NTSB-ASR p. 5 / l. 219–229; p. 8 / l. 373–378 -->

## Competing diagnoses

1. **The assessment that was not re-opened** — this chapter's pivot. The function changed; the safety case, the certification plans and the manual did not; the FAA never got the sheet with the ten-second sentence.
2. **Single-sensor architecture, regardless of paperwork.** An updated assessment could have re-affirmed Major; Boeing's simulator re-check did. What killed was one vane driving repeated full-authority trim. The FAA's return-to-service fix is this diagnosis made law.
3. **Certification governance.** 79 then 91 of 91 plans delegated to Boeing's own designees; a BASOO of 42 people over 1,500 of them; an AEG that decided training on a verbal request. On this account 30 March 2016 was a symptom, and the earliest failure is the delegation regime.
4. **Execution in service.** A mis-calibrated vane, an installation test that may not have been done, a log entry that left out the shaker; on 302, throttles that stayed at takeoff thrust and an airplane past 340 knots. KNKT lists the first three; the NTSB's dissent makes the last a cause, not a footnote. <!-- CHECK: NTSB-US p. 2 / l. 60–72 --> They shortened the time; they did not create the function.
5. **The training clause.** A million dollars an airplane, "HOLDING FIRM THAT THERE WILL NOT BE ANY TYPE OF SIMULATOR TRAINING," "jedi mind tricked this fools." The incentive is documented; the record does not show it in the room on 30 March, and the House found no evidence the technical pilot was told of the redesign. <!-- CHECK: House p. 126 / l. 6753–6757; p. 164 / l. 8842–8867 -->

## Counterfactual: the same airplane, and a crew that has been told

Keep the hardware and the software exactly as certified. Put MCAS back in the FCOM, put a page in the differences course, and give every crew one simulator session with a runaway that starts with a stick shaker at rotation. What changes?

The record bounds it from both ends. Flight 043 recovered *without* the name, with a third pilot and three minutes forty seconds; the assumption was four. Flight 302 had the name in an emergency directive and the procedure in memory, cut the switches, and could not turn the wheel at 340 knots against 2.5° of mistrim; knowledge did not restore the authority the design had taken. Between them sits 610, where the captain did for six minutes what the assessment assumed and lost it when he handed over. Disclosure and a simulator plausibly buy the recognition; they do not buy the physical margin. On this record the counterfactual changes the accidents in degree, not in kind, and design stays causal. But hold the other bound: the FAA's own November 2018 directive, which *did* tell crews, was followed by 302. Telling was necessary and was not sufficient. <!-- CHECK: KNKT p. 201 / l. 8239–8240; EAIB p. 122–125 -->

## Judgement

Course A was available on 30 March 2016 at the price of schedule and a risk to Level B, and it is the course the chief project engineer himself later described as the ordinary one—"the certification plans would have to be updated." Course B was the eventual remedy. Course C was chosen, and the record is unambiguous that its cost was borne by 346 people and two crews who were expected to do in four seconds what nobody had shown a line crew could do at that speed with those alarms. Confidence: high that the safety case should have been re-issued; moderate that re-issuing it would have changed the classification, because Boeing's own re-check said no; high that keeping the function in the manual and the differences course was the last cheap reversible act, and that its removal was made on a description of the airplane that had stopped being true that morning. <!-- CHECK: House p. 112 / l. 5950–5952 -->

## How the story was remembered

As greed with a countdown clock, or as foreign pilots who could not fly. The record supports neither alone. It shows an engineering authority who signed a redesign without three facts that sat in his own program's files, a regulator whose flying arm and training arm did not talk, and two cockpits that were different in almost every particular except the function nobody had named. The Ethiopian and American investigators still disagree in print about the vane and the crew; the KNKT names nine factors and refuses to rank them. The chapter keeps all of them.

## Where this stops being true

A design may lean on the operator to recover from a failure when four things hold: the failure is recognisable as itself; the time available has been demonstrated at the configuration actually shipped, with the alarms that will actually sound; the recovery is trained; and the failure lands inside a model the operator already holds. The runaway-stabilizer procedure met the third. Rev. D failed the first and the fourth on the day it was approved, and the second was never attempted at low speed. Where all four hold—an autothrottle disconnect a crew sees on every simulator check, say—the assumption is honest and this chapter does not apply.

## Transfer

Every plan that says "the user will notice and correct it" is a safety component with a response time in it. Treat it as you would a part: it needs evidence at the configuration you ship, and re-evidence when the configuration changes. Ask, for the version in front of you and not the one that was analysed: what cue does the operator get, how many seconds, and where did they learn what the cue means?

## Field exercise

In your next three design or change reviews, mark every sentence that assumes a human will catch a failure. For each, write the observable cue, the tested response window *at the current configuration*, and the named place the operator learned it. Pass when all three exist. Fail when any is inherited from an earlier version—and when the change review and the "no training needed" decision are dated the same day, stop.

Before somebody has to be brave, where is the last reversible decision?

*This chapter teaches the reader to ask what a recovery assumption rests on. Challenger asks whether the proof at a go/no-go boundary was ever made; Triangle asks whether a safeguard was designed to open for the person who needs it; this chapter asks whether the person expected to save the machine in four seconds was told the machine existed.*
