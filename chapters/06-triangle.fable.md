# The key on a string

*Triangle Waist Company · New York · 1909–1911 · Decision-forcing case*

About eight months before the fire, a presser on the ninth floor walks toward the Greene Street door at closing time with two shirtwaists folded into the pad of false hair under her own. A thread shows. The girl behind her pulls it, the waist comes out, and the presser is taken to the office. She says her mistress asked her to bring two waists. The firm does not prosecute. It has tried that—six girls arrested three years earlier, some fined, then lawsuits for damages—and Isaac Harris has concluded that "the best way is to discharge them and not be bothered with them any more." She is discharged. The line at the Greene Street door keeps moving: open the pocketbook, show the watchman, go down.

Nothing has burned. The rule that everybody leaves one way, past one man, is working exactly as designed.

## The hand you are dealt

**You are Isaac Harris**, the partner who runs the shop floor of the Triangle Waist Company. You have leased the ninth loft of the Asch Building for nine or ten years, four of them before the firm added the eighth and tenth. Your partner Max Blanck handles the business side. Between the three lofts you employ roughly five hundred people, most of them young immigrant women; about three hundred sit at the machines on the ninth floor.

Each loft has two stair doors. The Greene Street door, at one corner, sits beside the freight elevators; on the ninth floor it is reached through a partition. The Washington Place door, at the opposite corner, sits beside the two passenger elevators. Both open inward. Only the Greene Street stair continues to the roof; the Washington Place stair stops at the tenth floor. An open iron fire escape hangs in the rear court.

The nightly rule is the firm's, and it is old: everyone leaves by Greene Street; a watchman on each floor looks into handbags; keys and watchmen are the province of Louis Alter, the elderly relative who opens and locks the lofts. The Washington Place stair door on the ninth is not part of the exit routine. It is kept closed. There is a key in its lock, tied to the knob with a string. In summer, when the loft is hot, the door is sometimes open during machine hours.

- **Objective:** keep three hundred operators productive and the goods in the building.
- **Clock:** any closing time. The rule is re-made every evening at 4:45 on Saturdays and later in the week.
- **Primary resource:** freedom of movement, spent to buy loss control and supervision.
- **Authority:** the exit rule, the watchmen, what the second door is for.
- **Constraint:** you did not build the doors, the stairs or the fire escape; the trade searches bags; no law requires more than one exit.

## Evidence ledger

**Known.** There have been five fires on the premises, two of them serious enough for insurance claims in 1902; the smaller ones were put out with pails. Cuttings pile under the cutting tables between rag collections; the dealer took away more than a ton in mid-January. Section 80 of the Labor Law says factory doors "shall not be locked, bolted or fastened during working hours." A state inspector who called in January 1910 marked the doors unlocked. The value of goods actually found on employees in the past year is, by your own later reckoning, ten or fifteen dollars, "not over $25." A hand-bag search at one door is common in the trade, and the judge who later tries you will say aloud that no law compels a factory to send its people out of more than one door.

**Reasonably inferable.** A rule that everyone leaves one way trains three hundred people to think of one door. A door whose release depends on a key being in the lock and someone turning it is not the same as an open door. Nobody on the ninth floor is assigned to that door at closing; the watchman stands at Greene Street.

**Possible.** That the second door will never be needed. That in an emergency the elevators, the roof stair and the fire escape will absorb whatever the Greene Street door cannot.

**Unknown.** How fast a cuttings fire on the eighth floor would reach the ninth. Whether the standpipe hose works—the building superintendent has examined it but never run water through it. Whether operators know the fire escape exists; its window is behind the examiners' tables. Whether the key will be in the door on the day.

**Unknowable.** What three hundred people will do with three or four minutes.

## The decision

Three arrangements are plausible in 1910. All are within your authority.

**A. Keep the search, make the second door a real exit.** Take the key out of the equation: the door opens from inside without one, or a named person on the floor owns it at closing and it is tried under load. The gain is an exit that exists whether or not anyone finds the key. The exposure is a second, unwatched way out of a loft you have been searching for years, and the small cost of a fitting or a person.

**B. Give up the single-exit search on the ninth floor.** Accept that a few waists a year will walk out, or control loss another way—counts, inspection at the tables, the shipping room. The gain is two exits in daily use, so people learn them. The exposure is theft you cannot measure and a rule your partner and your trade regard as normal.

**C. Keep the prevailing arrangement.** One supervised exit, second door closed and keyed, key on its string. It passes inspection, it costs nothing, it matches every shop you know, and there has been no fire in the loft since 1902 that pails could not handle.

Commit. Then name one observation that would make you change your answer. If nothing would—if the second door is a matter of principle either way—you hold a doctrine, not a decision.

## What happened

At 4:45 on Saturday, 25 March 1911, the quitting bell had just rung when a cutter on the eighth floor saw fire in the scraps under a table on the Greene Street side. Cutters threw pails of water. The first street alarm reached the fire houses at 4:45; the second at 4:48; the third at 4:55.

The eighth-floor timekeeper's telautograph ran to the tenth floor only. She wrote a message; nothing came back. She telephoned the tenth and told the stenographer there was a fire on the eighth—"Tell Mr. Blanck about it." She did not ask to be put through to the ninth. Ninth-floor witnesses learned of the fire by a scream, by smoke, or by seeing flame at the windows.

On the eighth, a machinist forced his way through operators packed against the Washington Place stair door, tried to turn the key that was always in it, found it would not turn, and pulled the door open against the crowd. On the tenth, Blanck opened the Washington Place door, met smoke, and took his two children to the roof, where students from the university next door were pulling people across.

On the ninth, roughly a dozen witnesses later swore they tried the Washington Place door and could not open it. Two defence witnesses swore they opened it that day; both looked into the stairwell, saw flame or smoke rising from the eighth, and turned back. Others reached the two passenger elevators, which made a few more trips—one woman "fell into the elevator, and a whole lot of more girls fell in on top of me"—until people began sliding down the cables and dropping into the shaft. Some crossed the loft to Greene Street and climbed to the roof. Some went over the tables to the fire escape; one said the flames there were too thick, another was thrown from it and left hanging. Some went out the windows. A fire company's life net caught jumpers "until the net broke"; the same company later took nineteen bodies from the top of a Washington Place elevator car.

A hundred and forty-six people died. Margaret Schwartz, a ninth-floor operator, was last seen at the Washington Place door and died of smoke. Harris and Blanck were tried for her manslaughter on the theory that a door locked during working hours was a misdemeanour and that her death followed from it. On 27 December 1911 the jury, instructed that it must find the door locked, the owners aware of it, and the lock the cause of her death, acquitted after less than two hours.

The result tells you what happened when the eighth-floor fire met that ninth-floor arrangement. It does not tell you the door was locked; that was litigated and remains disputed. Nor does it tell you the arrangement was reasonable because it was acquitted.

## The people who inherited it

The ninth floor received no telephone warning and no drill. Its people still made decisions.

Mary Bucelli tried the door, saw no key, decided not to follow a crowd she thought would be crushed, climbed on a table, broke a window and went out on the fire escape. Ida Nelson tried the door, went to the fire escape, found the flames too thick, crossed the loft to Greene Street and climbed to the tenth floor, where a "college boy" got her out. Lillian Weiner tried the door twice, went to a window to jump, saw the flames below, went back to the door, then knocked at the elevator until it came. May Levantini, a defence witness, turned the key on its string, opened the door, saw the eighth-floor girls running down through smoke and flame, and went back to the elevator, where the crowd was too great; she slid down the cable and was in bed for weeks. William Greenspan, another, opened the door, took flame in the face, crossed the burning loft and ran up the Greene Street stair to the roof. Fannie Selmanowitz got into a passenger car as it started, "rubbing against the walls going down the shaft," with room for "not even a pin" more.

Kate Alterman's account survives only in a researcher's notes of the lost second volume: she and Margaret Schwartz reached the door together after the Greene Street side was in flame; a man tried it, then Margaret, then Alterman "pulled the handle in and out, all ways"; Margaret went down on her knees. Alterman covered her own head with a coat and ran through the fire on the Greene Street side to the roof. Whether she rehearsed those words was the defence's whole cross-examination.

On the eighth floor Louis Brown, the machinist, was not responsible for the door. He decided the crowd against it was the problem, forced it back, and pulled. Dinah Lipschitz at her desk decided to warn the tenth—the only floor her instrument reached—and held the line for two minutes. Jacob Woll's company decided to run the net until it broke and then go up. None of these people set the exit rule. Each spent the margin it left them.

## Reconstruction

The earliest reversible and consequential decision was the exit rule: one supervised way out, the second stair door closed and keyed, its release dependent on a key being present and someone turning it, nobody on the ninth floor owning that door at closing. It was reversible any evening. It was consequential because it decided what three hundred people would think of first and what the second door would do when they reached it.

That is a decision about a control, not about a padlock. It stands whether or not the door was locked at 4:45. On the defence's best evidence the door was closed with the key in it—an exit that required a stranger to the routine to find a key on a string in smoke. On the People's evidence the key was not there. Either way, the release depended on something being present that the arrangement did not guarantee.

Later reversible decisions were smaller and less reversible: the timekeeper's call to the tenth and not the ninth; the summer habit of leaving the door open, which lapsed in winter; whoever last turned the key. They spent margin. They did not design it.

Decision quality, execution quality and outcome quality separate here. The execution of the rule was, on the record, ordinary: watchmen watched, keys hung on strings, an inspector found nothing wrong. The outcome was catastrophic. The question is whether the decision to make an exit depend on a key was poor before the fire, and the answer does not depend on the fire.

## Competing diagnoses

**A control that failed toward containment.** The exit rule bought loss control by making one door a habit and the other a keyed door. Under load, a keyed door is not an exit for anyone who does not find the key. This diagnosis holds even on the defence's account.

**Housekeeping and suppression.** More than a ton of cuttings had accumulated since January; the fire started in them and spread, in the prosecutor's word, "like tinder"; the house hose on the ninth-floor landing was found burned on its rack and had never been tested with water. On this diagnosis, exits are secondary: a fire that crosses a loft in minutes defeats most doors.

**Warning wired past the ninth floor.** The eighth could reach the tenth directly and the ninth only through it. The ninth learned by scream. Give the ninth two more minutes and every exit—elevators, roof stair, fire escape, door—carries more people. This is an execution-and-design failure that no door rule fixes.

**Building and code.** Doors opened inward on landings too small for outward swing; one stair to the roof; a fire escape ending on a drop ladder above a skylight in a walled court; a partition in front of the ninth-floor Greene Street door. The tenant did not build any of it and the state approved all of it.

**Reasonable practice, bad outcome.** The arrangement matched the trade, passed inspection, broke no law that required two exits, and had never mattered in nine years. On this diagnosis, Harris chose C the way most proprietors did and drew the fire most did not.

These do not exclude one another. Housekeeping and warning set how many minutes there were; the exit rule set what the second door was worth in those minutes.

## Counterfactual: a door that opens without a key

Change one thing: on the ninth floor the Washington Place stair door opens from inside without a key. Leave the cuttings, the hose, the telautograph, the partition, the fire escape and the crowd exactly as they were.

The later problem changes in kind at one point: an exit exists for whoever reaches it. Roughly a dozen people who testified they could not open the door either go down a stair less than three feet wide, already carrying the eighth floor, into a well that two witnesses say was full of flame and smoke—or they turn back as those two did. Some would have lived; some would have met the eighth-floor crowd or the smoke and not. The record cannot count them, and the chapter must not.

Everything else changes only in degree. The fire escape still ends above a skylight; the elevators still stop; the ninth still gets no call. Restoring the door does not restore the minutes. "They locked the door" is therefore an incomplete diagnosis even where it is true; "they made an exit depend on a key" is the decision, and it was theirs to reverse.

## Judgement

The best-supported diagnosis, with moderate confidence, is a control designed to fail toward containment: the firm bought loss control with an exit rule that made one door a habit and left the other dependent on a key and a person, without owning that door at closing or testing what it would do under load. This was poor decision quality before any fire, and it did not require foresight of this fire—only the question of what the second door was for.

The record cannot establish that the door was locked at 4:45, that the owners knew its condition that day, or how many of the ninth floor's dead would have lived had it opened. Nor can it establish how much of the loss to assign to housekeeping, warning and building against the door. Where the record is silent, the chapter is.

## How the story was remembered

The fire became "the owners locked the doors" and then became the reason for the New York Factory Investigating Commission and a generation of workplace law. Both memories are true as afterlives. Neither is evidence about the 1910 decision. The first flattens a litigated door into a fact; the second treats a reform as proof that the pre-reform arrangement was culpable, when its architects were acquitted by a jury told to look for knowledge and cause. This chapter uses the trial record precisely because it is messier than the memory.

## Where this stops being true

A controlled exit is defensible when the release is fail-safe, obvious under stress, tested under load and independent of finding a particular person or key.

- A watched single exit with a second door that opens on a push is a reasonable loss control; the failure mode is a door that opens only with a key.
- Where a second exit is itself dangerous—an unenclosed stair full of the floor below's smoke—opening it may only move the crowd; the two defence witnesses who opened this one turned back.
- Where the hazard is slow, a keyed door with a person assigned to it can be adequate. The condition is the person and the test, not the key.
- A proprietor who chose C in 1910 and never had a fire made a poor decision with a good outcome; the method allows "uncertain," but it does not upgrade the decision.

## Transfer

**What transfers.** A control you impose for your own protection—a lock, a permission, a single sign-off, a gate in a process—must fail toward escape for the people on the other side of it. Ask what the release depends on: if the answer is a key, a person or a permission that may not be present, the exit is nominal.

**What does not transfer.** 1911 building code, garment-trade loss control, and criminal-liability standards; the reader is not being asked to unlock every door.

**Solo-actionable version.** Find one control you own that could become someone else's blocked exit—physical, procedural or informational. Try its release without you and without your key. If it does not open, that is the decision.

## Field exercise

- **Observable moment:** the next time a control you own is bypassed, requested or complained about.
- **One question:** "If the person on the other side needed this to open now, without me and without the key, would it?"
- **Recording method:** one line per control—what it protects, what its release depends on, who owns it at the moment of need, last time it was tried under load.
- **Pass condition:** every control has a release that does not depend on locating one person or one key, and has been tried.
- **Review condition:** any control whose release is "someone will open it" goes to the owner with the exit named.
- **Classification:** decision / execution / uncertain, recorded before any incident is used to judge the control.
