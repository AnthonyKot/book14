Case 8 · Coalition Provisional Authority · Baghdad, May 2003

The order that was not obvious

In May 2003 a man who had been in Iraq for one week decided what to do with an army of about four hundred thousand men. He wrote down, before he decided, exactly what could go wrong. Then he did it anyway. That is not the story of a fool. It is the story of a hard decision — and this piece is about why it was hard, and about what you should do with it, which is not to pick an answer.

Where he was standing

Paul Bremer landed in Baghdad on 12 May 2003. Sixty-two, a career diplomat, no Arabic, no experience of running an occupation. Baghdad was being looted; ministries had been burned; about four thousand police were on duty for seven million people. Saddam's army had not surrendered so much as evaporated: soldiers had gone home, and their barracks had been stripped.

Four days after arriving he issued his first order, banning senior members of Saddam's party from public jobs. That same day, Iraqi political leaders — mostly from the Shi'a and Kurdish communities the old regime had persecuted — urged him to go further and deal with the army. He promised he would, within days.

On 19 May he sent a two-page memorandum to the Secretary of Defense. Attached to it were two things: a draft order abolishing the Ministry of Defence, the intelligence services and the armed forces (he called it Tab A), and a separate outline of who would be paid afterwards (Tab B). "I propose to issue the attached order," he wrote. "I could also implement the policies in Tab B."

The memorandum contains the sentence this whole piece turns on:

"There is obviously something that catches in the craw about paying anything to people who worked at the heart of Saddam's regime. Against this, however, we must balance the risks of serious discontent, increased terrorism, and much higher crime rates that may result if we cut off all military and security sector pensioners in a heavily militarized society."

And this one, a few lines later:

"There will be some delay in making these payments, because … we do not have employee rosters."

So: he saw the danger, and he knew he had no way yet to pay the men he was about to dismiss. He signed four days later.

Why it was not obvious

Here is what makes this a real decision and not a cautionary tale. Almost every consideration cut both ways.

The army had gone home — but not entirely. Nobody in Baghdad could point to an Iraqi division standing to attention. Bremer's advisers said, reasonably, that you cannot disband what has already disbanded itself. But across the country American brigade commanders had been quietly contacting Iraqi officers and reconstituting units, and Jay Garner — the man Bremer replaced — had arranged American money to pay 300,000 Iraqi soldiers to stand by. Two US generals were in a room with Iraqi generals who had volunteered to serve when Bremer's adviser told them: "No, you are not doing that."

The people who had suffered under the army wanted it gone — but not everyone. The Kurdish and Shi'a leaders, on the Coalition's account, would not accept a reconstituted version of Saddam's army, and they were the majority and the Coalition's partners. Yet Iraqis interviewed later said the army, unlike the hated police and secret services, "generated considerable sympathy and respect" and was seen as part of the identity of the state — even by many Shi'a.

Paying the officers stuck in the throat — but not paying them was dangerous. Bremer's own words. His payment plan said everyone below the rank of colonel would eventually get a payment and a pension; colonels and above would be presumed part of the regime and get nothing unless they proved otherwise. That is exactly the cohort with the training and the standing to organise.

Speed mattered — but so did the machinery. He had promised the Iraqi leaders a proclamation within days. He also had no list of names and no way to pay anyone. Money was not the problem; the mechanism was.

Whose decision was it? Later accounts disagree. One study says it was made in Washington before Bremer arrived. Colin Powell said nobody could tell who had decided it. But the memorandum is written in the first person, proposes rather than reports, and the order changed in Bremer's hands between the draft and the signature: the rule cutting off colonels moved from the separate payment outline into the order itself. He was inside the room where it was decided, whatever else is true.

Nobody standing where he stood had a clean option. That is the point.

The three doors

Strip it down. Two things were actually on the desk that week: does the old army cease to exist now, and do its officers stop being paid now?

ADissolve now, cut off the officers now.

One order, one day. Every regime force abolished, every rank cancelled, colonels and above unpaid until they prove they were not the regime, everyone else paid when the rosters exist.

You get: a clean, decisive act; the majority's leaders satisfied. You risk: 400,000 men sent home with a promise instead of a payment — the risk in the memo.

BDon't dissolve yet.

Let the American commanders keep recalling and vetting units, as they were already doing. Keep the men standing by on Garner's payroll. Learn who and where they are. Decide later what to abolish.

You get: a force, and names. You risk: being the occupier who brought back Saddam's army, in the eyes of the people whose cooperation you depend on.

CAbolish the regime's organs today; hold the army and the pay.

Dissolve the secret police, the Special Security Organization, the Republican Guard, the Fedayeen — nobody was arguing to keep those. Leave the regular army and its officers' pensions on the books for a second, dated decision, once you can actually pay people. This door was in the memorandum itself: Bremer had already kept three ministries out of the order for practical reasons, and he had written that Tab B was something he "could also" do — not something he must.

You get: most of the political signal at a fraction of the human bill. You risk: a contested line between "the army" and "the regime," and time you have said you will not take.

Do not choose. Read it again.

Most decision exercises ask you to pick. This one asks you not to — not yet.

Instead

Go back through the three doors and, for each one, imagine the country a year later, and three years later. Not the first week; anyone can see the first week. Ask:

  • Where are the four hundred thousand men in a year? Employed by whom, armed by whom, loyal to whom?
  • Where are the officers — ten thousand of them, on one British official's count? Do they have an income? Do they have a grievance? Do they have each other's phone numbers?
  • What does the new Iraqi army look like in three years — who trained it, who leads it, and where did those leaders serve before?
  • What do the Kurdish and Shi'a leaders do if the old army is kept? What do the Sunni communities do if it is not?
  • And the question the memorandum itself asks: what happens in "a heavily militarized society" when the pay stops and the machinery to restart it does not exist?

Notice that Door C does not make these questions go away; it changes their timing. Notice that Door B answers some of them and creates others. Notice that Door A, the one that was chosen, is not stupid — it is the door that answers today's question best and next year's worst.

That asymmetry — decisive today, expensive later, and the cost paid by people who were not in the room — is the shape this book is trying to teach you to see.

What happened

He signed Door A on 23 May 2003. Order No. 2: the ministries and forces dissolved, ranks cancelled, everyone dismissed backdated to 16 April; payments and pensions promised; colonels and above presumed disqualified from both.

Within days former soldiers were at the palace gates. On 26 May five thousand demonstrated in Baghdad, demanding their army back, their salaries, and a new government. In Mosul, Iraqi police shot a protester dead and a riot followed; eighteen American soldiers were wounded. About a month later — the sources disagree on exactly how long — a monthly stipend for former career soldiers was announced.

In August a New Iraqi Army was created on paper: forty thousand men, three divisions, two years, a general with five staff and a PowerPoint. That summer, when its builders asked for forty-five former Iraqi lieutenant colonels to help lead it, the officers refused to serve without their old rank and pay. Three-quarters of the new force, in the end, had served in the old one.

Four years later Bremer told Congress: "I stand by the decision not to recall the army … The mistake I made, and I admit it, is when we announced that we were going to build the new army, we should have immediately said we are also going to pay the officers from the old army. It took us a month to do that."

Whether this order "caused" the insurgency is argued to this day, by serious people, in both directions. This piece does not settle it and the full chapter does not either. What is not in dispute is smaller and sharper: the man who signed had written down the risk four days earlier, had written down that the machinery to contain it did not exist, and signed anyway — with the machinery still on "the next step."

The thing to take with you

You will never abolish an army. You will retire a system, close a team, end a contract, cancel a programme. When you do, the people it employed or served become your decision, not a side effect of it. The question this case leaves you with fits on one line, and it belongs on the same page as the signature:

On what date does everyone this displaces receive what they are owed — and does the machinery for that exist today?

If the honest answer is "the next step," you are standing where Bremer stood on 19 May. Read the memorandum again before you sign.
