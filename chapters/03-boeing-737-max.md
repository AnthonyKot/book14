# Transparent to the crew

*Boeing 737 MAX program · Everett and Seattle · 2016–2019 · Decision-forcing case*

On Wednesday, 30 March 2016, at eight in the morning, in a conference room at Boeing's Everett plant, the stability-and-control group briefs the leadership of the 737 MAX program. The slides are titled "737 MAX / Stall Characteristics – Mitigation." Two months into flight test the airplane is not stalling cleanly with the flaps up at low speed; the mitigation is an update to the control law of an existing function, not a change to the airframe. The general manager approves it. The chief project engineer approves it too.

At 11:16 the same morning the program's chief technical pilot emails the FAA's Aircraft Evaluation Group. Subject: "MCAS lives in both FCCs." He asks to delete the function from the Flight Crew Operations Manual and the differences tables, because it is "completely transparent to the flight crew and only operates WAY outside of the normal operating envelope." The FAA will agree, verbally, at a later meeting.

Nothing has gone wrong. The airplane has a fix for its stall demonstrations, and the pilots who fly it need not know why.

## The hand you are dealt

**You are Michael Teal**, Vice President and Chief Project Engineer of the 737 MAX since 2011. Keith Leverkuhn runs the program; you own the engineering.

The MAX is a re-engined 737: larger LEAP engines hung further forward and higher on an airframe whose ground clearance dates from 1967. The new nacelles produce a nose-up pitching moment at high angle of attack, so column force stops building the way Part 25 requires. The answer was the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System, an extension of speed trim that, past an angle-of-attack threshold with flaps up, runs the stabilizer nose-down. As designed it lived where a certification test pilot pulls a wind-up turn—high Mach, high g—and could move the stabilizer about six tenths of a degree.

- **Objective:** certify the MAX in March 2017 as the same type as the NG, with computer-based differences training and no simulator—Level B.
- **Clock:** the week of 30 March 2016, two months after first flight; FAA test pilots fly the airplane for the first time in April.
- **Primary resource:** commonality—the schedule and training hours you spend, or do not, to keep the MAX "the same airplane."
- **Authority:** your engineering approval, and what you condition it on. Certification plans, the AEG's training decision and the manual belong to other chains, but they answer to what engineering says the airplane is.
- **Constraint:** without MCAS the airplane does not meet the stall rules; the flight-test window is open now.

## Evidence ledger

**In the room.** The briefing: the airplane fails flaps-up stall characteristics at low speed; the mitigation is an update to the MCAS control law; a go-forward plan with an "FAA Communication Plan"—Boeing and FAA pilots to fly the revised software in the simulator in June and in the air in July. What else was on the table, the record does not say.

**In the building—your program's records, dated on or before today.** Coordination Sheet Rev. D, dated today, as the Inspector General later summarises it: activation, which used to require Mach 0.67 or more and elevated g, now runs from Mach 0.2 to 0.84; the maximum stabilizer command per activation rises from 0.55° (the FAA's figure) or about 0.6° (the sheet's) to 2.5°. As built, the function reads one angle-of-attack vane at a time, and the version that flies will command again five seconds after the crew trims against it. Rev. D carries a functional hazard assessment: uncommanded MCAS is assumed to be recognised as a runaway stabilizer, a memory-item procedure every 737 pilot has; a "typical" reaction of about four seconds—FAA guidance: one to recognise, three to react—makes it Hazardous, and a statistical credit for how rarely a flight is outside normal parameters brings it to Major, which requires no redundancy. The same sheet, since October 2015, has said: "A slow reaction time scenario (>10 seconds) found the failure to be catastrophic due to the inability to arrest the airplane overspeed." Behind that sentence, a November 2012 simulator note: one Boeing pilot reached the cutout switches in about four seconds, the other took more than ten, and the engineer asked whether "we should step up to catastrophic." A December 2015 email from an engineer who is also an FAA-authorized representative: "Are we vulnerable to single AOA sensor failures with the MCAS implementation or is there some checking that occurs?" Whether any of this has reached you, the record does not show; you could call for all of it this week.

**Reasonably inferable.** For approving as presented: the FAA's guidance sets the reaction time; the runaway procedure exists; the class is Major and the vane's service record runs to hundreds of millions of hours. Against it: a function that once fired only where no line pilot goes by accident will now fire wherever a vane reads high, and the crews who must catch it in four seconds are, this morning, the subject of a request that they not be told it exists.

**Possible.** That the AEG, told what "transparent" now covers, would ask for a simulator; that a fresh look would leave the class where it is.

**Unknown.** How often a vane fails high in service, and what else lights up in the cockpit when it does; whether a line crew reads shaker, speed disagree and turning wheel as one problem or three.

**Unknowable.** How the first crew to meet it will read it.

## The decision

Your signature is the act; three ways to give or withhold it are within your authority this week.

**A. Approve the aerodynamic fix; condition it.** Sign the mitigation, and require the safety assessment re-issued for the airplane you will actually ship—low-speed table, 2.5°, one vane, repeat logic, the flight-deck effects of a vane failing high—and the owners of the certification plans, the AEG relationship and the manual to resolve what follows before the function is called transparent. Cost: weeks in a schedule counted in days, and a real risk to Level B.

**B. Send it back.** Ask for a configuration that does not rest on one vane or on the speed of the crew's response: two-sensor comparison, one activation per event, authority the column can always hold—the shape the same function has on the KC-46 tanker. The record gives no cost; a new control law qualified in flight test a year from certification is not weeks.

**C. Approve as presented.** The high-speed case was analysed and remains the critical one; the crew procedure exists; the class is Major. This is what the record shows the program did.

Commit. Then name one observation that would change your answer. If nothing would—if "the crew will catch it" is true by definition—you hold a doctrine, not a decision.

## What happened

Boeing approved as presented. The hazard classes were re-checked in the motion simulator after the change and did not move; but full authority was simulated only to the 0.6° high-speed limit, never to 2.5° at low speed, and repeated activation was not simulated—engineers and pilots "deemed" it no worse than one, in a June 2016 meeting email that never entered the certification record. The safety assessment stayed at Rev. C; the certification plans were not updated; Rev. D's coordination documents were not deliverables and never reached FAA certification engineers.

In August 2016 the FAA approved Level B differences training with nothing on MCAS; in November it delegated the stabilizer certification plan to Boeing's own designees on an FAA engineer's recommendation. The type certificate followed in March 2017.

**Lion Air.** On 28 October 2018 a replacement left vane, mis-calibrated by about 21° in repair, was fitted to PK-LQP in Denpasar. On the evening flight to Jakarta the stick shaker ran from rotation for 96 minutes. The captain gave the airplane to the first officer, ran the airspeed-unreliable memory items and—when the trim kept running nose-down—treated it as a runaway stabilizer and cut the switches. Three minutes forty seconds; twelve activations. They landed on manual trim. The log mentioned speed and altitude disagreements; not the shaker, the procedure or the cutout.

Next morning flight 610 lifted off with the same vane. Stick shaker at rotation. When the flaps came up the stabilizer ran nose-down for ten seconds; the crew trimmed against it; five seconds later it ran again. Over six minutes it ran more than twenty times and the crew countered each until the last few, while the first officer searched for a checklist that fit. At 23:30:48 UTC the captain handed over; the first officer's trim inputs were shorter than the nose-down runs. The recorder stopped at 23:31:54 with MCAS running. 189 people. On 7 November the FAA's emergency directive expanded the runaway-stabilizer procedure for erroneous AOA; neither it nor Boeing's bulletin named MCAS.

**Ethiopian.** On 10 March 2019 the left vane of ET-AVJ went to 74.5° seconds after liftoff from Addis Ababa—the Ethiopian bureau says an electrical fault, the NTSB says a bird. Stick shaker; speed disagree; then, flaps up, nine seconds of nose-down trim. The captain trimmed back, partly; five seconds later it ran again; the crew used the cutout. Out of trim at high speed with the electric trim dead, the first officer could not turn the manual wheel—about 2.5° of mistrim at 340 knots, some forty revolutions—while the captain held column forces calculated above 90 pounds. Thirty-two seconds before impact the switches were back on; five seconds later MCAS ran again. 157 people. This crew had the emergency procedure. The NTSB, dissenting, asked that the probable cause add "the flight crew's inadequate use of manual electric trim and management of thrust": the throttles stayed at takeoff power, and electric nose-up trim before the cutout was minimal.

The FAA grounded the fleet on 13 March 2019. It returned in November 2020 with MCAS reading both vanes, firing once per event, and limited so the column alone can hold level flight.

## The people who inherited it

The FAA Aircraft Evaluation Group representative who said yes to "transparent" believing MCAS was still a 0.55° high-speed function; the FAA engineer who recommended delegating the stabilizer plan while "not aware of the increased MCAS range from 0.55 degrees to 2.5 degrees." Each held a document describing an airplane no longer being built.

The Lion Air 043 captain, who used the third pilot to watch the path and the radio while he and the first officer worked the problem, then wrote up the disagreements and not the shaker or the cutout. The Jakarta engineer who read that log, flushed the pitot-static system, cleaned a connector, and released the airplane. The 610 crew, both with over five thousand hours—trained on a differences course without the system, in an airplane whose AOA DISAGREE alert was tied to an optional display they did not have—a defect Boeing had found in 2017 and deferred to 2020. Twenty times their choice was to trim against it; the choice they could not make was to name it.

The Ethiopian 302 captain, 8,122 hours but 103 on the MAX; the first officer, 361 hours in total, five weeks into line flying. They had the procedure and ran it; then, with the wheel not moving, the captain put the switches back—against the directive, as the Ethiopian bureau notes, and as far as the record shows because the force was beyond what two men could hold. The bureau says the wheel could not be turned; the NTSB says thrust and electric trim, used as the procedure assumed, would have kept the forces manageable.

## Reconstruction

The decision of 30 March 2016 was not to build a dangerous system. It was to change what a system could do without changing what anyone had said about it—and, the same day, to ask that the crew not be told. Three documents held the airplane's story: the coordination sheet, with the new envelope and the ten-second sentence; the safety assessment, with the old envelope and the four-second assumption; the manual, soon with nothing. Each was coherent. They stopped describing the same airplane the day the approval and the email coincided—coordinated or not, the House could not say; it found no evidence the technical pilot had been told of the redesign.

Teal told committee staff in 2020 that when he approved the redesign he did not know MCAS ran on one vane, could fire repeatedly, or that the ten-second result existed. That is later testimony, not a 2016 document. Take it at face value and the case is worse, not better: three facts in the program's own files never reached the signature, and the signature did not ask.

Boeing told the committee it had informed the FAA "on numerous occasions" that MCAS's range had expanded; the Inspector General found that FAA flight-test personnel knew while the AEG and the certification engineers did not. Both can be true: the new envelope reached the part of the FAA that flies airplanes, never the part that decides what pilots are taught. And the four seconds: the Joint Authorities Technical Review found no study behind them. Three flights, three responses, none the assumed one.

Why this day? November 2012 produced a data point but no gate; June 2013 fixed the language for a 0.6° high-speed function. On 30 March 2016 the wider authority, the old recovery assumption and an identifiable approval met, a year before certification, everything still reversible.

## Competing diagnoses

1. **The assessment that was not re-opened**—this chapter's pivot. The function changed; the safety case, the certification plans and the manual did not; the FAA never got the sheet with the ten-second sentence.
2. **Single-sensor architecture, regardless of paperwork.** An updated assessment might have re-affirmed Major; Boeing's simulator re-check did. What killed was one vane driving repeated full-authority trim. The return-to-service fix is this diagnosis made law.
3. **Certification governance.** 91 of 91 plans delegated to Boeing's own designees; 42 FAA overseers for some 1,500 of them; an AEG that decided training on an email and a verbal yes. On this account 30 March was a symptom; the failure is the delegation regime.
4. **Execution in service.** A mis-calibrated vane, an installation test that may not have been done, a log entry that left out the shaker; on 302, throttles at takeoff thrust and an airplane past 340 knots. KNKT lists the first three among nine unranked factors; the NTSB's dissent makes the last a cause, not a footnote.
5. **The training clause.** A million dollars an airplane; in 2017, "HOLDING FIRM THAT THERE WILL NOT BE ANY TYPE OF SIMULATOR TRAINING" and "jedi mind tricked this fools." Documented; not in the room on 30 March.

## Counterfactual: the same airplane, and a crew that has been told

Keep hardware and software exactly as certified. Put MCAS back in the FCOM and the differences course, and give every crew one simulator session with a runaway that starts with a stick shaker at rotation. What changes?

The record bounds it from both ends without closing it. Flight 043 recovered *without* the name, with a third pilot and three minutes forty seconds; the assumption was four. Flight 302 had the emergency procedure—not the name; the directive did not use it—cut the switches, and did not recover; the bureau says the wheel could not then be turned at 340 knots, the NTSB says electric trim and thrust management would have kept the airplane controllable. Disclosure alone was not sufficient on 302; disclosure with a representative simulator session was never tested; whether it changes either accident in kind or only in degree the record cannot settle. What it settles: in all three the function fired repeatedly from one vane, and a page in a manual restores neither margin.

## Judgement

Course A was available on 30 March 2016 at the price of schedule and a risk to Level B—the course the chief project engineer later called ordinary—"the certification plans would have to be updated." Course C was chosen. It accepted a materially changed recovery assumption without evidence at the configuration to be shipped, and let the regulator be asked, the same day, to make the function invisible on a description that had stopped being true. Confidence: high that the safety case should have been re-issued and the AEG briefed on the new airplane; moderate that re-issuing it would have moved the class, because Boeing's own re-check said no; no claim that A alone would have saved either flight. What A buys is the question, asked at the right configuration with the right alarms, on a record the regulator can see.

## How the story was remembered

As greed with a countdown clock, or as foreign pilots who could not fly. The record supports neither alone: an engineering authority who signed a redesign without three facts in his own program's files; a regulator whose flying arm and training arm did not talk; two cockpits different in almost every particular except the function nobody had named. The Ethiopian and American investigators still disagree in print about the vane and the crew; the KNKT names nine factors and refuses to rank them.

## Where this stops being true

A design may lean on the operator to recover from a failure when four things hold: the failure is recognisable as itself; the time available has been demonstrated at the configuration actually shipped, with the alarms that will actually sound; the recovery is trained; and the failure lands inside a model the operator already holds. The runaway procedure met the third. Rev. D failed the first and fourth on the day it was approved; the second was never attempted at low speed. Where all four hold—an autothrottle disconnect a crew sees on every simulator check—the assumption is honest and this chapter does not apply.

## Transfer

**What transfers.** Every plan that says "the user will notice and correct it" is a safety component with a response time in it. Treat it as a part: it needs evidence at the configuration you ship, and re-evidence when the configuration changes. Ask, for the version in front of you and not the one that was analysed: what cue does the operator get, how many seconds, and where did they learn what the cue means?

**What does not transfer.** Part 25 certification law, the cockpit, the physics of a trim wheel at 340 knots.

**Solo-actionable version.** Take one thing you own that relies on someone catching a failure. Write the cue, the seconds, and the place they learned it, for the version running now. If any of the three is inherited from an earlier version, that is the decision.

## Field exercise

- **Observable moment:** your next three design or change reviews.
- **One question:** "What here assumes a human will catch a failure—and was that tested at this configuration, with these alarms?"
- **Recording method:** one line per assumption—cue, tested response window at this configuration, where the operator learned it; marked evidenced, inherited or assumed.
- **Pass condition:** every assumption is evidenced.
- **Review condition:** any assumption marked inherited or assumed goes back to its owner before approval; re-run whenever configuration, cues or training change; when the change review and the "no training needed" decision share a date, stop and ask why.
- **Classification:** decision / execution / uncertain, recorded before any incident is used to judge the assumption.

Before somebody has to be brave, where is the last reversible decision?

*This chapter teaches the reader to ask what a recovery assumption rests on. Challenger asks whether the proof at a go/no-go boundary was ever made; Triangle asks whether a safeguard was designed to open for the person who needs it; this chapter asks whether the person expected to save the machine in four seconds was told the machine existed.*
