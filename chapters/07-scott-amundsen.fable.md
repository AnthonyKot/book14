# Four ways to move a sledge

*Scott and Amundsen · 1909–1912 · Dual decision-forcing case*

In September 1909 a telegram reaches Norway: the North Pole is claimed. Roald
Amundsen has spent years raising money for an Arctic drift expedition in the
*Fram*. By his own account he decides within moments to turn the whole
enterprise around and go south — and to tell almost nobody but his brother.
The plan he writes that month at Bundefjord ends with a date for the return
from the polar journey: January 25.

In London, Robert Falcon Scott is assembling a very different machine. The
*Terra Nova* will carry a hut, laboratories and a large scientific staff. For
transport across the Great Ice Barrier: three experimental motor sledges,
ponies in stalls under the forecastle, thirty-three sledging dogs collected
across Siberia, and men trained to haul. One officer on the muster roll is
listed simply as "Ski Expert."

Two expeditions will cross the same ice toward the same point. Neither knows
the other's design yet. Nothing has gone wrong. The decision that
concerns this chapter has to be made now, in offices and harbours — because
the transport architecture hardens with every purchase, every hire and every
month of training not spent.

## The hand you are dealt — twice

The same objective, two sets of constraints. Play both hands before reading
on.

**Hand one.** You are the leader of a British national expedition. Your
objective is a broad scientific programme *and* the Pole; both are public
commitments, and the Pole is the one the newspapers and subscribers will
count. Your base must be McMurdo Sound, where the science is. Your own polar
experience says dogs disappointed you, and ponies, which served Shackleton to
the foot of the great glacier, are known quantities. <!-- CHECK: Scott's
Discovery-era dog experience and its interpretation require a source beyond
Amundsen's characterization; Scott's own "Voyage of the Discovery" not
inspected --> Your officers and men are naval; man-hauling is a practised,
respected technique among them. Money is short and public.

**Hand two.** You are the leader of a small private expedition. Your stated
objective, in your own later words, makes no apology: the Pole is "the main
object." Your crew is picked for cold-weather craft — several expert
dog-drivers, one champion skier. You have no established base, no obligation
to any coastline, and, because the destination is secret, no public debate
about your choices. You have less money and, as you yourself will write of
your rival, less Antarctic experience.

- **Clock:** months, not hours. Ships must sail in mid-1910 to catch the
  season.
- **Primary resource:** specialization and preparation time, spent to buy
  transport margin.
- **Authority:** transport system, personnel, training programme.
- **Constraint:** you cannot buy competence after departure. Whatever your
  team cannot already do well, the ice will not teach them gently.

## Evidence ledger, 1909–1910

**Known.**

- Shackleton's party, using ponies to the foot of the Beardmore Glacier and
  man-hauling beyond, planted a flag at 88° 23′ S in January 1909 — about a
  hundred geographical miles short — and came back starving but alive.
  <!-- CHECK: "came back starving" — Shackleton's "Heart of the Antarctic" not
  inspected; near-starvation on return widely reported but needs the source -->
- Dogs driven by skilled teams have made the fastest polar journeys on
  record; Norwegian and Inuit practice treats dogs-plus-ski as a single
  integrated craft.
- Ponies cannot be fed off the land and sink deep where a dog runs on the
  crust. A dog that drops into a crevasse can be pulled out by its harness; a
  pony that drops through a snow-bridge may be gone. Dogs can be fed on dogs;
  a pony cannot be fed on ponies.
- Motor sledges have never operated in Antarctica: a genuine experiment with
  genuine promise — if they work, they move depot loads without consuming
  fodder.
- British skiing and dog-driving skill is uneven; one Norwegian ski expert
  and one dog specialist have been hired to cover the gap. Several of
  Amundsen's men have travelled on ski behind dogs all their lives.

**Reasonably inferable.**

- A transport system is not the sum of its components. Each mode needs its
  own handlers, food, pace and season, and each interface between modes is a
  place where a failure changes who carries the weight.
- Ponies fix the calendar. They cannot march in early-spring cold, so a pony
  expedition starts late, returns late, and crosses the Barrier homeward as
  the season turns.

**Possible.**

- Motors may revolutionize Barrier haulage — or fail in ways no one can repair
  on the ice.
- Dogs may be unable to climb the crevassed glaciers to the plateau. This is
  what most of Scott's party believes; Shackleton's account is read as
  supporting it.

**Unknown.**

- Barrier weather in autumn; depot reliability with untested animals; whether
  any route besides the Beardmore exists at all.

**Unknowable.**

- What any of these men privately weighed before committing. The record shows
  decisions and stated reasons, not minds.

## The decision

Three designs are on the table; each can be argued by a competent planner in
1909.

**A. Specialize.** Dogs and skis, nothing else. Buy the best animals
available, hire drivers as carefully as you would hire a navigator, spend the
winter perfecting gear, plan the animals' deaths into the food ledger. The
gain is a single system operated at expert level. The exposure: if dogs
cannot climb to the plateau, or the specialized crew fails, there is no
second system — and if you cannot recruit real drivers, option A collapses
into a bad version of itself.

**B. Diversify.** Motors, ponies, dogs and men, staged: machines and animals
spend themselves crossing the Barrier, men haul the summit. No single failure
sinks the plan, and the pony stage is proven by Shackleton's near-success.
The exposure is quieter: four competences to maintain, three interfaces to
manage, a schedule fixed by the most fragile animal — and if the earlier
stages underperform, the reserve system, human muscle, absorbs whatever load
remains.

**C. Change the objective instead of the transport.** Narrow the goal or
enlarge the margins: fewer parallel commitments, more depot mass laid
earlier, a shorter first-season reach. This trades glory and science for
slack, and must be defended to backers who were promised both.

Commit to one — in each hand. Then write down the piece of evidence that
would reverse you. If nothing would, you hold a doctrine, not a decision.

## What happened

Amundsen's *Fram* wintered on the Barrier itself, at the Bay of Whales — a
full degree of latitude closer to the Pole than McMurdo, on ice most
contemporaries believed to be afloat. Through the autumn of 1911 his depot
parties laid three tons of supplies at 80°, 81° and 82° S, marking each cache
with lines of numbered bamboo flags running transversely for miles, so a
party could miss the depot and still find it. On 8 September 1911 he started
for the Pole too early: eight men, ninety dogs, temperatures in the −50s and
−70s Fahrenheit. The party retreated, losing several dogs and, in the
published account, "one or two frostbitten heels." <!-- CHECK: the retreat
was disorderly and produced a serious dispute with Hjalmar Johansen, who was
then excluded from the polar party; this is absent from Amundsen's published
text and needs a documented source --> On 20 October five men, four sledges
and fifty-two dogs left again. At the top of an unscouted glacier route
through the mountains they shot twenty-four dogs as planned, at a camp they
named the Butcher's Shop, and fed dogs and men on the carcasses. They reached
the Pole on 14 December 1911 and were back at Framheim on 25 January 1912
with eleven dogs — the calendar date written into the plan at Bundefjord in
September 1909.

Scott's third motor sledge went through the sea ice during unloading and
sank. The two survivors ran "well, but not very well" and failed early in the
southern journey. <!-- CHECK: motors abandoned roughly 50 miles out; distance
and date need Vol. I or Vol. II confirmation --> The autumn depot journey
cost pony losses that Scott's last message would cite as forcing a late
start, and its main depot — One Ton — was laid at 79° 28½′ S rather than 80°:
"It is a pity we couldn't get to 80°," Scott wrote, "but as it is we shall
have a good leg up for next year." The polar march went by stages as
designed: ponies shot at the glacier foot, dogs sent back, men hauling from
the lower Beardmore onward. On 3 January 1912, within 150 miles of the goal,
Scott reorganized a four-man unit into five, sending back Teddy Evans, Lashly
and Crean; Bowers came on without skis, pulling on foot between skiers. The
five reached the Pole on 17 January to find the Norwegian tent. All five died
on the return — Edgar Evans below the glacier, Oates walking out of the tent
into a blizzard, Scott, Wilson and Bowers eleven miles from One Ton Depot,
short of food and critically short of fuel, in March cold Scott's final
message called a surprise no one could have expected.

The outcome is evidence about the world, not by itself a verdict on either
design. For that we go back under the horizon.

## The people who inherited it

The polar party inherited the plan's arithmetic, but they were not
passengers. Scott's journal records decisions still open to them: at One Ton
the previous February he had declined to push failing ponies further south
when Oates urged it — "It would have been ridiculous to have worked some out
this year as the Soldier wished" — a genuine judgement call between animal
capital and depot distance, whose cost was paid thirteen months later by five
men camped eleven miles short. On 3 January the five-man decision added a
fifth mouth to a four-man tent, cooker and ration system, and put one man on
foot among skiers. These were choices, made by people, inside the envelope
the architecture had left them.

The support parties inherited the other half. The last returning party —
Evans, Lashly, Crean — hauled home; Evans collapsed with scurvy and survived
because his companions refused the arithmetic, one staying with him while the
other walked the final stretch alone for help. <!-- CHECK: Lashly and Crean's
rescue of Lt. Evans, including Crean's solo march, is from Vol. II /
participant accounts not inspected here --> At base, the dog teams that
doctrine had spared were sent south to meet the polar party; the instructions
for how far and to what purpose had changed hands and wording several times,
and the teams turned back from One Ton in deteriorating weather.
<!-- CHECK: the sequence of orders to Meares, Atkinson and Cherry-Garrard
requires participant accounts (Cherry-Garrard) and later scholarship; not
established from inspected sources --> Scott's diary from the Barrier records
the other side of that failed rendezvous: "We hope against hope that the dogs
have been to Mt. Hooper; then we might pull through."

On the Norwegian side the plan spent animals instead of margins: the dogs
inherited the Butcher's Shop, on schedule. Prestrud, frostbitten in the
September retreat, inherited exclusion from the polar journey and command of
a survey eastward. The false start distributed its costs downhill too; the
published account smooths this, and the smoothing is itself part of the
record's texture.

## Reconstruction

The earliest reversible and consequential decision sits in 1909–1910: the
transport architecture and, inseparably, the competence purchased to run it.
After the ships sailed, depot radius, start date, party size and the load men
would carry when other modes stopped were all constrained by that design.

- **Decision quality.** Scott's diversified system was not reckless; it
  followed his own experience, Shackleton's partial success, and a real
  second objective. Its weakness was subtler: it diversified *modes* without
  diversifying *mastery*. The party was expert at exactly one of its four
  systems — man-hauling — and that is the system the design guaranteed would
  carry the final load. Amundsen concentrated risk on one system operated at
  the highest available level, with margins — depot mass, transverse marking,
  planned attrition, schedule slack — built around it.
- **Execution quality.** Amundsen's execution included one serious
  self-inflicted emergency — the September start, driven by fear of the
  rival's calendar — corrected at the cost of dogs, heels and trust. Scott's
  execution of his own plan was mostly competent and in places excellent; the
  shortfalls that mattered (depot latitude, fuel at the caches, the change to
  five) were marginal choices inside a design that had already spent its
  margins.
- **Outcome quality.** One party dead eleven miles from its depot; one party
  home to the day. Outcome is the loudest of the three measures and the least
  informative about the other two.

## Competing diagnoses

**System-design failure.** Each non-human mode was operated at middling
competence, so each underperformed, and every shortfall rolled downhill onto
the only system with no fallback beneath it — the men. By this reading the
March disaster was the bill for decisions taken in London offices and Siberian
horse markets. <!-- CHECK: ponies purchased in Siberia by
Meares/Bruce, and criticism of their quality, needs a participant or
scholarly source -->

**Reasonable risk, bad outcome.** This reading deserves full weight. A
similar plan had returned Shackleton alive from further out on thinner
margins. The polar party was on schedule to survive until the Barrier leg,
where it met prolonged cold that Scott's last message declared beyond
reasonable expectation — a claim later meteorological work has treated as
partly defensible rather than as excuse-making. <!-- CHECK: modern analysis
of February–March 1912 Barrier temperatures (e.g. Solomon) not inspected;
"unusually severe" must not be asserted as settled --> Add a fuel shortage at
the depots "for which I cannot account," an early death that slowed the party
through the glacier, and eleven final miles, and the case that ordinary luck
returns this party alive is genuinely open.

**The late reversible choice.** A third diagnosis says the decisive act was
not the 1909 architecture but the 3 January reorganization: five men on a
four-man logistic base, one off skis, and the cascade forced in rations and
fuel. This is the strongest candidate for "latest reversible decision" even
if the architecture explains more.

These do not exclude one another. Architecture set the size of the margin;
weather, illness and late choices spent it.

## Counterfactual: give Scott more dogs

Add dogs to the *Terra Nova* manifest — double them, triple them — and change
nothing else. No expert drivers beyond the one hired; no party-wide ski
fluency; no redesigned depot line; no change to the scientific programme, the
McMurdo base, or the doctrine, stated by Scott to his assembled shore party in
May 1911, that the Pole "can best be solved by relying on the ponies and man
haulage" because "everyone seems to distrust the dogs when it comes to glacier
and summit."

The later problem changes in degree, not in kind. More dogs driven at the
skill level available move somewhat more depot mass somewhat faster; perhaps
One Ton reaches 80°, perhaps the start improves by days. But the calendar is
still fixed by ponies, the glacier and plateau are still walked on doctrine,
the final load still lands on men, and the return still crosses the Barrier
in late season. Dogs were never the missing part. The missing part was the
operating system around dogs — drivers, skiing, route, season, depot
geometry, a doctrine willing to use animals to the end — and that is
precisely what this counterfactual is forbidden to smuggle in. "He should
have used dogs" is not a lesson; it is a label stuck on a system difference.

## Judgement

Best-supported diagnosis: a design-level decision failure on the British side
— diversification of equipment without diversification of competence, the
human fallback bearing unbounded transferred load — *interacting with* severe
late-season conditions and marginal execution shortfalls a wider margin would
likely have absorbed. Confidence: moderate. The record cannot establish that
a well-run mixed system fails on that ice in that season; it can establish
that this mixed system left its people the smallest margins at the furthest
point, and that its rival returned on the day written into a plan two years
old. The 1912 weather's true rarity, the fuel-loss mechanism, and what the
unedited diaries would alter remain open.

A caution on the record itself: the Scott journal quoted everywhere,
including here, is the text published in 1913, prepared after his death. The
manuscript diaries are held at the British Library, and the published text is
not automatically identical to them. No specific distortion is claimed here;
the uncertainty is stated because it belongs on the ledger.

## Where this stops being true

Specialization won on the Barrier because the Barrier is a single environment
one method could dominate, and because Amundsen could actually staff that
method with masters. Reverse either condition and the recommendation reverses.

- Where terrain, mission or season genuinely vary, a portfolio beats a
  monoculture — *if* each instrument is competently crewed and the interfaces
  are planned. Shackleton's mixed system, run hard, nearly sufficed.
- Where expert capacity in the single method cannot be hired, a disciplined
  mixed system at good-enough level may beat a specialized system run badly.
- And specialization carries its own concentrated bets. Amundsen wintered on
  ice widely believed to be afloat and took an unscouted route to the plateau;
  had either bet failed, this chapter would interrogate the man who removed
  every fallback. His system was not safe. It was coherent, and its risks
  were chosen, priced and rehearsed.

## Transfer

**What transfers.** A backup is a capability, not a possession.
Diversification reduces risk only when each mode is operated at survival
grade and the load transferred by one mode's failure is computed and
assigned. If the fallback plan is "the people will carry it," the people are
not a reserve system; they are the crumple zone.

**What does not transfer.** Polar travel technique, the animal ethics of
1911, and expedition-era acceptance of mortal risk. Do not map dogs and
ponies onto vendors and staff as if the ice were an office.

**Solo-actionable version.** For every "backup" in a plan you touch, write
three fields: who operates it, when they last did under realistic load, and
what weight lands on which humans if the primary fails. A blank field is the
finding.

## Field exercise

Take the next plan you can inspect and select its three claimed backups.

- **Observable moment:** the meeting or document where a backup is invoked as
  a reason the plan is safe.
- **One question:** "Who has run this backup under realistic load, and when?"
- **Recording method:** a three-column note per backup — operator, last
  realistic test, load transferred on primary failure.
- **Pass condition:** all three backups have a named, practised operator and a
  bounded transferred load.
- **Review condition:** any backup whose operator answer is "the team,
  generally" is reclassified from *backup* to *load on people*, and the plan
  re-read with that word in place.
- **Classification:** decision / execution / uncertain — assigned without
  letting the eventual outcome rewrite the ledger.
