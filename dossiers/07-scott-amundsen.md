# Pilot 07 — Scott versus Amundsen

Status: **DRAFTED AND ADVERSARIALLY REVIEWED — canonical chapter written; reader/domain review pending**

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**  
   Scott’s mixed transport system—motor sledges, ponies, dogs and eventual man-hauling—could
   move depots and a polar party safely across varied terrain while supporting scientific and
   geographic objectives. Amundsen believed a specialized ski-and-dog system, supported by
   dense depots and accumulated polar practice, offered the stronger margin.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**  
   Candidate decision point: transport-system design and training before departure, when
   Scott could concentrate more heavily on dogs and skiing or retain a mixed system. A later
   operational point may prove more reconstructable after source review.

3. **What was known at that moment**  
   Dogs had proved effective in polar travel when handled by skilled teams; ponies had serious
   environmental limitations but had been used by Shackleton; motor sledges were experimental;
   British expedition members varied in ski and dog-handling experience; Scott pursued more
   than a simple race.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**  
   Weather and surface conditions; actual motor reliability; pony survival; depot visibility;
   illness and nutritional effects; how Amundsen’s route and timing would perform; the exact
   contribution of each factor to the deaths.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**  
   A. Specialize in dogs and skis, investing heavily in training and accepting planned animal
   attrition.  
   B. Retain mixed transport to diversify failure modes and support scientific work.  
   C. Narrow the objective or enlarge schedule and supply margins rather than imitate either
   surface method.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**  
   The polar party and supporting parties who hauled loads, laid depots, turned back and
   attempted to sustain or meet the returning team.

7. **What they had to do**  
   As mechanical and animal transport ended, men became the transport system. The final party
   had to manage load, food, fuel, navigation, illness, weather and depot intervals while
   returning from the pole.

8. **How the story was later remembered**  
   Scott became a national hero of endurance; later revision made him an incompetent foil to
   Amundsen’s professionalism. Both versions can flatten the evidence. The chapter should
   compare systems and margins, not national character.

9. **The unique decision question**  
   **In a staged plan, who or what absorbs each shortfall—and what lies beneath the terminal
   stage?**

10. **Primary sources available**  
    Scott’s journals, expedition diaries and photographs through the Scott Polar Research
    Institute; Amundsen’s complete *The South Pole* in the public domain. Anchors:
    <https://www.spri.cam.ac.uk/museum/diaries/> and
    <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4229>

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Dual decision-forcing comparison.
- **Role:** Expedition leader designing the transport system before departure; the reader
  receives the same objective twice under Scott’s and Amundsen’s actual constraints.
- **Objective:** Reach the South Pole and return the party alive; scientific objectives must
  be stated separately rather than retrofitted as excuses.
- **Decision point:** Before equipment, personnel training and animal procurement make the
  transport architecture difficult to change.
- **Primary resource:** Specialization and preparation time spent to gain transport margin.
- **Counterfactual:** Give Scott more dogs without changing training, doctrine, depot plan or
  expedition objectives. If little changes, “he should have used dogs” is incoherent; the
  transferable variable is the complete operating system.
- **Competing diagnosis:** Exceptional weather, fuel loss, illness and depot execution were
  sufficient to defeat a reasonable plan; or the final five-man party decision, not initial
  transport design, was the decisive reversible choice.
- **Boundary:** Mixed methods are valuable when each is competent, interfaces are planned and
  failure of one does not transfer an impossible load to people.
- **What transfers:** Trace every shortfall through the handoffs and identify the final
  absorber before people are forced to supply unbounded margin.
- **What does not transfer:** Polar travel technique, animal ethics and expedition-era risk.
- **Solo action:** Draw one plan's stages and record the promised output, minimum output and
  named absorber at every handoff.
- **Exercise:** Inspect one plan with three dependent stages. Pass when every handoff has a
  bounded gap and a named absorber with demonstrated capacity.
- **Nearest overlap:** Challenger. Challenger asks whether a protection shares the primary
  system's uncertainty; Scott–Amundsen asks where accumulated shortfalls finally land.
