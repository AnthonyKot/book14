# Pilot 08 — Iraq, 2003

Status: **READY FOR PROSE — decision locked 2026-08-18 against the archived CPA record**

Locked to **CPA Order No. 2, "Dissolution of Entities," 19–23 May 2003**, seat: L. Paul
Bremer, Administrator of the Coalition Provisional Authority. This replaces the 2026-08-13
preliminary scoping, which took the seat to be the UK Prime Minister at the pre-invasion
authorization and was cut for being unplayably broad. Narrowing pass and adjudication:

Sources of record (all in `sources/08-iraq-2003/`, hashed in `sources/MANIFEST.md`): the
signed order (`Order2`); Bremer's memorandum to Rumsfeld of 19 May 2003 with Tabs A and B
(`Bremer`); Iraq Inquiry Section 12.1, "Security Sector Reform" (`Chilcot`); SIGIR, *Hard

**What this chapter does not decide.** Whether invading Iraq was lawful, wise or justified;
whether de-Ba'athification (Order No. 1, 16 May) was right; whether the war "caused" the
insurgency. None of those has to be settled to play this choice, and the chapter says so in
its own voice rather than leaving the reader to assume a verdict.

## What the record locks, and what it leaves open

**Locked (stated by two or more of the archived sources, or by a primary document in hand).**

- **The order and its date.** CPA Order No. 2 dissolved the Ministries of Defence,
  Information and State for Military Affairs, the intelligence services, the armed forces and
  the regime paramilitaries; cancelled all military ranks; released conscripts; and dismissed
  every employee retroactive to 16 April 2003, the date of Franks' Freedom Message (Order2
  casi p. 1–2 / l. 20–60; Chilcot p. 32 / l. 1801–1830). Signed by Bremer, 23 May 2003; every
  page footer reads `CPA/ORD/23 May 2003/02`.
- **The order paid people, and cut off the officers.** Sections 3(4) and 3(5) provide a
  termination payment and continuing pensions, including to war widows and disabled veterans.
  Section 3(6) then deems *any person holding the rank of Colonel or above* a Senior Party
  Member, and no Senior Party Member receives either payment, subject to a rebuttal procedure
  "to be prescribed" (Order2 casi p. 2 / l. 62–84). Both popular accounts are wrong: the order
  did not strip everyone of pay, and it did not leave the officer corps whole. It cut off
  precisely the cohort holding command authority and the ability to organise. Tebbit put the
  same point to the Inquiry: the Americans said "it had to happen anyway, because the army had
  disappeared. Well, true, but not the 10,000 officers. So I didn't quite buy that" (Chilcot
  p. 34 / l. 1932).
- **Bremer proposed the order; he was not handed it.** His memorandum of 19 May: "In the
  coming days I propose to issue the attached order (Tab A)… I could also implement the
  policies in Tab B on payments" (Bremer p. 1 / l. 7–11). It closes by asking Rumsfeld to
  concur "in principal," after which "the next step is development of payment plans" (Bremer
  p. 2 / l. 65). cc Wolfowitz, Feith, Haynes (Bremer p. 2 / l. 90).
- **The risk was named in writing, by the decision-maker, four days before signature.** "There
  is obviously something that catches in the craw about paying anything to people who worked
  at the heart of Saddam's regime. Against this, however, we must balance the risks of serious
  discontent, increased terrorism, and much higher crime rates that may result if we cut off
  all military and security sector pensioners in a heavily militarized society" (Bremer p. 2 /
  l. 57–61). This is a decision case, not an ignorance case.
- **The scale was known.** "The order will affect large numbers of people: There were some
  400,000 employees of the MOD alone" (Bremer p. 1 / l. 33). SIGIR puts the total at some
  500,000 men across all the dissolved forces (SIGIR p. 96 / l. 3636).
- **The payment mechanism did not exist, and Bremer knew it.** "There will be some delay in
  making these payments, because… we do not have employee rosters, or contacts with
  administrative officials of the dissolved entities" (Bremer p. 2 / l. 67–73). The
  intelligence community was still assembling the data.
- **The alternative was live, funded and in motion.** CFLCC's Phase IV plan, ECLIPSE II,
  assumed the bulk of the Iraqi Army would be recalled (OnPoint p. 93 / l. 4416; p. 113 /
  l. 5407). CFLCC staff had opened negotiations with senior Iraqi officers about the army's
  role in restoring order (OnPoint p. 113 / l. 5427). Garner "began working to bring the old
  Iraqi Army units back and had even arranged for the US Government to pay the salaries of
  300,000 soldiers, 12,000 police, and up to 2 million public servants" (OnPoint p. 113 /
  l. 5443). Across the country, US brigade commanders "had contacted Iraqi military leaders
  and, through them, had begun to reconstitute army units" (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3679). CENTCOM's
  Phase IV plan, approved by the President, anticipated using the army (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3672).
- **The order was drafted to foreclose that alternative, explicitly.** Tab B, the payment
  policy Bremer attached to his own memorandum: former employees "can be hired as individuals
  by Coalition military or civil authorities, for example as security guards. They are not
  hired as reconstituted old units, as soldiers, or as part of the future Iraqi military"
  (Bremer p. 6 / l. 254–257). *Author inference, marked as such:* a clause that specifically
  bars hiring "as reconstituted old units" reads as a response to something being reconstituted.
  The record establishes the clause and establishes the reconstitution; it does not establish
  that one was written because of the other.
- **The objections reached the room before the signature.** "Key U.S. generals in Iraq
  expressed concerns about the order before it was issued" (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3672). When
  McKiernan and Webster briefed Slocombe on CFLCC's plans, in a meeting with Iraqi generals
  who had volunteered to serve, Slocombe told them "No, you are not doing that" (OnPoint
  p. 115 / l. 5504). Tim Cross: the decision was taken "against all advice from Garner and
  myself" (Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1928). Garner protested when he heard (OnPoint p. 100 / l. 4816).
- **The UK was told and did not object.** Bremer raised dissolving the MOD and the security
  organisations with Sawers on 12 May; Sawers expressed no concerns and called it a "good
  dynamic start" (Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1940). Slocombe saw Hoon in London on 13 May and
  emphasised "a new Army, rather than a reconstituted version of the old"; "There is nothing
  in the record to suggest that Mr Hoon or those accompanying him raised any concerns"
  (Chilcot p. 34–35 / l. 1945–1986). The Chiefs of Staff met on 14 May and recorded none
  either (Chilcot p. 35 / l. 2007), though Boyce later told the Inquiry he had "laid down"
  that the army should not be disbanded (Chilcot p. 35 / l. 2013).
- **What happened next.** Within days, crowds of former soldiers at the CPA gates demanding
  salaries and pensions; on 26 May, 5,000 officers and soldiers demonstrated in Baghdad,
  demanding recall of the army and payment (OnPoint p. 117 / l. 5635). In Mosul, Iraqi police
  shot a protester, a riot followed, eighteen of Petraeus's soldiers were wounded (SIGIR p. 98
  / l. 3709). Bremer then moved on pay, in at least two steps, and the sources date them
  differently: SIGIR has him modifying the policy "ten days later," announcing that the CPA
  would pay salaries and pensions to members of the disbanded army (SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3717);
  *On Point II* dates the monthly stipend scheme for career soldiers to 23 June, four weeks
  after the order (OnPoint p. 117 / l. 5644); Bremer told Congress that once commanders came to
  him, "I immediately agreed," but that "it took us a month" (Hearing p. 186 / l. 3757–3762).
  These are probably two measures rather than a contradiction, but the corpus does not pin the
  first one to a date. The chapter should say about a month to the stipend, show the two steps,
  and not manufacture a precision the sources do not carry.
- **Bremer owns it, on the record, in 2007.** Asked whether he regretted it: "there really was
  no army to disband. It certainly was a mistake to use the word, disband… I stand by the
  decision not to recall the army and to rebuild the army from the bottom up. The mistake I
  made, and I admit it, is when we announced that we were going to build the new army, we
  should have immediately said we are also going to pay the officers from the old army. It
  took us a month to do that" (Hearing p. 185–186 / l. 3726–3762). Note the tension with the
  signed text: Bremer adds "those pensions continued all the way through our time there. They
  were always paid" (Hearing p. 186 / l. 3765), whereas Section 3(6) as signed barred pensions
  to colonels and above. Later practice may have diverged from the instrument; the chapter
  must not let the 2007 sentence overwrite the 2003 clause.

**Open (disputed or unresolved in the record).**

- **Who decided, and where.** RAND: "the decision to disband the Iraqi armed forces was… made
  in Washington… in early May 2003, before the deployment of Ambassador L Paul Bremer to
  Baghdad" (Chilcot p. 33 / l. 1889). Powell: "There was no meeting on it; there was no, 'Gee,
  is this a good idea?' You couldn't even tell who had decided it" (Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1923;
  SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3653). Against that: Feith records being briefed by Bremer and Slocombe on
  9 May about "their plan" (Chilcot p. 34 / l. 1919); the 19 May memorandum is written in the
  first person as a proposal; the NSC had not vetted it, and Powell first learned of it at the
  principals' meeting of 22 May, the day before signature (SIGIR p. 96 / l. 3645). The honest
  statement is that the policy formed inside a small OSD–CPA circle in early May, that Bremer
  was inside it rather than downstream of it, and that no document in this corpus records an
  instruction removing his discretion. NSPD-24, which would define the formal scope of his
  authority, is still classified.
- **Whether the order changed anything on the ground, or ratified a fact.** Slocombe: "We
  didn't disband the army. The army disbanded itself… all the facilities were trashed"
  (Chilcot p. 33 / l. 1868). Riley: the CPA "was left with no choice" (Chilcot p. 33 /
  l. 1880). Against that: the brigade-level reconstitution already under way, and Tebbit's
  officers. Unresolved, and the chapter keeps it unresolved.
- **How much of the insurgency this explains.** Manning: these policies "added to the
  difficulties" (Chilcot p. 35 / l. 2017); Hoon: "disbanding the army fuelled the insurgency
  in a way that made it much harder to contain" (Chilcot p. 36 / l. 2045). Against them,
  Sawers: "I don't think it is credible to lay the insurgency, the roots of the insurgency, in
  the decision to disband the army… It may, in some areas, have compounded it, but it wasn't
  the fundamental reason behind it" (Chilcot p. 36 / l. 2057). Petraeus: "tens of thousands, if
  not hundreds of thousands, of additional enemies" (SIGIR p. 98 / l. 3721). Hughes, who was
  working with the old military in April and May, calls it a "strategic blunder" (OnPoint
  p. 118 / l. 5689). A separate and less contested effect: Reith told the Inquiry that without
  the order "there was still some structure there we could have built on, whereas, as it was,
  we really had to start from scratch" (Chilcot p. 36 / l. 2067). The chapter reports the range
  and does not adjudicate it.
- **Whether Order No. 2 can be separated from Order No. 1.** Order No. 1 excluded the top four
  levels of the Ba'ath Party from public employment; the two orders interact through
  Section 3(6)'s definition of a Senior Party Member. The chapter must handle them as a pair
  at the point where they overlap, and not credit Order No. 2 with Order No. 1's effects.

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**
   That Saddam's instruments of repression could be abolished outright and replaced from the
   bottom up, and that the political gain — proving to the Shi'a and Kurdish majority that the
   old regime was not coming back — would outweigh the temporary hardship of the men put out
   of work, because most of them had already gone home anyway.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**
   Signing Order No. 2 as drafted on 23 May 2003, rather than deferring it while the recall
   and vetting work already running under CFLCC, ORHA and the brigade commanders was either
   used or formally closed, and rather than sequencing the payment mechanism ahead of the
   dismissal. The window is 19–23 May: the memorandum proposing it is dated 19 May, Powell
   hears of it on 22 May, the signature is on 23 May. Before the signature, all three options
   below were open. After it, ranks were cancelled, employment was terminated retroactively to
   16 April, and the counterparties the US commanders had been negotiating with no longer held
   any position to negotiate from.

3. **What was known at that moment (19–23 May 2003)**
   In the room: that some 400,000 MOD employees were affected; that there were no rosters and
   no payment mechanism, so payments would be delayed; that cutting off military pensioners in
   a heavily militarised society risked "serious discontent, increased terrorism, and much
   higher crime rates"; that the new force would take two and a half years to reach three
   divisions (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3684); that Section 3(6) would cut off every officer of colonel
   rank and above. In the building: that CFLCC and CENTCOM had planned on recalling the army,
   that Garner had funded a payroll for 300,000 soldiers, that brigade commanders had begun
   reconstituting units, and that Garner, Cross, McKiernan and Webster objected. The chapter
   uses the 737 MAX device here and marks which is which.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**
   Whether recalled units would obey the Coalition or fracture; whether Kurdish and Shi'a
   leaders would accept a reconstituted army, and what they would do if they did not; how many
   soldiers could actually be found and paid without rosters; whether an army whose barracks
   had been looted could be reassembled at all; whether unemployed soldiers would take up arms
   or wait.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**
   A. **Sign as drafted.** Gain: a single decisive act, credibility with the Kurdish and Shi'a
   leaders, who — on the CPA's account — objected to any reconstituted version of Saddam's army
   (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3670), and no surviving instrument of the regime. Exposed
   risk: 400,000 dismissals with no payment mechanism and a two-and-a-half-year gap before a
   replacement force exists.
   B. **Defer and continue recall-and-vetting.** Let the CFLCC negotiations and the brigade-
   level reconstitution run, vet unit by unit, dissolve later from a position where you know
   who and where the men are. Gain: an existing security instrument and a payroll that already
   has names on it. Exposed risk: the Coalition is seen to restore Saddam's army, the Kurds and
   Shi'a react, and the vetting never catches up with the reconstitution.
   C. **Narrow the order to the regime security organs.** Dissolve the intelligence services,
   the Special Security Organization, the Republican Guard and the paramilitaries — the
   entities nobody proposed keeping — and hold the regular army, the MOD and the officer
   pensions pending a separate decision. Gain: the political signal at a fraction of the human
   bill. Exposed risk: the line between the regular army and the regime's instruments is
   contested, and drawing it takes time nobody thinks they have.
   Trigger for reconsideration, in all three: the absence of a rosters-and-payments plan. It is
   named in the decision-maker's own memorandum and is the one thing entirely inside his
   control.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**
   The dismissed — some 400,000 by Bremer's count, some 500,000 by SIGIR's — and among them
   every officer of colonel rank and above, cut off from pension and termination payment by
   Section 3(6). Their families. The Iraqi generals who had volunteered to serve and had been
   given guidance by McKiernan and Webster. The brigade commanders who had contacted Iraqi
   military leaders and begun reconstituting units, and now had to unwind that — over leaflets
   the Coalition had dropped before the invasion promising no reprisals to soldiers who laid
   down their arms (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3675). Petraeus's soldiers in Mosul. The
   Iraqi policemen who fired on the protest. The CPA staff who had to build a payment system
   for people who no longer had employers.

7. **What they had to do**
   For the dismissed officers: support a family with no salary, no pension, no termination
   payment and a rebuttal procedure that did not yet exist, in a country where the weapons
   caches were unguarded and the men knew where they were. For the US commanders: tell the
   Iraqi officers they had recruited that the arrangement was void, hold ground with no local
   partner, and absorb the demonstrations. For the CPA: assemble, from intelligence
   reporting, a payroll for half a million people who had been dismissed a month earlier.

8. **How the story was later remembered**
   As the single blunder that created the insurgency, or — Bremer's version — as a decision not
   to recall an army that no longer existed, marred only by a month's delay in announcing
   payments. The chapter refuses both. It holds the causal question open and puts the weight on
   the sequencing: dismissal before mechanism.

9. **The unique decision question**
   **May you abolish an institution before the thing that must perform its function — including
   the mechanism for paying the people you are dismissing — exists?**

10. **Primary sources available**
    The signed order; Bremer's own memorandum with the draft order and payment policy; the
    Chilcot record of the UK thread; three official histories and one congressional hearing.
    No Iraqi-authored primary source; the chapter states this.

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Decision-forcing on the signature, then a reconstruction of the alternative that
  was running and the weeks between dismissal and payment.
- **Role:** L. Paul Bremer, Administrator of the CPA, in the days 19–23 May 2003. The reader
  gets what was in the room (his own memorandum, Tab B, the Kurdish and Shi'a position) and
  what was in the building (ECLIPSE II, Garner's funded payroll, the brigade-level
  reconstitution, the objections of Garner, Cross, McKiernan and Webster), and the chapter
  marks the difference — the 737 MAX device, reused deliberately.
- **Objective:** Prove to the Iraqi majority that Saddam's instruments of repression are gone
  for good, and stand up a force that can hold the country, without provoking the collapse
  your own memorandum warns about.
- **Clock:** four days. The memorandum goes to Rumsfeld on 19 May; Powell hears of it at the
  principals' meeting on 22 May; the signature is on 23 May.
- **Authority:** the Administrator may promulgate binding orders in occupied Iraq. What he may
  not decide alone: troop levels, the funding of a new force, or the pace at which the payment
  machinery can be built.
- **Primary resource:** political credibility with the Shi'a and Kurdish majority, spent to buy
  a clean break — against security capacity and 400,000 households' income.
- **Counterfactual:** keep Order No. 2 exactly as written, with the same dissolution and the
  same Section 3(6), but do not sign until the payment mechanism is running and the officers'
  rebuttal procedure is published. Assume no better people and no more troops. If the
  demonstrations, the Mosul riot and the recruitment pool change only in degree, the causal
  weight sits with the dissolution itself and the chapter says so; if they change in kind, the
  weight sits with sequencing. Bound the counterfactual with the fact that the rosters did not
  exist: "wait for the mechanism" may mean waiting months, which is itself a decision with a
  cost.
- **Competing diagnoses:** (i) sequencing — abolition before the replacement and the payment
  mechanism existed (this chapter's pivot); (ii) the dissolution itself, independent of timing
  — Manning, Hoon, Petraeus; (iii) ratification, not decision — Slocombe and Riley: the army
  had already gone, and the order changed labels rather than capacity; (iv) diffusion of
  ownership — Powell's "you couldn't even tell who had decided it," a decision taken outside
  any process that would have surfaced the objections; (v) Order No. 1, not Order No. 2, via
  the Senior Party Member definition; (vi) distributed causes — the insurgency had several
  roots and this was one input.
- **Boundary:** abolishing an institution before its replacement exists is right when the
  institution is itself the active danger, when the people it employs are not the ones you are
  relying on for security, or when delay would let it entrench. The intelligence services and
  the Special Republican Guard arguably meet that test in May 2003. The regular army and its
  pension roll do not, on this record — which is why Option C is in the choice screen and not
  in the footnotes.
- **What transfers:** when you dissolve something, the people you dismiss become a decision of
  yours, not a side effect. Their payment mechanism belongs on the same page as the
  dissolution, dated.
- **What does not transfer:** occupation law, the authority of a viceroy, war powers, and the
  legality of the invasion.
- **Solo action:** for any shutdown, cancellation or reorganisation you sign, write on the
  same page who is dismissed, what they are owed, on what date it reaches them, and what
  happens if the mechanism is not ready.
- **Exercise:** observable moment — the next time a team, contract, service or system is
  retired. One question: on what date does everyone displaced by this receive what they are
  owed, and does that machinery exist today? Record the answer and the date beside the
  decision. Pass when the date is earlier than the shutdown date or explicitly accepted as
  later, in writing, by the person signing. Review at the shutdown date. Classification:
  decision.
- **Nearest overlap:** Bay of Pigs (a decision whose second phase had no owner) and 737 MAX
  (in the room versus in the building). Distinguisher: here the decision-maker wrote the risk
  down himself, four days early, and signed anyway — this is the book's clearest case of a
  named risk accepted rather than a risk unseen.

## Kill conditions, tested at lock

The three conditions carried over from `extract/codex-iraq-narrowing.md` §10, each checked
against the archived corpus on 2026-08-18:

1. *No contemporaneous record that Bremer held the recall alternative before signature.* **Not
   met.** Tab B of his own 19 May memorandum forecloses hiring "as reconstituted old units"
   (Bremer p. 6 / l. 254–257); SIGIR records that key US generals in Iraq "expressed concerns
   about the order before it was issued" (SIGIR p. 97 / l. 3672); Slocombe was briefed on
   CFLCC's plans in person and refused them (OnPoint p. 115 / l. 5504).
2. *Washington's instruction removed his discretion.* **Not established.** RAND's claim stands
   in the chapter as a competing account (Chilcot p. 33 / l. 1889), but the 19 May memorandum
   is a proposal seeking concurrence, Feith records it as "their plan," and NSPD-24 is
   classified. The chapter presents the ownership dispute rather than resolving it.
3. *Order No. 2's effect cannot be separated from Order No. 1 and self-demobilization.*
   **Partly true, and handled.** The chapter's pivot is sequencing, not total causation, so it
   does not need to attribute the insurgency. Where the two orders interlock — Section 3(6) —
   they are treated as a pair.
