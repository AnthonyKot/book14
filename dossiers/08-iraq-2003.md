# Pilot 08 — Iraq, 2003

Status: **PRELIMINARY — evidence excellent, scope dangerous; cut unless narrowed**

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**  
   Coalition forces could remove Saddam Hussein’s regime quickly and transfer authority to a
   manageable Iraqi political order without a prolonged, resource-intensive occupation.
   Different institutions held different versions of this promise; the chapter must not
   invent one unified belief.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**  
   Candidate pilot point: late 2002/early 2003 authorization of military action while post-
   conflict objectives, authority and resources remained insufficiently reconciled. This is
   too broad until the inquiry record yields one meeting, role and concrete choice.

3. **What was known at that moment**  
   Regime removal could produce administrative collapse, disorder and humanitarian needs;
   multiple departments had conducted planning; the US would dominate post-conflict capacity;
   the Iraqi state and society contained political, ethnic and institutional fractures; the
   evidence about prohibited weapons contained significant uncertainty.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**  
   The scale and duration of Iraqi resistance; how state institutions and military units
   would behave after regime collapse; regional intervention; the legitimacy and capacity of
   successor authorities; actual weapons holdings; required troop and reconstruction levels.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**  
   A. Invade on the proposed timetable and accept adaptive post-conflict planning.  
   B. Delay commitment until objectives, authority, force requirements and contingencies are
   jointly approved.  
   C. Pursue containment, inspections and coercive diplomacy rather than regime removal.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**  
   Iraqi civilians and public servants, coalition troops and junior commanders, aid workers,
   local leaders and newly created governing bodies operating amid insecurity and institutional
   breakdown.

7. **What they had to do**  
   Maintain security, essential services and political relationships with unclear authority,
   inadequate information and resources designed primarily for major combat rather than the
   post-conflict system.

8. **How the story was later remembered**  
   As intelligence failure, deception, inadequate troop numbers, disbandment/de-Ba’athification,
   poor execution, or an illegitimate war that no execution could redeem. The chapter cannot
   contain all diagnoses; it must choose one decision and state the moral and legal questions
   it does not resolve.

9. **The unique decision question**  
   **May you authorize the first phase when success depends on a second phase whose owner,
   resources and acceptable end state are unresolved?**

10. **Primary sources available**  
    The seven-volume Iraq Inquiry, evidence submissions, witness transcripts, declassified
    documents and parliamentary records. Anchor:
    <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-report-of-the-iraq-inquiry>

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Diagnostic reconstruction unless a sufficiently sharp pre-invasion decision point
  is found.
- **Role:** UK Prime Minister or senior cabinet decision-maker; exact meeting and authority
  pending evidence lock.
- **Objective:** Defined by contemporaneous documents, not later summary—disarmament, regime
  change, alliance management and post-conflict stability must not be conflated.
- **Decision point:** The last UK decision at which delaying participation remained politically
  and operationally possible and post-conflict deficiencies were visible.
- **Primary resource:** Political and schedule commitment spent to gain preparation and exit
  optionality.
- **Counterfactual:** Supply a fully staffed post-conflict authority and additional preparation
  time without assuming Iraqi compliance or perfect security. Test whether this changes the
  problem in kind or merely reduces the severity of a strategically unsound objective.
- **Competing diagnosis:** The decisive failure was the invasion decision itself, not planning;
  another is that post-conflict breakdown was an execution failure produced by later US
  decisions beyond the selected UK actor’s control.
- **Boundary:** Acting before the second phase is fully specified can be justified in a true
  emergency when delay is costlier, the next-phase owner is explicit and minimum viable
  capacity exists.
- **What transfers:** Do not approve phase one using benefits that exist only if an unowned
  phase two succeeds.
- **What does not transfer:** War powers, intelligence access, alliance politics, legality and
  occupation governance.
- **Solo action:** Put the owner, resources and stop condition for phase two on the phase-one
  approval page.
- **Exercise:** For three commitments, write who owns the state after “success.” Pass when the
  owner, minimum resources and failure trigger are explicit before approval.
- **Nearest overlap:** Bay of Pigs. Bay of Pigs tests removal of an operational prerequisite;
  Iraq tests approval while the post-success system is unresolved.
