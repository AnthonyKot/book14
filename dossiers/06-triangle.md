## What the record locks, and what it leaves open

**Locked (undisputed at trial).**
- The firm ran three lofts (8th, 9th, 10th) at Washington Place and Greene Street; roughly 200 on
  the 8th, 300 on the 9th, 60 on the 10th (Bostwick summation p. 2154 / l. 60165). Harris had held
  the 9th loft alone for about four years before adding the 8th and 10th (p. 1846 / l. 54393).
- Each loft had two stair doors: Greene Street (next to the freight elevators, behind a
  partition on the 9th) and Washington Place (next to the passenger elevators). The Washington
  Place stair stopped at the 10th; only the Greene Street stair reached the roof (Whiskeman
  p. 45 / l. 1668). Doors opened inward; a fire escape hung in the rear court and ended above a
  skylight (notes pp. 883–887 / l. 27816–27854).
- The everyday exit routine: employees left by the Greene Street side, past a watchman who
  looked into handbags; the Washington Place stair door was not part of the nightly exit
  (Gullo p. 364 / l. 14849; Monick p. 488 / l. 19927; L. Weiner p. 640 / l. 26042; Wexler, 8th
  watchman, notes p. 1029 / l. 28213). Keys and watchmen were Louis Alter's department (Harris
  p. 1853 / l. 54670).
- Even the defence's account of the 9th-floor Washington Place door is a *closed door with a
  key in the lock, tied to the knob by a string* (Levantini p. 1293 / l. 32097; S. Bernstein
  p. 1519 / l. 41162; Rapp p. 1601 / l. 44460; Alter p. 1753 / l. 50665). Nobody testified that it
  was a free-swinging exit.
- Theft was the stated purpose of the single-exit search. Harris: six girls arrested about
  three years earlier; the value of goods actually found taken in the year before the fire
  "would not exceed" $25 (p. 1868 / l. 55237–55270). Crain admitted this "as bearing upon the
  question as to whether or not these defendants did not have a like purpose in having but one
  exit instead of two" (p. 1870 / l. 55321), and stated that "there is no provision of law that
  compels factory owners to have their employees go out of more than one door" (l. 55342).
- Section 80 of the Labor Law: doors "shall not be locked, bolted or fastened during working
  hours" (charge p. 2190 / l. 61247). A Labor Department inspection card of 6 January 1910
  recorded the doors as unlocked during working hours (Williams p. 1219 / l. 29646–29682).
- Five earlier fires on the premises, two with insurance claims in 1902 (Harris pp. 1860–1862 /
  l. 54894–55024). Cuttings accumulated under the tables; the rag dealer's last removal was
  15 January 1911, 2,252 lb (Levy pp. 246–253 / l. 9923–10273). The building superintendent had
  never tested the standpipe hose with water (Stern pp. 1247–1255 / l. 30270–30634). Witnesses
  who were asked said there had been no fire drills; Crain later struck those references
  (p. 1212 / l. 28927).
- Warning ran 8th → 10th, not 8th → 9th: the timekeeper's telautograph went only to the 10th
  and got no answer; her telephone call reached Mary Alter on the 10th; she did not ask for the
  9th (Lipschitz pp. 1806–1810 / l. 52722–52899). Ninth-floor witnesses learned by scream, smoke
  or flame (Rosenfeld p. 1704 / l. 48692).
- Fire alarm 4:45 p.m., second alarm 4:48, third 4:55 (Donohue pp. 81–83 / l. 3148–3250). The
  bell for quitting had just rung.
- Verdict: not guilty, jury out 2:55–4:45 p.m., 27 December 1911 (p. 2204 / l. 61563).

**Open (litigated, unresolved).**
- Whether the 9th-floor Washington Place door was locked at 4:45 on 25 March 1911. Roughly a
  dozen People's witnesses tried it and could not open it (Bucelli, Gullo, Nelson, Monick,
  K. Weiner, L. Weiner, Mayers, Walker, Brenman, S. Bernstein-operator; Alterman and Gartman in
  notes). Defence witnesses Levantini and Greenspan say they opened it that day; many others say
  it was never locked in daily use. A lock with the bolt shot was found on the Washington Place
  side on 10 April; the experts disagreed whether heat could shoot a bolt (notes pp. 1904–1996).
- Whether the owners knew its condition that day (the charge made knowledge an element).
- How many minutes the 9th floor had. No witness times it.

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**  
   A three-loft garment factory could control theft and supervise 500 people by routing everyone
   out one door past a watchman, while the second stair door stayed closed and keyed, and the
   building's other systems—stairs, elevators, fire escape—would serve if a fire ever came.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**  
   The standing exit rule itself: one supervised exit per loft, the second stair door kept closed
   with its release dependent on a key being present and someone turning it, no one on the 9th
   floor charged with making that way passable, and no test of what the second door would do
   under load. This is a routine, not an act on the day; it could be changed any evening. It is
   the decision regardless of who turned the key on 25 March.

3. **What was known at that moment (1909 – March 1911)**  
   Five fires on the premises; a ton of cuttings between rag collections; 300 people on a floor
   whose only rehearsed exit was behind a partition at one corner; a fire escape few operators
   knew existed and whose window was obstructed by tables (notes p. 691 / l. 27457); a statute
   forbidding locked doors during working hours; an inspector who checked "unlocked"; theft
   found worth about $25 a year; hand-bag inspection common in the trade.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**  
   Whether the second door would be needed; how fast a cuttings fire on the 8th would reach the
   9th; whether the elevators would keep running; whether the key would be in the lock; whether
   anyone would think of the Washington Place stair when the habit was Greene Street.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**  
   A. Keep the search, but make the second door a real exit: no key needed from inside, or a
   person on each floor whose duty is that door at closing; test it.  
   B. Give up the single-exit search on the 9th and rely on other loss controls (Blanck, per
   Steuer's recital of his testimony, used both stairs and said it was his duty to see the doors
   were not locked—summation p. 2126 / l. 59203; his own words survive only in notes).  
   C. Keep the prevailing arrangement: it complied with inspection, cost nothing, and matched
   the trade.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**  
   About 300 people on the 9th floor with no telephone warning; the 8th-floor machinist who
   pulled the 8th door open; the two Washington Place elevator cars and whoever ran them; the
   firefighters whose nets broke; Blanck's children on the 10th.

7. **What they had to do**  
   Choose among a Greene Street door reached through a partition and, by several accounts,
   already in flame; two passenger elevators; a fire escape reached by climbing over tables;
   a closed door that some could not open and two say they did; windows. These options must be
   reconstructed by floor and by minute, and the 8th/9th/10th differences kept distinct.

8. **How the story was later remembered**  
   As "the owners locked the doors" and as the fire that made the New York Factory
   Investigating Commission and modern workplace law. The trial record is messier: the door was
   litigated and the jury acquitted. The chapter must keep the dispute and refuse to let the
   afterlife decide it.

9. **The unique decision question**  
   **May an organization impose a control whose release depends on a key, a person or a
   permission—when its failure removes somebody else's last exit?**

10. **Primary sources available**  
    The complete surviving trial transcript (local, hashed); Cornell Kheel Center archive for
    context and photographs (rights item by item).

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Decision-forcing on the routine (role: proprietor), then a downstream escape map.
- **Role:** Isaac Harris, the partner who ran the shop floor and had held the 9th loft longest;
  the decision is the firm's standing exit rule for the 9th floor.
- **Objective:** Keep 300 operators productive and the goods in the building.
- **Clock:** any closing time before 25 March 1911; the rule is re-made every evening.
- **Decision point:** the last routine evening on which the second stair door's release could
  have been made independent of a key and a person.
- **Primary resource:** freedom of movement spent to buy loss control and supervision.
- **Counterfactual:** make the 9th-floor Washington Place door open from inside without a key,
  leaving fire, cuttings, elevators, fire escape, partition and warning unchanged. Test what
  changes in kind (an exit that exists) and what only in degree (crowding on a stair about two feet nine inches wide (Whiskeman p. 52 / l. 2014),
  smoke in the well, the 8th-floor door already in use).
- **Competing diagnoses:** (i) the control that failed toward containment; (ii) combustible
  housekeeping and no tested suppression; (iii) a warning system wired past the 9th floor;
  (iv) building design and code (inward doors, one stair to roof, fire escape to a skylight);
  (v) reasonable contemporary practice with a bad outcome.
- **Boundary:** a controlled exit is defensible when release is fail-safe, obvious under stress,
  tested under load and cannot depend on locating a particular person or key.
- **What transfers:** a security or process control must fail toward escape, not containment;
  the owner of a control is not the person who needs it to fail open.
- **What does not transfer:** 1911 code, garment production, criminal liability standards.
- **Solo action:** find the control you own that could become someone else's blocked exit—
  physical, procedural or informational—and try its release without you and without its key.
- **Exercise:** in three routines, mark the last independent exit and whether it depends on
  permission, a key or one person; pass when each dependency has a tested release path.
- **Nearest overlap:** BP Texas City (degraded safeguards around occupied space) and Titanic
  (accepted practice, inspection passed, bad outcome). Distinguisher: Triangle's safeguard was
  not degraded; it was designed to depend on a key.
