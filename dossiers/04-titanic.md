# Pilot 04 — Titanic

Status: **PRELIMINARY — strong inquiry record; contemporary-practice boundary is central**

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**  
   In clear weather, a fast liner could maintain course and speed near reported ice and rely
   on vigilant lookouts to identify danger early enough to manoeuvre. This was established
   contemporary practice, not a belief unique to Titanic.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**  
   Pilot decision point: the evening of 14 April 1912, after the bridge knew the ship had
   reached a region where ice had been reported and before the collision, when Captain Smith
   could maintain speed, slow materially or alter farther south.

3. **What was known at that moment**  
   Multiple reports placed ice near the route; the night was clear, moonless and unusually
   calm; lookouts had been warned; the ship was proceeding at about 22 knots; not every ice
   message had the same operational status or reached the bridge in the same form.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**  
   The precise location, density and visibility of ice ahead; whether calm water would make
   an iceberg harder to detect; the stopping and turning performance needed once an object
   became visible; which messages Captain Smith personally saw and how he interpreted them.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**  
   A. Maintain speed and strengthen the lookout, consistent with common clear-weather
   practice.  
   B. Reduce speed materially while crossing the reported ice region.  
   C. Alter farther south, accepting time and fuel cost without certainty that the new track
   is clear.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**  
   Lookouts and bridge officers required to detect and avoid the iceberg, then crew and
   passengers facing a lifeboat system with capacity and loading limitations.

7. **What they had to do**  
   The bridge attempted an avoidance manoeuvre with seconds available. After collision, crew
   had to assess unfamiliar damage, uncover and load boats, manage information and decide
   when to launch with uncertain remaining time.

8. **How the story was later remembered**  
   As hubris, “unsinkability,” missing binoculars and a race for a record. Several popular
   claims exceed what the inquiries establish. The stronger case concerns a normal practice
   whose safety depended on detection conditions that were poorly understood.

9. **The unique decision question**  
   **When a safe practice depends on detecting trouble in time, what should change at the
   edge of detection?**

10. **Primary sources available**  
    Complete British and US inquiry transcripts, reports and testimony, freely searchable
    and downloadable through the Titanic Inquiry Project:
    <https://www.titanicinquiry.org/downloads.php>

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Decision-forcing case with a second downstream choice during evacuation.
- **Role:** Captain Smith or officer responsible for the night navigation decision; the
  role card must not invent a private motive.
- **Objective:** Maintain a safe and timely Atlantic passage.
- **Decision point:** After entering the reported ice region, before 11:40 p.m.
- **Primary resource:** Passage time spent to gain detection and manoeuvre margin.
- **Counterfactual:** Reduce speed materially while preserving course, lookout and all later
  equipment. This changes available reaction distance but does not guarantee detection or
  prevent a collision; it tests whether speed is being treated as a magical single cause.
- **Competing diagnosis:** The navigation decision was reasonable under contemporary
  practice and the catastrophic loss arose mainly from hull damage, lifeboat regulation and
  evacuation execution.
- **Boundary:** Maintaining speed can be reasonable when detection performance is known,
  stopping or avoidance margin is demonstrated and uncertainty does not degrade the sensing
  method itself.
- **What transfers:** A control is not a safeguard if its response time exceeds the warning
  time available.
- **What does not transfer:** 1912 navigation custom, ship handling and wireless procedure.
- **Solo action:** Ask, “At the first reliable warning, how many seconds or cycles remain—and
  has that recovery actually been demonstrated?”
- **Exercise:** For three monitoring controls, record warning time, response time and the
  condition that degrades detection. Pass when all three have a positive demonstrated margin.
- **Nearest overlap:** Boeing 737 MAX. Titanic tests detection plus manoeuvre margin; Boeing
  tests diagnosis plus human recovery margin.
