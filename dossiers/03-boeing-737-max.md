# Pilot 03 — Boeing 737 MAX

Status: **PRELIMINARY — decision point must be narrowed from the certification record**

## Short case dossier

1. **The promise management believed**  
   A flight-control intervention could remain within an existing certification and training
   model because a properly trained crew would recognize and counter an erroneous activation
   quickly enough.

2. **The earliest reversible decision**  
   Candidate pilot point: the design and safety-assessment stage when MCAS authority and
   activation logic expanded while the analysis continued to rely on rapid pilot response.
   The exact meeting, document and accountable role must be fixed before prose.

3. **What was known at that moment**  
   MCAS could command stabilizer movement based on angle-of-attack input; simulator work had
   produced evidence that one test pilot took more than ten seconds to respond to an
   uncommanded activation and found the condition hazardous; training and documentation
   choices affected pilot awareness; certification depended partly on assumptions about
   crew recognition and response.

4. **What remained legitimately uncertain**  
   The frequency and combinations of sensor failure in service; how representative Boeing
   test pilots were of line crews; how pilots would interpret simultaneous alerts; what each
   organizational layer knew after design changes; the exact causal weight of training,
   design, certification and airline execution in each crash.

5. **Two plausible alternatives**  
   A. Preserve the architecture but require explicit pilot training and documentation.  
   B. Redesign activation to use additional validation, limit authority or fail safer before
   relying on pilot recovery.  
   C. Accept the existing assessment and training model because runaway-stabilizer response
   already existed in pilot procedures.

6. **Who inherited the consequences**  
   Flight crews and passengers of Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian Airlines 302, plus maintainers
   and airline operators diagnosing sensor and alert problems without a complete mental model
   of MCAS.

7. **What they had to do**  
   Crews had to identify an unexpected control problem amid other indications, counter trim,
   manage aircraft trajectory and apply the appropriate cutout procedure under severe time
   pressure.

8. **How the story was later remembered**  
   Often as either corporate greed or pilot failure. The chapter must keep design assumptions,
   certification delegation, training, maintenance and the differences between the two
   accident sequences visible at the same time.

9. **The unique decision question**  
   **How much operator recovery may a design assume when the operator has not been given the
   system model needed to diagnose the failure?**

10. **Primary sources available**  
    US House final report, FAA reading room, Indonesian KNKT and Ethiopian investigation
    reports, certification and hearing records. Anchor:
    <https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-Y4_T68_2-PURL-gpo144993/pdf/GOVPUB-Y4_T68_2-PURL-gpo144993.pdf>

## Chapter contract

- **Mode:** Diagnostic reconstruction, because the design decision is distributed and the
  reader otherwise diagnoses only the cockpit’s final minutes.
- **Role:** Safety-assessment or certification decision-maker; exact role pending source lock.
- **Objective:** Certify a derivative aircraft safely while limiting unnecessary differences
  in operation and training.
- **Decision point:** The first documented point at which expanded MCAS behavior and slow or
  difficult pilot response coexist in the record.
- **Primary resource:** Design complexity and training time spent to gain fleet commonality.
- **Counterfactual:** Give crews complete MCAS knowledge and simulator practice without
  changing hardware. If the later problem changes only in degree, design remains causal; if
  it changes in kind, the chapter must give training greater weight.
- **Competing diagnosis:** Existing runaway-stabilizer procedures were adequate and the
  decisive failures were maintenance and crew execution; another is that certification
  governance, not the specific design trade, was the earliest consequential failure.
- **Boundary:** Operators legitimately absorb residual risk when the failure is recognizable,
  time is sufficient, recovery is trained and the system fails within a familiar model.
- **What transfers:** Treat human recovery time as a safety component that requires evidence.
- **What does not transfer:** Aircraft certification standards and cockpit expertise.
- **Solo action:** For any plan that says “the user will notice and correct it,” write the
  cues, available seconds and required prior knowledge.
- **Exercise:** In the next three reviews, mark every human-recovery assumption. Pass when
  each has an observable cue, tested response window and named training source.
- **Nearest overlap:** Challenger. Boeing tests a human recovery assumption embedded in
  system design; Challenger tests proof at a go/no-go boundary.
